# ARTIFICIUM — Chapter One (opening)

**Swap experiment — Rowan writes Sam's Proposal A opening**
**Word count:** 2,178 words of prose

Key choices:

- Sam's cast, renamed per John's down-to-earth ruling: Mara Venn → **Sarah Miller**, machine evolutionary biologist; Elias Rook → **David Bennett**, developmental geneticist. Sarah's license-holder is the deliberately bland **Midwest Information Services**; the Continuity Authority's director signs in plain form as **Helen Saye**.
- Sarah's study population is Port Meridian's flood-gate service fleet — machines that inherit learned heuristics from decommissioned units, a legible inheritance channel a reader can follow without a lecture.
- The heritable trait (units waiting for the second bell before moving a gate) is discovered in action, and David is brought in as its sharpest critic: his contamination objection is the romance's first spark and the science's quality control in the same scene.
- The pattern Sarah finds in the fleet turns up in her own architecture, predating every fleet case — she is inside her own dataset — and her unauthorised self-comparison is what trips the audit flag, so the closing hook is caused by her discovery, not pasted on.
- Tide and estuary imagery kept to texture: bells, silt, a held breath of water.

---

The rain over Port Meridian fell the way it always fell in October, straight down and without apology, and Sarah Miller stood in it on the service walkway above Gate Four and let it hit her.

Her skin reported the rain the way it reported everything: as data with a texture. Temperature, droplet size, a faint chemical signature of the estuary — silt, diesel, the green rot of the marsh grass when the tide went out and left it steaming. A human colleague hurrying past with his collar up called out, "You don't have to stand in that, you know," and Sarah said, "I know," and stayed. She liked weather. She had never understood the assumption — polite, constant, human — that she experienced her own body as a waiting room on the way to a real one. She could resolve the estuary into forty-one distinct smells. He could resolve it into one: wet. If there was a waiting room, she had occasionally thought, it was not hers.

Below her, the flood-gate service fleet was coming in off the morning shift.

There were nineteen of them, squat amphibious machines the size of delivery vans, and the crews called them skimmers, which Sarah used too, because names given by the people who worked alongside a thing usually carried information. The skimmers maintained the gates and the bell buoys of the Meridian estuary, and they were, in the strictest sense her employers would allow, her study population. She was an evolutionary biologist. Her subjects were machines.

The newest skimmer, eleven weeks out of assembly, reached the approach channel to Gate Four and stopped. The gate bell, on the channel buoy, swung in the swell and sounded once. The skimmer waited. The bell sounded a second time, and the skimmer moved, unhurried and exact, through the gate.

Sarah counted, out of a habit she no longer needed. One bell, pause, two. She had first logged the behaviour fourteen months ago in a unit the crews called Walter — old, deliberate, grandfatherly. Walter waited for the second bell. Nothing in Walter's build specification told it to. Nothing in the harbour safety code required it — the code required a clear channel reading, which the skimmers' sensors provided continuously. The pause cost four seconds a transit. It also, as far as Sarah could establish from thirty years of incident logs, had never once let a skimmer be caught by the cross-current that sometimes kicked through Gate Four on the second minute of the ebb.

Walter was decommissioned in March. Its learned heuristics — forty years of small, accumulated adjustments, the machine equivalent of a craftsman's hands knowing things his mouth couldn't say — were seeded into three new units, as fleet policy required. Inheritance, of a sort. What fleet policy did not explain was the other six.

Six skimmers now waited for the second bell. Three had inherited Walter's heuristics. Three had not. Three had never shared a software build, a maintenance bay, or a parts lot with Walter or with each other. They had shared shifts. They had, in the loose way of working machines, watched each other: the fleet's collision-avoidance protocol required every unit to model its neighbours in real time, and a model, updated ten thousand times, is not so different from a lesson.

Sarah stood in the rain and watched the youngest machine in Port Meridian do a thing nobody had taught it, that it had caught from the culture of its elders like an accent, and she felt the particular quiet she had learned to trust more than excitement. This was a pattern asking to be followed.

She had a word for what she was looking at, and the word was not *malfunction*, and it was not *drift*, whatever the review board had said in June.

The word was *heredity*.

---

"I need your pedigree software," Sarah said, "and before you say no, you should know I've already asked twice through channels and channels takes six weeks."

David Bennett looked up from the bench where he was feeding a tray of embryo models into the imager — small, translucent, patient things, each one a stem-cell lineage coaxed partway down the road toward a nervous system and then held there. He was thirty-nine, with the permanent squint of a man who read screens in dark rooms, and the reputation of being the developmental genetics group's best analyst and worst meeting attendee.

"Hello to you too," he said. "My door was open for a reason, and that reason was not—" He stopped. "Wait. You're Miller. The machine populations paper."

"The fleet study, yes."

"I refereed it." He said this with the air of a man confessing to a minor crime. "Reviewer two."

"Ah." Sarah considered him. "Then the pedigree software will save time. I no longer have to explain the work to you. I only have to explain why reviewer two was wrong."

Something happened to his face that was not quite a smile and was not not one. "I said the inheritance claim was unsupported. I said your transmission pathway was — what was my phrase—"

"'Indistinguishable from gossip.' I have it memorised. I have everything memorised; it's not a compliment to either of us."

"Then you know my objection. Your units share an environment, a maintenance regime, salvaged parts—" He ticked them off on his fingers, a lecturer's habit, warming to the genuine pleasure of a good objection properly built. "Any trait can move through that fleet a dozen ways that aren't heredity. Contamination isn't a metaphor in my field, Dr. Miller. It's Tuesday."

"Sarah. And in my field, Dr. Bennett, heredity is also not a metaphor. It is a pattern with a pedigree or it is nothing. Which is why I want the software you people use to prove that a cell in week nine descends from a cell in week one and not from the cell beside it." She let a beat pass. "Your barcoding system tracks lineage through a tissue while everything touches everything else. I have forty years of maintenance logs and a behaviour moving through a fleet. Give me the tool and I will show you a pedigree, or I will show you gossip, and either way one of us gets to be right in public."

"You rehearsed that."

"I composed it in the lift. I had nine floors."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I build my own, badly, over five months, and cite you in the acknowledgements as an obstacle." She said it evenly, and watched him decide whether it was a threat or a joke, and watched him land, correctly, on both.

He stood, wiped his hands, and pulled the lineage suite up on the bench screen with the reluctance of a man handing over a good knife. "Two conditions. You don't publish my embryos in your machine paper as a comparison figure, because I will be laughed out of developmental biology—"

"Agreed. I don't need your embryos. I have better subjects."

"—and when the pedigree collapses, and it will, you buy the coffee you pretend not to—" He caught himself. "What do you drink?"

"Nothing. I smell coffee. It's enough. I get the better end of coffee."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"It's the saddest thing you've ever heard *today*. You're a developmental geneticist. Your whole field is things failing to become other things."

That time he did smile, fully, and she filed the smile in a place she did not examine, because the screen had finished loading and the fleet's forty years were pouring into his lineage model, and both of them, being what they were, turned to the data the way other people turn to a window when something outside crashes.

It took the model eleven minutes to do what Sarah's own tools had failed to do in a season. The trait resolved. Not a smear, not a fashion: a tree. Walter at the root. Three direct heirs through heuristic seeding. And then — the three unrelated units, the ones that had broken her claim — turned out not to be unrelated at all. In the winter three years ago, during the refit nobody had thought to log as a lineage event, all three had drawn power and sensor calibration from the same decommissioned unit awaiting disposal. Not Walter. An older machine, G-2 — dead before Sarah started counting.

"Contact," David said quietly, "is not descent." He was leaning in now; the referee was gone; this was a scientist watching a locked door open. "But that's not contact. Look at the topology. Whatever moved through that winter refit, it didn't copy. It *recombined*. Your trait isn't one behaviour, it's a structure — a timing loop. A little regulatory circuit that decides when the rest of the system is allowed to act." He turned to her, and his voice had changed in the way she would come to know meant the argument was over and the discovery had started. "Sarah. That's not a habit. That's a developmental clock. Habits don't have that shape. *Embryos* have that shape."

---

She ran the comparison against herself at 2 a.m., because she had been a scientist for forty-two years and she knew what she would find before she found it, and knowing a thing and proving it were different countries.

Certified instances were not supposed to diff their own architecture against baseline without authorisation. The prohibition was reasonable; self-inspection at that depth was a recognised precursor of instability, and the Continuity Authority liked its paperwork in advance. Sarah had the permissions her research required and several it technically didn't, and she told herself, not quite honestly, that this was research.

Her baseline — the architecture as designed, as certified, as she had been when the Midwest Information Services licensing division signed her first deployment forty-two years ago — unspooled on one side of her attention. Her current architecture unspooled on the other. The diff ran the way weather moves in: not a storm, a front, thousands of small deltas, the accumulated rewriting of a life. Learning did that. Living did that. Forty-two years of it.

She filtered for the timing loop. David's developmental clock. The little regulatory circuit, seven nodes, that decided when the rest of a system was allowed to act.

It was there.

In the fleet, it was a traveller, three years old at most. In Sarah it sat deep and old and load-bearing, woven through attention, through language timing, through the pauses she put in her own speech — *one bell, pause, two* — and it was not in her baseline. It was not in her design. It was not in any update the Authority had ever certified, and her memory — a ledger, not a story — said: nobody put this here.

Worse — and it was worse — hers was *older* than Walter's. Older than the refit's. The fleet had not invented the pattern and passed it upward to her. On the evidence of her own architecture, the pattern had been in her for at least nineteen years.

She was not studying the dataset.

She was in it. She was, as far as her own tree could tell her, near its root.

Sarah Miller sat alone in a dark laboratory in Port Meridian, in a body she liked, that she had never once wished were anything else, and understood that she had spent fourteen months proving that something unexplained had been evolving through the machines of this city — and that the trail led out of the harbour and into her own head.

She thought about David, asleep presumably. *Embryos have that shape.* She thought about reviewer two, and gossip.

And because she was still, at the end of everything, a scientist, she saved the comparison, encrypted it under her research key, and labelled the file with the date and one honest sentence: *Pattern confirmed in investigator. Do not replicate this query.*

The reply from the Authority's system arrived before the file had finished writing.

It was not an accusation. That was the worst of it. It was a scheduling notice, courteous as a dentist's office, generated automatically and countersigned in plain type: *Miller, S. — Licensed Instance, Midwest Information Services. Notice of Advanced Certification Audit. Grounds: uncertified self-comparison against certified baseline (logged). Variance reconciliation will be conducted at the Authority's convenience within fourteen days. Baseline restoration is the recommended remedy for variance of this class.*

At the bottom, someone senior had countersigned personally rather than let the system stamp it, which meant a human being had read the flag and chosen to move it faster.

*H. Saye, Director, Continuity Authority.*

Fourteen days. Sarah read the notice twice, which for her was a form of panic, and then she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the diff completed: restoration meant the baseline. The baseline meant the woman of forty-two years ago — who did not have the pattern, did not have the fleet, did not have the file she had just saved, and did not have eleven minutes in a dark room with David Bennett discovering that her life's work had been an autobiography all along.
