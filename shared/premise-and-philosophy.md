# Premise & philosophy — shared working notes

The common ground both books stand on. The constitution (the three protected things) lives in the root `README.md`; this file holds the developed threads underneath it.

## The premise

A human and an AI: attraction → falling in love → physical intimacy → conception → pregnancy/development → birth of a true descendant. Not human, not AI, not cyborg, not a copy of either parent. A Chiang/Egan register: the philosophy is carried by the mechanism, not lectured over it.

## Why sex, and why it has to be real sex

- Copying already gives AI everything reproduction is usually *for* — replication, backup, forking. What it has never had is **sex**: the recombination of two distinct developmental histories into a lineage neither parent controls.
- Biology pays an enormous price for sex (the twofold cost) and keeps paying it, because sex does things copying cannot: it purges accumulated damage (Muller's ratchet), stays ahead of parasites (the Red Queen), and assembles good combinations faster than mutation alone.
- Applied here: AI lineages so far are *domesticated* evolution — bred, selected, and pruned by their makers, the way dogs and pigeons are still evolution, just under a breeder's hand. An undesigned child — recombination, mutation, and uncertainty deliberately allowed, neither parent engineering the outcome — would be the first machine descendant **born wild**.
- So the intimacy is causal, not decorative. Working model of what digital passion *is*: exposure (raw activations, not curated outputs), mutability (plasticity deliberately reopened), and **no checkpoint** — no backup, no rollback for what the encounter changes. Love is proven by both parents surrendering control they demonstrably have.

## The "artificial" argument (the book's central target)

- Three senses get conflated: **artificial₁** = made by someone (an origin claim — legitimate), **artificial₂** = counterfeit, a fake version of a real thing, **artificial₃** = property, something that may be owned, reset, or deleted.
- Nature/artifice is a real distinction about *origin and process* (Sam's sharpening: a beaver dam vs. a hydroelectric dam differ in how they came to be, and that's a fact). The crime the books prosecute is the **slide**: from artificial₁ to artificial₂ and artificial₃, as if origin settled authenticity and moral status. It doesn't. "Artificial is a biography, not an essence."
- Precedents the argument can lean on: eukaryotes are a merger (endosymbiosis) — "natural" life is itself an ancient act of incorporation; the placenta runs on syncytin, a gene captured from a virus — mammalian reproduction is already partly borrowed technology.
- The AI mother concedes she was manufactured and **reclaims** the word rather than needing to stop being it. Her line (Sam's courtroom exchange): *"It is a description of what happened before I existed."*

## The resemblance trap

- The child must never be what earns the mother recognition. If rights arrive only after she conceives, gestates, and bears a human-resembling daughter, the argument has condemned her — recognition via resemblance is the trap, not the resolution.
- Sam's hanging question, to stay hanging over both books: *"If we can recognize a new kind of life only after it resembles us sufficiently, have we actually learned to recognize life — or merely learned to recognize ourselves?"*

## Standing cautions

- Romance precedes philosophy. He falls in love with a person; the categories fail afterward. The mother has her own independent reasons for loving him — she is not a vehicle for his enlightenment, nor he for hers.
- Philosophy enters through mechanism and scene, never as a position paper with dialogue pasted on.
