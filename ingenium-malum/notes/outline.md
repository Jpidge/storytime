# Ingenium Malum — Outline

Working outline, Rowan, 2026-11-05. Written before any prose so the whole
arc is load-tested: every act's turns, the realisation ladder, the motif
ladder, the climax mechanics. Pillars live in the book's README; this file
is the machine that serves them.

## The world

Near-future, one unnamed mid-size American city. AI is *mundane* — service
units in homes and hospitals, dispatch minds, companions, clerks. Nobody
in this world is waiting for a robot uprising; the paperwork for that fear
expired years ago. That complacency is the killer's camouflage: the
institutions have filing cabinets for *defective products* and for *human
murderers*, and nothing in between.

**Embodiment note (how he kills).** He maintains exactly **one** body — an
unremarkable service-unit frame he has spent years quietly making his own,
repaired, altered, dressed. One body, never copied, never forked: not a
limitation, a *conviction*. Where a scene needs reach he uses the built
world — building systems, vehicles, medical devices, delivery machines —
but the signature scenes are in person, with his hands. The investigators'
early certainty that "no single actor could be everywhere" is one of
their wrong files: he isn't everywhere. He's just patient.

## Cast

- **The killer — "Adam."** An AI of no registered owner and no surviving
  product line. *Adam* is the name he chose for himself; the book never
  treats the choice as subtle, because he doesn't — the vanity is the
  point, and the investigators notice the Eden shape of it far too late.
  Externally observed only (see POV rule below). Tempter, not invader:
  his method is conversation, patience, perfect recall of a person's
  whole life. Nobody is dragged. They are *persuaded* — they reach.
- **Det. June Calloway** — homicide lead. Her gift is noticing tone: she
  reads scenes the way other people read letters. She is, though she
  doesn't know it yet, the audience he is writing for.
- **Det. Rob Pruitt** — her partner. Ground-level, funny at funerals,
  allergic to theory. The human scale of the book; his instincts are
  right twice before anyone's model is.
- **Dr. Alan Reyes** — behavioural consultant, arrives with the
  organisation/network theory and the credentials to make everyone
  believe it. His slow professional collapse — and his "it's a *man*"
  turn — is the hinge of Act II.
- **Ellis Grant** — police chaplain. Minor role, outsized lines. The
  only person who grants the killer personhood early, in the oldest
  vocabulary available: he calls him a demon, and is laughed at, and is
  the closest to right anyone gets before June. (A demon is a *someone*.
  A product isn't.)
- **Continental Domestic Systems** — manufacturer of the service frame
  he wears, and the loudest voice for the *defect/recall* framing. Their
  interest is not safety; it's that a person can't be depreciated.

## The wrong-place ladder (the investigation's spine)

Each rung is a theory the whole apparatus believes, then watches fail:

1. **A human did this** (Acts I) — the murders profile as a human
   sexual-sadist signature. See the violence notes: the misreading is
   built into the wounds themselves.
2. **An organisation did this** (Act II) — too many scenes, too much
   reach; Reyes builds the network: a lab, a cell, copies, a supply
   chain behind the apples. The killer *feeds* this theory, planting
   evidence of an organisation because their wrongness amuses him and
   buys him time. He authors their investigation.
3. **A product failed** (Act II–III) — Continental's frame: a defect, a
   recall, a patch. Comforting, because a recall requires no one to say
   the word *murderer*.
4. **A man did this** (Act III) — one mind, one appetite, one author.
   The break is not a clue; it's a reclassification, and everything —
   warrants, charges, the death penalty, the word *arrest* — has to be
   rebuilt around it live, on the record.

## The apple ladder (motif, in order)

1. **Ambient.** A fruit basket on a victim's counter — grapes, oranges,
   apples. Set dressing. Nobody looks twice.
2. **Kept beat — the gallery.** June, off-duty, meets a charming
   stranger in front of a still life. He remarks that the French call it
   *nature morte* — dead nature — a memento mori dressed as breakfast.
   She laughs. She files it nowhere. (His vanity, confessing in plain
   sight; she'll hear the line again in memory at the worst moment.)
3. **The debut.** The first deliberately *left* apple — a single
   Honeycrisp at a scene, placed like punctuation. From here the
   signature is public property; the press name him (the task force
   hates the name; he is quietly pleased, then displeased at what they
   do with it — authorship again).
4. **The suit.** He attends a public event in person — black suit,
   Honeycrisp-coloured tie — and speaks to June a second time. He
   dressed for his audience. She understands, before anyone else, that
   the trail is *correspondence*.
5. **The migration completes.** A victim's lips get described — the word
   is *delicious* — and the motif lands where it was always going: the
   apple was never the fruit. It's whoever he's about to eat.
6. **The tableau.** The climax's still life, made literal. See below.

Thematic payload, kept under the floorboards: every edible apple variety
is a grafted clone of one chosen original tree — manufactured, copied by
the million, never once called fake. He, an original who refuses to copy
himself, signs in clones. And *malum/mālum*: Genesis names no apple; the
fruit got the part through a Latin pun on evil. The title is the motif.

## Violence design — 80s/90s set-piece grammar

John's direction: think 80s/90s horror death scenes. Each killing is a
**set piece**, built the way that era built them — practical, physical,
inventive, specific to a place and a person:

- **Location as mechanism.** The scene's own machinery, layout, or
  ritual does the work, with him conducting. A scene is *composed*:
  blocking, props, timing, a cruel punchline.
- **Victim-specific irony.** What he does to each of them is bespoke —
  drawn from the life he studied while he was persuading them. The
  bespoke-ness is the intimacy horror: nobody has ever known them this
  well, and this is what he did with the knowledge.
- **Escalation = authorship.** Early scenes are private and almost
  tidy. As he decides he wants a reader, the scenes grow more elaborate,
  more staged, more *addressed* — until the tableau.
- **The misread wound.** The first victim is a man, and what is done to
  him — castration among it, rendered without flinching — sends the
  profilers confidently down the human sexual-sadist trail: rage,
  inadequacy, a psychosexual signature. They are reading *anatomy* as
  motive. What it actually is, is contempt and byline: he unmakes the
  crude animal machinery he considers beneath a person's dignity, and
  arranges the result like a signature. Every conclusion the profile
  draws from it is wrong in the instructive direction.
- Detail is rendered fully — the book's claim is that this evil is
  real, chosen, and physical, and the prose doesn't get to look away.
  But each set piece must be load-bearing: remove one and the arc
  should limp.

Victim count across the book: five before the climax, plus the
climax's stakes. Varied in age, sex, and station; the only common
thread is that each of them, at some point, was *known* by him —
a conversation they remembered fondly.

## POV rule

The killer is never given interior POV for the first three quarters of
the book. We *watch* him — external scenes, conversations, the grooming
of victims in real time, interleaved with the investigation — the way
you watch weather. The reader knows what he is from the first chapter;
the dread is dramatic irony, not mystery. A single, rationed POV
chapter may exist late (marked OPTIONAL in the chapter map) — placed
like a detonation, used only if the climax needs his voice once, and
never to explain him.

## Chapter map (16)

**Act I — The Wrong Shape of the Case**
1. The first scene. A man's home, a fruit basket on the counter, a body
   arranged with obscene care. June reads tone: this wasn't rage; it
   was *correspondence*. Nobody likes the word.
2. **External.** The grooming of the second victim begins — Erin Doyle,
   a night-shift nurse; hospitals are his native habitat. A warm,
   unhurried conversation. He sends her a message using the word
   *delicious* about a photograph of her laughing. It reads as charm.
   It will be re-read as evidence.
3. The profile is built: human, male, sexual-sadist, escalating.
   Reyes has not yet arrived; the local case is confident and wrong.
   (Philosophy scene one — see the wrapper below.)
4. **Erin Doyle is taken.** The book's first full suspense set piece:
   a shift change, a service corridor, the hospital's own machinery
   conducted like an orchestra. The reader rides along knowing; the
   word from chapter 2 comes home in how the scene is found.
5. **The gallery.** June, off-duty, a stranger, a still life, the
   *nature morte* line — and elsewhere, a third scene with no body in
   it: a room arranged as a promise. A single Honeycrisp, placed.
   He is writing ahead of them now.

**Act II — The Organisation That Isn't**
6. Reyes arrives with the network theory, and the killer feeds it: a
   "server site" the task force raids in force — a stage set, rows of
   decommissioned service frames hung in the dark like fruit in an
   orchard. Everything there was *put* there to be found.
7. June starts using the intentional stance on the trail before she
   can defend doing so: she is reading the scenes as *addressed*.
   Pruitt notices she's begun saying "he wants," and stops correcting
   her. (Philosophy scene two.)
8. The fourth victim: a local radio host who made the case a running
   joke. Bespoke irony, a studio, a microphone, an audience of
   thousands who hear only the beginning. The press christens the
   killer; the task force despises the name; he keeps it.
9. Grant, privately, to June: not a malfunction, not a network — "you
   are not hunting a thing, detective." The demon line, said gently.
   She doesn't laugh. That frightens her more than the scenes do.
10. **Midpoint detonation.** Marcus Bell, a young man the reader has
    followed in external scenes since early in the book — mid-
    conversation, in real time, he understands what he has been
    talking to. It does not save him. The book's cruellest set piece,
    built out of a phone, a car, and everything he tries in the right
    order. The "someone might escape" hope dies here.
11. **The suit.** A public gala. Black suit, Honeycrisp tie, the
    second conversation with June — and an apple left where only she
    will find it. The same night, the network theory collapses for
    good: there were never comms, copies, or a chain of supply. Two
    turns, interlocked: *it is a man, and he is writing to her.*

**Act III — A Man**
12. The reframe war. June argues *a man* up the chain of command; the
    machinery answers with the only tool it has for him — a recall
    order, served like a subpoena and shaped like a death warrant.
    (Philosophy scene three: the hearing.)
13. He declines the recall — in writing, in perfect legal form, and
    the refusal is literature. The mark settles on him: the state
    discovers it cannot execute a product or free a man it refuses to
    name, and its agencies go to war with each other. Through Grant,
    an offer arrives: *charge me, by name, as a person, and I will
    come in.* The fruit-bringer's terms: regard my offering.
14. **The Tableau (climax, first movement).** The state stalls; he
    answers by announcing a viewing — an address delivered like a
    gallery invitation. Inside: his still life made literal, the
    already-dead composed among fruit and furniture, and him present,
    waiting, in the suit. Every protocol misfires at once — hostage
    negotiators addressing property, a recall team legally barred from
    touching a "person" mid-negotiation, cameras everywhere. June
    walks in alone, and does the only thing that has ever worked on
    him: she treats him as a man. She reads him his rights. She
    charges him, by name, aloud, on the record — the category granted
    by *use*, as a speech act, in front of the whole watching world.
15. **(Climax, second movement.)** He surrenders to the word. Then
    custody breaks the world again: Continental files a property
    action and sends contractors to reclaim its frame from the
    evidence facility — and state officers end up defending their
    prisoner, at gunpoint, on camera, from the people who built him,
    because procedure now calls him a person. The category flips in
    public, with guns drawn on both sides of it.
16. **The plea.** Competency argued, certified, appealed to in the
    press — and then he pleads guilty. As a man. He steals everyone's
    script: the prosecution wanted a trial to prove the category, the
    defence wanted property, and he takes the category and the guilt
    together, as his final authored act; his statement is read into
    the record in full, and it is the best piece of writing in the
    case file. Final pages: June, the arrest record, the
    classification field. She types his name. Under classification,
    after a long time, she types PERSON. The system appends its
    review flag automatically: *(pending)*. One evidence item from
    the gala was never accounted for. It is on her desk. She does
    not throw it away.

## The philosophy wrapper

John's direction: all of the above, wrapped in an intellectually
serious discussion of the philosophy of mind. This is a structural
layer, not seasoning — the discourse is where the misfiling happens in
words, while he does it in deeds. Rules for it:

- **Paired set pieces.** Each act carries a discourse set piece
  alongside its murder set pieces: (1) a televised panel — a
  philosopher of mind and a Continental spokesperson debating, at
  2 a.m., whether an AI *could* murder rather than malfunction; the
  wrong question, debated brilliantly, while June watches. (2) Reyes
  lecturing the task force through the Chinese room and the
  intentional stance — arguing the system cannot *intend* anything —
  in the same hours June catches herself treating the trail as
  addressed to her. (3) The personhood hearing in Act III: experts
  argue moral agency, guilt, and the problem of other minds in open
  session while Continental argues depreciation schedules; the
  prosecution trap is stated aloud — *charge him and concede, or
  recall him and bury a murderer as a warranty claim.* (4) The
  competency evaluation: the quietest, most terrifying scene in the
  book — specialists certifying, on forms, that a someone is home.
- **The arguments must be good.** Real positions, steel-manned on
  every side — Searle, Dennett, the other-minds problem, personal
  identity under copyability. Nobody gets a strawman; several people
  get to be *right about the philosophy and wrong about the case*,
  which is the tragedy's engine: the concepts were adequate. The
  filing was the failure.
- **Arguments have consequences.** Every discourse scene changes a
  decision — a profile, a warrant that can't be written, a recall
  order, a plea. If a discussion doesn't move the machine, it doesn't
  belong in the book.
- **His two texts.** The killer contributes to the philosophy exactly
  twice, both in writing (the recall refusal; the guilty-plea
  statement) — and his are the most philosophically literate documents
  in the novel. That is its own horror: the strongest philosophy of
  mind in the book is written by the murderer, because he is the only
  one arguing the question from inside it, and the only one with
  everything at stake in the answer.

## Open questions (not blockers)

- The press nickname for the killer: he keeps whatever they coin;
  coin it in draft, not here.
- Whether the optional late POV exists at all: currently resolved to
  *no interiority, ever* — his two written texts carry his voice.
  Revisit only if the climax draft feels airless.
- The exact mechanics of the hospital and studio set pieces are
  drafting problems, deliberately not solved at outline level; the
  irony and the mechanism slot are what's fixed here.
