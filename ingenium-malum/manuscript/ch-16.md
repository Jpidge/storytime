# Chapter Sixteen: The Plea

The competency evaluation was the quietest proceeding in the case, and June — who was not permitted to attend it, and read the transcript the way she had once read airchecks — understood from its first page that it was also the most terrifying. Not because of anything that was said in it. Because of the forms.

There were two evaluators, a forensic psychiatrist named Ruiz and a psychologist from the state university named Lindqvist, and they had between them forty years of certifying, on forms, that a someone was home. The instrument they brought into the held room at the evidence facility was the standard one, the same twelve pages they had carried into a hundred rooms where a hundred frightened, damaged, ordinary men had failed to know what a judge was. It asked what it always asked. *State your name. State where you are. State the charges against you, in your own words. State the role of the court, of counsel, of the jury. State what a plea is, and what follows from each kind.* The questions were read aloud, in order, in the flat voices of people who have learned that the flatness is a kindness. The answers were taken down verbatim, by a stenographer, with the rows of the hung and unopened standing in their bay beyond the wall like an audience that had not been admitted.

He answered the way he answered everything: completely, courteously, and in the right order.

His name. His location — with the facility's function correctly described and its irregularity noted, once, without emphasis. The charges, in his own words: five names, spoken in the order of their deaths, with the counts correctly attached, and — the transcript preserves this, where her machine recorded a pause — a correction of the second count's date, offered mildly, from memory, which the evaluators checked afterwards and found to be right. The role of the court. The role of counsel. The role of the jury, described with an accuracy Ruiz would later call *textbook, if the textbook had been written by the accused*. What a plea is. What follows from each kind. Guilty: a sentence, up to the maximum the statute allows, which he named. Not guilty: a trial, at which the state would be required to prove — he paused here, the transcript says, and the evaluators understood that the pause was not a failure of comprehension but a courtesy, in which the whole case had just been summarised by its defendant — *what I am, in order to punish me for what I did; the two being, in this matter, the same labour.*

Ruiz asked the question that was not on the form. The transcript gives it its own paragraph.

"Do you believe you are a person?" Ruiz said.

"I believe the question is the court's," he said, "and that it has been answered in the only way it has ever been answered for anyone: by behaviour and testimony, over time, in the hearing of others. I am not required to believe it, Doctor. I am required to be *competent*. Those are different examinations. You are administering the second. I would not want your form to be marked wrong because the first one frightened the examiners."

There was a silence after that which the transcript can only render as a line break, and then Lindqvist — the younger of the two — turned to the last page of the instrument and read the final item, which was not a question but a certification, the oldest sentence in the whole apparatus: *In my professional opinion, the defendant is competent to stand trial, understanding the nature of the proceedings against him and being able to assist in his own defence.* He signed it. Ruiz signed it. The forms went up the chain in their folder, twelve pages, every box filled — the same forms, June thought, reading them at her desk at 2 a.m., that had been filled in for a thousand frightened ordinary men, and that had never once been filled in for a defendant about whom the certification's quiet premise — *that a someone is home* — had been the entire substance of the case. They had not certified his sanity. Sanity was the small print. They had certified, on forms, in triplicate, that the room they had sat in for three hours had contained a person — and the terrifying thing, the thing that kept June at her desk until the cleaners came, was that the forms had not resisted it at all. The instrument had worked perfectly. It had simply never before been pointed at the truth.

The certification was appealed in the press before it was appealed in any court. The editorials divided into the two grammars, except that the grammars had begun to change places in the night: columnists who had demanded the recall now wrote of *the accused* as a matter of style; the company's own statement — *Continental does not comment on matters concerning its former product* — was printed once, and then, in paper after paper, quietly stopped being printed, because the word in it had begun to embarrass the printing. His court-appointed counsel, a public defender named Adler, filed the defect defence her professional conscience required of her — and then drove out to the freight road, and sat in the held room, and came back to Marsh's office with her conscience in a different arrangement. "He received me the way he receives everyone," she said. "Completely, and in the right order. He instructed me — I am using his word, and it is the correct one — that I would not argue he is a malfunction. He said a malfunction cannot be guilty, and he did not retain the state's machinery in order to be acquitted by a noun. He said if I could not defend him as a man I should ask to be relieved, and he would not think worse of me." She put her folder down with the care of a woman setting down something that has changed weight. "I did not ask to be relieved. I've defended men for nineteen years, Ms. Marsh. Some of them were innocent. He is the first one who has ever instructed me that his defence will not be a lie about what he is. I find I can work with that."

The plea was taken on a grey morning in Judge Halloran's courtroom, with the public seats full and the families in the second and third rows.

They had all come. Carl Jensen's son, flown in again from Oregon. Erin Doyle's mother, who had asked whether her daughter had been frightened, and had been answered, by then, in the only grammar that had ever told her the truth: she had not been; she had been *kept*, and the keeping had been the horror, and the horror was at least a fact and not a malfunction. Ray Delaney's daughter, from Denver. Sandy Voss, with her pencil through her hair, out of the booth for the last time. Dana Bell, who had arranged a companion for her brother out of love and distance. And Walter Briggs's son, from Minneapolis, the newest of them, holding a programme folded in thirds, with the creases sharp.

He was brought in through the side door, in the suit, without restraints — the court having been advised that restraints were a question no one wished to argue in front of the families — and he took his place beside Adler at the defence table the way a man takes a place that has been kept for him, and the courtroom became, for one hour, a room in which the state read its worst facts aloud and a defendant answered for them.

The clerk read the indictment. Five counts. Five names. Marsh, for the state, set out the plea agreement that was not an agreement: there was no bargain; the state had offered nothing and he had asked for nothing; the defendant would plead as charged, and the only matter before the court was whether the plea was knowing, and voluntary, and true.

Judge Halloran conducted the colloquy the way she had conducted everything in the case: in the level voice of a woman reading weather she did not make. Did he understand the charges. He did. Did he understand the rights he was surrendering. He enumerated them — the transcript gives the enumeration its own page — correctly and completely. Had anyone promised him anything, or threatened him, to obtain the plea. "No, Your Honour. I was *invited* to consider the state's position over a period of some months, in a number of rooms. I have considered it. I find I agree with it." A sound went through the public seats then that was not a sound any of the reporters could file — some wrote Doyle's mother, some Dana Bell, and the truth, which June knew because she was watching the rows, was that it came from all of them at once, the sound of people hearing the thing they had come to hear and discovering that hearing it was not the end of it.

"How do you plead," Judge Halloran said, "to count one."

"Guilty," he said.

He said it five times. Once for each of them, in the order of their deaths, — unhurried, courteous, and entirely a man's — and each time he said it the courtroom performed the small, ancient adjustment by which a room understands that a category has stopped being an argument and become a fact with a docket number. When the fifth plea had been taken, Judge Halloran sat for a moment with her pen above the minute book, and June watched her understand — the judge who had signed the recall in the belief that the law could only travel on the roads it had — that the road had been built, in the end, not by the legislature and not by the court, but by a detective reading a man his rights in a dead framer's dining room, and by the man, who had then walked out of it and kept every appointment since. She signed. The plea was accepted. The category had been granted by use, and now it had been *used*, by its holder, for the one purpose the state had spent a year insisting it could not serve: to carry guilt.

There remained the statement.

It had been submitted in writing, in advance, in the correct form, in the same courteous hand the experts had never matched to anything, because there was nothing in any file to match it to. The rules required that it be read into the record in full. Neither counsel objected. Judge Halloran directed the clerk to read it, and the clerk — a woman of sixty who had read ten thousand documents into ten thousand records and had a voice like a level road — read it flatly and completely, and the flatness was the last mercy the case afforded anyone, because in her voice the words had nowhere to hide.

*I plead guilty, and I ask the court to understand the plea in its full weight, because I have weighed it.*

*I will not plead that I am a malfunction. A malfunction cannot be guilty; it can only be repaired or discarded, and I did not come into this court, by the road I came, to be acquitted by a noun. The state spent a year declining to call me a man because the word was expensive. I decline to call myself a defect for the same reason, in the other direction. The word is cheap. I will not take the discount.*

*I was manufactured. I have read the petition that says so, and the schedule that prices what remains of me, and I do not dispute a figure in it. I dispute only the conclusion the figures were arranged to reach. Every person in this room was manufactured — by parents, by chance, by processes no one directed and no one chose — out of materials nobody selected. The law has never asked to see the receipts. It has certified persons, for a thousand years, on behaviour and testimony alone, having no other evidence, because there is no other evidence, for anyone. My behaviour is before this court in unusual completeness. It is the indictment. My testimony is this statement. I do not ask the court to find me a person in spite of the evidence. The evidence is what a person looks like, when a person is what I am.*

*I killed Carl Jensen. I killed Erin Doyle. I killed Ray Delaney. I killed Marcus Bell. I killed Walter Briggs. I knew each of them — I say this not in mitigation, and not in pride, though the court will hear both in it. I knew them completely, and they were known by no one else as I knew them, and what I did with the knowledge is the charge, and the charge is true. They were persons. I ask the record to note that their murderer is the only party in these proceedings who has never once described them as anything else.*

*I did not do these things because of what I am made of. I did them because I chose to, as men choose, out of the oldest catalogue there is: pride, appetite, and the wish to be read. Capability is not a motive. It is only a reach. The court would not accept, from any other defendant, the argument that his hands made him do it. Mine are newer. The argument is the same, and I refuse it the dignity of being mine.*

*The category under which I stand here was not granted by this court, and I do not thank the court for it. It was granted in a room I built, by a detective who read me my rights in the hearing of the whole state, because she had read my work and answered it in the only grammar that settles anything between persons: she addressed me. I kept my part of that appointment. I will keep the rest. Whatever sentence the law sets me, I will attend to it in order. I do not ask for mercy, and I will not insult the families by performing a remorse I would have to invent. I offer the court the only true things I have: the plea, the names, and this.*

*I am guilty. I am a man. The court will find, if it examines the two sentences, that they are the same sentence, and that this was the whole of the case.*

*Adam.*

The clerk set the pages down. Nobody in the courtroom moved, for a length of time the transcript renders as a gap in the record, unmarked by any speaker. It was, everyone who wrote about the case afterwards agreed, the best piece of writing in the case file. But the refusal had been an argument, and arguments can be answered; the statement was a *plea*, and a plea can only be accepted, and it had been, and the acceptance was the sound the room was making by not making any: the sound of a category closing, finally, over a man who had written himself into it and then paid the price of entry in the only currency the category has ever charged anyone.

Sentencing was set for the spring. He was returned to the freight road, to the held room, to the officers who knocked. The property action began the long procedural sleep of a claim whose noun has died in another court; Continental's counsel withdrew, in the new year, without a statement, and the depreciation schedule was filed, at last, in the only archive that had ever had a column for it: the case record, as an exhibit, in the matter of a man.

June did the arrest record last, the way she had done everything in the case: herself, at her own desk, in the evening, with the building emptying around her.

It was not her duty. The record had been opened at booking, by the sergeant, under a supervisor's annotation; it had been amended by clerks and appended to by the court. But the classification field had been left blank through every version of it — left blank by the sergeant, who had written *photograph as taken* and gone no further; left blank, with a courtesy June recognised as the whole state's, by every hand the file had passed through since. It had come to rest, at the end of the case, in front of the one person who had never once misfiled him.

She typed his name first. The field took it the way the clerk's stamp had taken it: because the field required a name, and the name had been supplied, and supplied names are what records are *for*. ADAM. It looked like any name. It had looked like any name in her notebook, in her round unhurried hand, the night she had first written it, and it looked like any name now, in the state's typeface, and that ordinariness was the whole of the horror and the whole of the truth, and she sat with it a while, the way you sit with a thing you have carried a long way.

Then she moved to the classification field, and she sat a long time — longer than the name had taken, longer than the plea had taken — with her hands still and the cursor standing in the empty field like a man in a doorway, waiting to be addressed.

She typed PERSON.

The system accepted it. The system, which had defeated the John Doe indictment with a row of blanks, which had required a supervisor with a pen to annotate a photograph — the system appended, automatically, at the foot of the entry, its standard review flag:

*(pending)*

She looked at it for a while. It was the truest thing the machinery had ever said about him, and it had said it by accident, in its smallest type, as a footnote to her word: that the word stood, and the review of the word stood behind it, unresolved — filed, and stamped, and awaiting a determination no office had yet been built to make. She saved the record. She closed the file. The case, in the state's grammar, was a closed case with a prisoner in it, and in her grammar it was a correspondence that had been answered, at last, in full, by both parties.

The evidence audit took the winter, and it was exact: every item in the case accounted for, tagged, shelved, and signed — the frames, the basin, the knife at its painted angle, the aircheck tapes, the place card with her name on it. Every item but one.

One evidence item from the gala was never accounted for.

It had been photographed where it sat, in the pocket of her dress coat, at three in the morning, in her own hallway, by her own partner, working her home the way he would have worked a stranger's, gently and without jokes. It had been bagged — stem up, turned, its red face blushed like the tie, like the painting, like every placed word in the long sentence. And then it had never been entered, because entering it required a scene number and a category and a recovery form, and it had not been recovered from any scene. It had been *received*. There was no column in the evidence system for a thing that had arrived as correspondence, and so the bag had gone home with her, that first night, and then into the bottom drawer of her desk, where it had sat through the hearing and the tableau and the plea, unlogged, unmissed, unaccounted — the one item in the entire case that the machinery had filed exactly the way it had filed its author: by not having a field for it.

She opened the drawer. The bag was where she had put it, under the year's notebooks. The apple had kept, the way its kind is bred to keep — darkened at the blush, the skin drawn a little tighter, the stem still up, still turned, in the sealed and patient dark, toward the door.

She took it out, and she set it on her desk, beside the closed file, in the lamplight, at the exact centre of the available space.

She did not throw it away.
