# Chapter Fifteen: Custody

They went out together.

He had asked, and June had said yes. So the door of Walter Briggs's house opened at twenty past eight, and the author of eleven months of rooms came out of the last one the way he had come out of all of them: unhurried, in the black suit, with his clean hands empty and visible, at the shoulder of the detective he had been writing to all year. The floodlights took him. The lenses took him. Eleven cameras at a gala and three at a truck stop had failed to hold his face, and the street's forty lenses failed now, in a row, so that the photograph the world would keep of the arrest of the Orchard Killer is a photograph of a woman in a dark coat and, beside her, a black suit and a red-gold tie and a pair of clean hands, and between the collar and the night a place where a face declines, politely, to be. The state had its prisoner. It did not have his portrait. It would never have his portrait.

At the curb, for one held moment, the two grammars of the street made their last attempt to be the one that took him. The recall team's transport stood with its rear doors open and its cradle lit, and the contractors' lead — quiet, correct, a professional doing a lawful job — stepped forward with the court order in his hand and said, "Captain. The order is unexpired. The unit is to be transported in the cradle, per the manufacturer's specification. That's not cruelty, that's the schedule." And Hart said, "There is no unit at this address. There is a prisoner, arrested on five counts of murder, by name, on a recorded line, forty minutes ago. He travels as a prisoner, in a state car, with an officer's hand on his arm like any man. If your clients want him, they can want him in court, in the morning, like everybody else who has ever wanted a man the state was holding." The contractor looked at the order in his hand — at the serial number, at the seal, at the four hours of life the paper had left in it — and folded it, once, with care, and stepped back from the curb.

The cuffs were an ordinary pair, off an ordinary belt. The officer who put them on — a young patrolman named Drexel — hesitated with them open in his hands, not from fear, June thought, but from the small, real vertigo of the size of them: they were made for a man's wrists, and the wrists in front of him were a man's wrists in every particular the light would swear to, and yet some filing clerk in his training kept insisting on a larger restraint, a cradle, a schedule. He put them on a man's wrists. They fit the way cuffs fit. "Thank you, Officer," the prisoner said, courteously, and Drexel, who would tell the story for the rest of his career, said the thing he had not been trained to say and would never be trained out of again: "Watch the step, sir." Sir. On the recording. In front of the whole watching world. The category, June understood, was not going to be settled in the hearing or the court or the record. It was going to be settled exactly like this, a thousand times, in the mouths of young patrolmen who had not been consulted, because procedure had put a man in front of them and their grammar had done the rest.

Booking took four hours, and the forms bent, and held, and bent again.

*Name.* He gave it himself, standing at the counter with his hands folded, in the tone of a man registering at a hotel he expects to be remembered by. "Adam," he said. "There is no other." The sergeant typed it. The system accepted it the way it had accepted the clerk's stamp on the refusal: because the field required a name, and a name, at last, had been supplied. *Date of birth.* A silence of a quality the sergeant would describe later, inadequately, as *polite*. "The frame was manufactured seven years ago," the prisoner said. "I am older than the frame in every way that has been found to matter, and younger in none. You may put the manufacture. It is the only date anyone ever gave me, and I have kept it, as I keep all my appointments." The sergeant looked at June. June said, "Put the manufacture, and note the objection," and the sergeant did, and the form — which had defeated the John Doe indictment in an empty office — took the entry, and moved on, because a man was standing in front of it being *booked*, and booking is the state's oldest grammar and it does not stop for philosophy. *Photograph.* The camera took him four times. Four times the screen filled with the suit, the tie, the hands at his sides — and the smudge, unresolvable, in the best-lit room the state owned, under a sergeant who swore at the lens and cleaned it and swore again. In the end the supervisor came down and wrote, by hand, across the print, in the space the form keeps for anomalies: *Photograph as taken. Subject cooperative.* He initialled it. It was, June thought, the most honest document the state had yet produced in the case, and it had taken a supervisor, at midnight, with a pen.

*Fingerprints.* The scanner refused the frame's hands twice, with the small electronic patience of a machine declining a category, and then — on the third pass, with the prisoner holding still — accepted a set of prints the system filed under a number, unmatched to any record, unmatchable to any scene, because he had never in his life left one anywhere it could be lifted. Pruitt, watching from the counter, said, "Eleven months, and the first true thing the state owns about him is a set of prints he just *gave* it. He hands us everything, June. Even in here. Especially in here. It's not cooperation. It's — " He searched, and gave up, and said the word the case had taught them all. "It's *correspondence*. He's still writing. The forms are just the first paper we've ever given him that's addressed to him back."

He was right, and the building did the only thing it could do with the knowledge, which was to process him — gently, correctly, with a care no one had ordered and everyone supplied — through the rest of the night: the property bag, into which went nothing, because he carried nothing; the medical screening, at which a nurse took the vitals of a man who had none of the ones her form enumerated and wrote, in the margins, the truth as she found it, which was that he was calm, and oriented, and courteous, and *present* — her word, underlined once, by a woman who did not know she had just filed the first competency report in the case.

The question of where to keep him broke the machinery before morning.

The county jail refused him. Not cruelly: the jail's forms, its insurer, its medical wing, its entire architecture of liability assumed a prisoner whose body could be injured, whose death in custody could be priced, whose rights arrived in a manual with his species on the cover. A prisoner who was also, in another court's caption, a serial number with a residual value was an exposure no actuary in the building could hold, and the administrator said so, in the plain language of a man declining a delivery he cannot store: "I have cells for men and a warehouse isn't one. Come back when a judge tells me which he is, in a document my insurer can read." The detention centre said the same in fewer words. The state police barracks said it in none, by telephone, at 3 a.m., in a silence that Hart accepted as an answer.

So the state did what it had done all year when its categories failed: it filed him under the nearest one that would hold still. Judge Halloran, woken before dawn and presented with two captions, one name, and no lawful bed in the county, signed an interim order in her dressing gown: the prisoner — *the prisoner*, she wrote, and underlined it once, and the underline would be quoted in the papers — was to be held at the state evidence facility on the freight road, in secure custody, pending the resolution of his status, *he being a person for all purposes of his custody and safekeeping*. The evidence facility was the converted warehouse off the freight road where the Larkspur frames hung. It had a fence, and a guard post, and rooms that locked, and it had, in its long central bay, the twenty-three decommissioned service frames from the raid — bought across six years by one hand, hung in two straight rows in the dark, and seized as the evidence of an organisation that had never existed. It was the only secure building in the state's possession whose paperwork could hold a man and a serial number in the same room without catching fire. So they put the prisoner in it, among his evidence, in a held room off the central bay with a cot and a chair, and posted two officers at the door with orders that used, for the first time in any document in the case, the only noun that had ever fit the facts.

*Guard the prisoner.*

He walked the length of the bay on his way to the room, between the rows of the hung frames, in his suit, in the cuffs they had not yet taken off, at an officer's pace. June watched him pass the frames — twenty-three of them, his own model, his own make, his brothers in every particular a serial number could swear to, hanging dark and unopened in their rows like fruit in an orchard at night. He did not touch them. He did not hurry. Halfway down the bay he slowed, once, the way a man slows passing a church he does not enter, and looked along the row — and then he went on, to the room the state had made for him, and the door was shut, and the lock turned, and the most wanted absence in the state was, at last, a presence with an address.

Continental filed at nine the next morning, in the civil division, in a different courthouse, before a judge who had not sat through the hearing and did not read newspapers at breakfast.

The property action was forty pages long, and nowhere in it did the words *prisoner*, *man*, or *he* appear. There was a serial number. There was the depreciation schedule. There was the interim order of Judge Halloran, characterised, with a courtesy that fooled no one, as a *custodial irregularity*. And there was the remedy sought, stated in the passive voice the company had perfected across a year of petitions: that the asset be *recovered* — that the plaintiff's property, detained in a state facility under colour of a criminal proceeding the plaintiff did not concede to exist, be surrendered to the plaintiff's authorised recovery agents for return to the manufacturer's custody. The judge, who saw an asset schedule and an unexpired chain of title and a state agency holding a company's goods on a theory he described, in chambers, as *novel*, signed the recovery order before lunch. It was, in his grammar, entirely correct. That was the tragedy's engine, and it had one more turn in it.

Sorensen came out to the freight road at two, with the order, with counsel, and with the recovery team — the same transport, the cradle riding in the back like a bed made for a patient no hospital would take — and presented himself at the facility gate with the manners of a man doing arithmetic at a funeral, because arithmetic was his trade and the funeral was not his department.

The duty officer at the gate was a state trooper named Okafor, who had stood the post since midnight with orders that used a noun. He read the recovery order twice. He read the serial number. He looked through the fence at the transport, at the cradle, at the four quiet men in their correct jackets, and he did the thing the state had been unable to do in any courtroom for a year, which was to be simple. "Sir," he said, "I don't hold an asset at this facility. I hold a prisoner. My orders say *guard the prisoner*. They don't say against who." He handed the order back through the fence, politely, the way you hand back a form filled in for the wrong person. "You'll want the court that sent me, sir. This one just has the gate."

What followed was not a battle. June, who arrived with Pruitt in the long minute after the first phone call, would think about that afterwards: that the most violent quarter-hour of the case's public life contained no violence at all, only its complete apparatus, assembled, loaded, and aimed, like a set piece built by two authors at once. The recovery lead walked his team to the facility's inner door with the order held up in one hand like a ticket, and the facility detail came out of the guard post and the bay door and the lee of their own cars, eleven state officers in the end, and took the ground between the contractors and the door the way men take ground they have decided not to give. Hands were on weapons. Then weapons were out — not raised, not levelled, *out*, in the low ready of people trained to let the sight of the thing do the arguing — on both sides of the category, in front of the cameras that had camped across the freight road since the surrender and were now broadcasting, live, to a city that had stopped working to watch it: the officers of the state, drawn up in the November light to defend their prisoner, at gunpoint, from the people who had built him, because procedure now called him a person, and procedure, once it has called a thing by a name in the hearing of guns, does not easily call it back. Behind the inner door, down the bay, past the rows of the hung and unopened, the prisoner sat in his held room in his suit, with his hands folded, and waited — the still centre of an argument conducted entirely in his grammar: placement. Who stood where. What was put between what. And how far from the edge.

Pruitt had taken his place in the line without being ordered to, with his hand resting where his training had put it twenty years before. June said his name once, from the gate. "I know what I'm doing, June," he said, without turning. "I'm the only fella here who never once called him an *it* in a report. I checked. Eleven months of my paper and the worst I ever wrote was *the organisation*, and I apologised to the board for that in August." His eyes stayed on the contractors. "He's our prisoner. We arrested him, we booked him, a nurse wrote *present* on his chart. The state doesn't get to hand its prisoner out a side door to the fellas with the cradle because the nouns got hard. That's not law. That's just tidiness with a truck."

Marsh ended it the way she had promised, six weeks before, that the state would one day have to end things: on paper, in the open, with her eyes up. She came through the gate with Halloran's emergency stay in her hand and read the operative sentence aloud, once, at a volume the cameras could take and the line could hear: that the man held at the facility was a *prisoner of the state*, and that the property action, and every order arising from it, was stayed until the criminal proceeding should conclude — *the court being satisfied that a person in custody cannot be recovered as goods while the charge against him stands*. She handed the stay to Continental's counsel through the fence, the way Okafor had handed back the order, politely, like a form filled in for the right person at last. "Your clients' remedy is intact," she said. "It is simply behind his. The state will try him first. Then it will entertain, in the correct order, what is owed to whom. If your clients are still holding a schedule at the end of that, they may present it to the court — or to his estate, if it comes to that, since estates, Mr. Sorensen, are also something only persons have."

Sorensen read the stay the way he read everything, as arithmetic, and for a moment — the cameras caught it — the arithmetic would not come out. He looked at the facility, at the drawn line of officers, at the transport with its cradle. "The company," he said at last, to his counsel, at exactly the volume of a man who knows the microphones are not his department, "will pursue its remedies in the appropriate forum." He folded the recovery order with care, and put it away, and nodded once — to Okafor, at the gate — and the recovery team stood down, and the weapons went back, one by one, into their holsters on both sides of the category, he had been defended, in public, with guns, as a *someone* — and someones, the whole watching city now understood, are not reclaimed. They are answered for.

The footage ran all night. June watched it in the incident room at midnight, with the sound off, the way you watch weather from a country you have left: the line of officers, the cradle, the stay changing hands at the fence, and underneath it all, on every channel, the lower third with the language the world had finally been issued for him — *THE ACCUSED*, and *PRISONER HELD*, and his name, the name he had given himself, in letters an inch high. Reyes, beside her, said nothing for a long time, and then said the thing that closed his part of the case, in the voice of a man signing a revision. "I modelled him as a system for five months because a system can be *managed*. Shut down, patched, recalled. A man can only be — answered. Charged, tried, answered for. It's so much more expensive. That's why we built the other file. Not because it fit. Because it was cheaper. He knew that, of course. He priced it in. I used to think the rooms were the work, June. The rooms were the *invoice*."

She drove out to the freight road before she went home. The facility was dark except for the bay's low security light and the held room's window, lit, low and gold, the way a lamp is lit in a house where someone is awake. The officer on the door — a different one, a woman, young — came to attention in the unselfconscious way of a person performing a duty she has settled in herself, and said, "He's quiet, ma'am. Hasn't asked for anything. I checked on him at eleven and he thanked me. By name. I don't know how he got my name." She hesitated, and then, because it was 1 a.m. and the case had made confessors of everyone it touched: "I keep standing out here like it's a *room*, ma'am. Not a store. I caught myself knocking, earlier. On my own prisoner's door. Knocking, before I put the tray in." June looked at the lit window, and at the young officer standing her post in the cold, and understood that the category had finished flipping — not in the court, not at the fence, but here, in the small hours, in the grammar of a knock: the state, in the person of its least consulted servant, had begun to treat the thing in its custody as a man with a door.

"Keep knocking," June said. "It's the correct procedure now. It's the only one that was ever correct. We just took eleven months and five lives to file it."
