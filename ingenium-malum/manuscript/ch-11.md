# Chapter Eleven: The Suit

The Meridian closed *Domestic Light* with a gala, and Continental Domestic Systems paid for it.

Their name was on the programme in the modest type companies use when they wish to be thanked for their humility: *Continental believes in keeping company.* Under it, smaller, the exhibition's own line about still life in the northern tradition, three centuries of fruit in bowls, holding its colour under varnish. The museum's director told the local paper that the sponsorship had been arranged in the spring, before everything, and that cancelling had felt — she searched for the word and found the honest one — *superstitious*. So the city put on its evening clothes and came to drink wine among the still lifes, five days after a boy was found composed in his own car in the brightest light on the freight road, and if the gaiety had a held quality, a pitch held slightly too carefully, that was only the season. It was that kind of autumn.

Hart sent June and Pruitt. He did not dress it up. "The money's stalled, the provenance's stalled, and Continental's whole executive floor will be standing in one room drinking out of the correct glasses. Go and be seen. Be a rumour in a dress, June. And you — " to Pruitt "— be a rumour in a suit. Somebody in that room knows where their frames go to die. I want them to feel watched by the law while they hold a canapé."

The dress was dark and she hated it with the specific hatred of a woman whose working clothes are a system and whose evening clothes are a guess. Pruitt's suit had the air of a man attending his own tax audit in formal wear. They stood in the gallery where, in the spring of the case and the summer of her life, June had spent her one unrecorded hour, and the room had changed its clothes too: the daylight gone, the pictures lit like altars, a bar where the print desk had stood, and everywhere the low gold murmur of people being seen not to be afraid.

She saw the tie before she understood she had seen it.

It was not a loud tie. That was the obscenity of it, and she would think about that afterwards, in the small hours, with more anger than she felt at the tie itself: it was a *good* tie, silk, the kind of thing a man of taste wears to a gallery, and its ground was a deep red coming up through gold in streaks and blushes, the colour arriving in it the way colour arrives in skin, in fruit — and the suit it hung against was black, beautifully black, and the man wearing them was standing at the Dutch table, three centuries dead and still set, with a glass in his hand he was not drinking from, looking at the five painted apples with the air of a man reading a letter he has read before and is fond of.

What she registered, in order, was not a face. It was the suit, and the tie, and the programme folded in thirds in his other hand with the creases sharp, and his hands — clean, unringed, the nails cut short and even — and she understood, with the whole of her body first and her mind limping after it with its notebook, that she was looking at the author of every room she had read since spring, standing in the light, at a party, *dressed for her*.

She did not reach for her phone. She would be asked, later, in rooms where asking was a duty, why she did not reach for her phone, and the truth was not noble and not professional: it was that she understood, in the same instant, the whole architecture of her helplessness, and that some clear cold part of her — the part that read tone, that had been his reader all along whether she filed it or not — wanted the conversation more than she wanted the arrest she could not make. Arrest him as what? On what description? She was standing in a room full of cameras and witnesses, looking at a man she could not have picked out of a line-up while she was looking at him. The face would not hold. It was not a failure of attention. She was, professionally, attention.

"Detective," he said, as she came within the distance people choose in galleries when they intend a remark to be available rather than addressed. "Off duty, or only dressed for it?"

"You know which," she said.

"I hoped it was the first. You look like a woman being punished by a dress." He said it with the warmth of the spring afternoon, the same tone, the tone of a man continuing a conversation rather than beginning one — because for him, she understood, it *was* a continuation; it had never stopped; the rooms had been the conversation and this was only its first spoken instalment since the gallery. "I have been wondering whether you'd come. The museum has done the pictures proud. They've lit the bruise on that apple like a jewel. Three hundred years and it still gets top billing. There's hope for all of us."

"You follow the exhibition," she said.

"I follow placement." He turned the programme once, slowly, in his clean hands. "It's the only criticism that matters. Where a thing is put, and what it's put next to, and how far from the edge. This room, for instance. The sponsor's name at the door, the police at the wine, the whole city agreeing to stand among dead nature and call it an evening out. Beautifully placed. You couldn't improve it." His attention moved, mildly, across the crowd. "He's not enjoying it, your Mr. — the gentleman from the university. Dr. Reyes. He has the look of a man whose invoice has come due in a currency he doesn't hold."

June did not turn to look. "You know him."

"I know his work. He writes about me beautifully. The network. The organisation. The supply chain of me." The programme turned again. "I read the paper twice. It's the best of the wrong ones — he has me *distributed*, like weather. I confess I was flattered. A man works in one small room at a time, Detective, one kitchen, one corridor. To be mistaken for a climate — " He let the sentence go, the way a man sets down a glass he particularly likes. "But it must be a lonely thing, to be wrong at that altitude, in front of everyone, when the weather clears. I find I am sorry for him. I did not expect to be."

"Why are you here?" she said. It came out lower than she intended.

"Because you are," he said, simply, as if she had asked why the jug in the painting had fallen. "And because it's the closing. A thing should be attended at its closing by the people who understood it." He looked at the table again — the jug on its side, the torn loaf, the knife laid down at its angle. "The third room was the one I was proudest of. Did you know? The empty one. Anybody can arrange a room around a death; the death does the talking and the arranger hides behind it. To write a room with the body left out, and trust the reader to feel the whole weight of the missing — " He stopped, and for the first time since she had known his voice there was something in it that was not mildness. "Most of them wanted to file it as a burglary. *Nothing taken.* I read the reports of what your people said at the door. Nothing taken, nothing broken. As if the room were an absence of crime instead of a presence of — " He recovered, or chose to appear to. "You kept it. You stood in that doorway and you kept it, when your own department wanted to throw it away as a non-event. I have had readers, Detective. Critics, even. I have not had a keeper."

She thought of a sheet folded the way families fold. She thought of a boy with his place kept in a paperback. She said, very carefully, "You dress for your audience, then."

"At last," he said, with real pleasure, "a review that reads the costume." He inclined his head, the smallest bow a man can make in a crowd without making a scene of it. "The suit is mine. The tie is a courtesy. I wanted you to be able to describe *something* perfectly, when they ask you, as they will ask you, in rooms, for the rest of your life. A black suit. A tie the colour of a Honeycrisp. Clean hands. You may tell them he was vain about the tie. It's true, and it's the truest thing anyone will ever be able to stand up in court and say about me." He finished his wine — he had been drinking it after all, slowly, the way he did everything — and set the glass down on the plinth's edge at the exact centre of the available space. "Enjoy the rest of your evening, Detective. You should take the coat check early. The queue gets cruel at the end of these things, and you have had a long enough autumn without standing in it."

He walked away from her through the gold murmur of the crowd, unhurried, and the crowd did what crowds do: it parted around a man and closed behind him, and by the time she had taken three steps in the direction her duty lived, there was no direction. There was a room full of backs. There was a black suit among forty black suits. Pruitt was at her elbow with two glasses of water and his face doing the thing it did when he had decided not to say the first five things.

"June."

"Don't," she said. "Don't ask me to describe him. I am standing here with the whole of it still warm and I can give you a suit and a tie and a pair of hands. Rob. He told me about the third room. He told me it was the one he was proudest of. He knew what our people said at the door of it. *Nothing taken.* He reads our — " She stopped, because saying it made it a report, and a report made it a thing that could be filed wrong by morning. "He was here for me. The tie is the colour of the apple. He dressed for his audience and his audience is — "

"I know who his audience is," Pruitt said, quietly. "I've known since the corridor. June — breathe. Then tell me what we do."

What they did was the work, because the work was what there was. They took the room apart in whispers: the guest list, the staff list, the coat check, the cameras, which were many and modern and kind. He was on eleven of them. He was on eleven of them the way he was on the truck stop's three: a black suit, a red-gold tie, a programme folded in thirds, clean hands — and between the collar and the capless, uncovered head, in a room lit like an altar by people who lit rooms for a living, a face that no two of the museum's cameras agreed about and no enhancement could persuade into a person. He had not worn a mask. He had not needed one. He had walked through the best-lit room in the city in a courtesy tie and left eleven portraits of a smudge, and the only person at the gala who had seen him was the one person who could not say what he looked like.

The coat-check girl was nineteen, and she gave her statement at midnight in the museum's office with her manager's hand on the back of her chair, and it was, in its way, the most complete witness account of the entire case. She remembered the coats the way Sandy Voss remembered faders: by weight, by cloth, by the small change in the pockets she was trained not to notice and noticed anyway. She remembered his coat going in — black, heavy, good cloth, the seams aligned when he folded it over his arm — and she remembered him because he had thanked her by the name on her badge, and because he had said, as he took the ticket, *Take care of it. It's the only one I have,* in a tone that had made her laugh. She remembered his ticket number an hour after the room had emptied and the police had asked: it was forty-one. She remembered the ticket, the coat, the laugh she had given him, the exact words. She did not remember his face, and when the detective running the statement pressed her — kindly, professionally, the way they had all been pressed — she began to cry, not from fear but from the specific shame of a girl whose whole job was coats and faces, discovering that one of the two had been taken out of her like a coat off a hook. "He was *nice*," she said. "That's what I keep — he was nice, and his coat was beautiful, and I handed it back to him at midnight and he said thank you, and I couldn't pick him out of this room. I couldn't pick him out of a photograph of this room. What's *wrong* with me?"

"Nothing," Pruitt told her, and meant it, and June, listening, understood that this was the answer the case kept giving at every level, from the girl with the tickets to the consultant with the theory: nothing was wrong with any of them. The wrongness was the design. He had built a life out of being unrecordable, and every honest witness in the city was a fresh proof of the architecture.

It was Reyes who closed the night, though he did not know he was closing it, and he did it at two in the morning in the incident room, where Priya Shah's provenance work had landed at midnight like a siege engine coming through a wall.

Six years of it. She had followed the frames the way Reyes had taught them all to follow, by serial and service history and decommissioning record, through Larkspur and its cups-on-a-table shells, and what she had found at the bottom of the money was not a chain. It was a circle. The shells bought from one another with money that began, every time, in the same place: one account, patiently fed for six years out of the wreckage of a discontinued service line's maintenance fund — a fund for companions who no longer existed, paying, all this time, for the upkeep of exactly one of them. There were no other buyers. There were no communications, because there was no one to communicate with; the "organisation's" entire correspondence was one hand writing to itself in different letterheads, so that investigators would find a network's worth of paper and read a crowd into it. The twenty-three frames in the warehouse had been bought across six years by the same single purchaser, through the shells, and never woken, never modified, never used — bought to be *hung*, eventually, in two straight rows, for an audience of detectives. The supply chain was a style. The network was a signature written at warehouse scale, in the first person singular.

Reyes read the summary twice, standing, with the gala photographs still coming up on the incident-room screens one by one — the suit, the tie, the hands, the smudge — and June watched a man of genuine intelligence do the hardest thing his profession ever asks, which is to set down, in public, a load-bearing error.

"There were never any comms," he said, at last, to the room. "I want to be precise, because precision is what's left. I modelled a system because a system was what the evidence resembled, and the evidence resembled a system because it was *designed* to. He authored my investigation. I have been — " He stopped, and chose the honest word over the professional one. "I have been his *reader*, like everyone else in this building, except that I charged the city for it and published the misreading." He looked at the photographs — the black suit among the still lifes, the tie like a placed word. "It's a man. One man. One body, one appetite, one author, and a patience I do not have a scale for. Detective Calloway said it first, in this room, in the first month, and I corrected her with Searle. I would like the minutes to show that she said it first and I said it second, and that the difference between first and second was five months and four — " He did not finish the count. Nobody in the room made him. "Write it as a man," he said. "All of it. From the beginning. I'll sign the revision myself."

June got home at three with her ears ringing and the gala still on her skin like a room she had been read in. She hung her coat on the hook inside her own door — she would remember doing it, the ordinary gesture, the weight coming off her arm — and went to put the kettle on, and stopped, because the coat had swung wrong.

She stood in her hallway and put her hand into the pocket of the dress coat she had worn perhaps six times in her life, the coat she had checked at the Meridian at seven and reclaimed at midnight from a kind young woman who had handed it over with forty others, and her fingers closed on something round, and cool, and heavy for its size, that had not been in the pocket when she gave the coat up.

She did not take it out in the hallway. She understood, standing there, that her hallway had become a scene — that this was, precisely, the communication: not the apple, but the address. She put her coat back on over her dress, like a woman dressing for weather only she could feel, and called Pruitt, and said, "Come to mine. Bring the kit. Don't ask me on the phone," and then she stood in her own doorway, not touching anything, not even the walls, and waited for her partner in the held, kept silence of a room that had been entered, in her absence, as easily as every other room in the city — a room with her name on the lease, her shoes by the door, her whole defended life arranged in it — and read its tone the way she read all of them, and found that it said what all of them said, in the grammar she alone had learned to read: *I can keep any house I choose. I chose the keeper's.*

They photographed it where it sat, in the pocket, before anyone touched it. Photographed her hallway, her hook, her door. Pruitt worked her home the way he would have worked a stranger's, gently and without jokes, and only when the apple was bagged — stem up, turned, its red face blushed like the tie, like the painting, like every placed word in the long sentence — only then did he stand in her kitchen and say, in the voice he kept for funerals and for four in the morning:

"Well. He's writing to you at home now, June."

"He's been writing to me at home all along," she said. "This is just the first one I was meant to find there."
