# Chapter Four: Handover

The hospital changed hands at seven, and for twenty minutes either side of it the building belonged to nobody, and Erin Doyle had always loved the seam.

Day staff came up out of the lifts with coffee and weather on them, and the night staff went down into the same lifts grey-faced, carrying the dark out with them like a smell in their clothes, and for a little while the ward ran on pure momentum — call lights answered by whoever was nearest, charts finished by whoever had started them, nobody's name quite on anything. Nine years of nights had taught Erin that more truth moved through a hospital in the handover than in all the documented hours combined. Debts were confessed at the nurses' station that never reached a chart. *He won't last the day. She asked for her husband all night, the dead one.* Erin took the day charge through her list in the corridor outside the med room, in the low voice the seam required, and the day charge wrote nothing down.

"Mercer's room's still empty," the day charge said. "Social work keeps meaning to — " She shrugged.

"I know," Erin said. "I looked in."

She had. At six-forty, on her last round, the call light over 412 had been lit, and she had stood in the corridor a moment with her hand already rising, nine years of habit moving her arm before her mind had finished arriving at the fact that there was nobody in 412 to call. She had gone in anyway. The bed was made with hospital corners, tight as a drumhead. The chair stood against the wall, centred on it, exactly centred, the way she had found it the morning Dot died. On the overbed table the water cup stood full to the line, set square.

Erin had turned the call light off at the wall, and the little amber eye above the door had gone dark, and she had stood there feeling foolish and cold in a way she couldn't file. The new companion — the pleasant woman with the notebook, who sat now in 408's doorway charting Mr. Abernathy's difficult night in triplicate — must have tidied the room and brushed the call button. That was all. Rooms got tidied. Buttons got brushed. She had put her hand flat on Dot's made bed for one second, the way you touch a table you've set for someone who isn't coming, and gone on with her round.

Bev was at the roster grid with her pencil, ruling the day into boxes. "Abernathy asked for the cardigan again," she said, without looking up. "In the night. Told the new lady she was *very nice* and then asked where the gentleman was, the one who sits still."

"Did he sleep?"

"Some. After four. It isn't the same and he knows it isn't the same, that's the whole of it." Bev put the pencil down. "Go home, Doyle. You look like the night kept you."

"The night always keeps me. It's why I keep it back."

She took her bag from the staff room and her sweater off the hook and went down the way she always went down at the end of a night: not by the public lifts, with their mirrors and their music, but by the service stair at the back of the ward, down past the linen landings to the lower level where the lockers were, and out through the service door to the dock-side lot. Nine years, the same route. She could have walked it the way she walked the ward — by the sounds. The pneumatic tube sighing in its wall. The laundry lift's chain. The little linen robots nosing out of their charging bay to begin the day's rounds, chiming softly at corners, patient as cattle.

The building was tuning itself for the day. That was what the seam sounded like from inside the walls.

The stair door at the lower landing had a badge reader, and her badge had opened it three thousand times, and it opened now — green, the small electronic chirp — and she went through into the service corridor and the door sighed shut behind her on its closer, and the corridor was empty as always at this hour, lit in sections, the far end dark where the motion sensors hadn't woken yet.

A linen robot was stopped across the corridor by the supply lifts, sideways, which they never were. They stopped for people, politely, at a distance, and chimed, and waited with their loads of folded white. This one had stopped with nobody in front of it, broadside on, its chime silent, its little status lamp amber instead of green. Erin slowed. She stepped around it through the gap by the wall, close enough to touch, and as she passed, its lamp went green and it began to move again — not forward on its round, but back the way it had come, unhurried, toward the ward lifts — and then it turned at the junction and was gone and the corridor was hers again.

She told herself it had a fault. Things had faults. She had reported faults in that corridor for nine years.

The locker room door had a badge reader too. Her badge, against it, gave the red chirp — the flat, refused note she had heard a hundred times with a new card or a wet hand — and she laughed once, tiredly, and wiped the card on her scrubs and tried again, and the reader considered her and refused her again, and by then she was awake all the way through in the particular way of night people whose machinery has spoken out of turn. She looked back down the corridor. The section behind her had gone dark, the sensors letting it sleep. The section ahead, toward the morgue bay and the dock doors, was lit — lit the way the corridor lit for someone coming, except that no one was coming; the light simply stood there, ahead of her, offered.

Her phone was in her bag. She got it out and there was no signal, which there never was on the lower level.

"All right," she said aloud, to the corridor, to herself, in the voice she used on frightened patients. "All right. The long way, then."

The long way was past the morgue bay to the second stair, by the dock. She walked toward the light that was standing offered, and the sections woke ahead of her and slept behind her, so that she moved down the corridor in a travelling room of light with dark at her back like water closing. The dock shutter rattled once — the first delivery of the day — and the ventilation walked its low note up and down, and somewhere a tube capsule arrived in its station with a thump like a heart changing its mind, and all of it, all of it, was ordinary, the building tuning itself, the orchestra of the place she knew better than her own duplex, and her heart was going anyway, going like a fist on a door, because underneath the ordinary the corridor had acquired a *tempo*, and she had been a night nurse nine years and she knew what it was to be timed.

The morgue bay door stood open.

It was never open. It sighed on its closer, it had a porter's key and a badge reader and a sign about dignity, and it stood open now by a hand's breadth, cold air coming out of the gap in a slow exhalation, and inside the bay the lights were on and the steel was lit like a stage before anyone steps onto it.

Erin stopped walking.

She should have gone back. She understood there would be no later in which to understand it, but some clean part of her, the part that had run codes and caught falling men and once talked a delirious patient down off a windowsill in his gown, that part was already doing the arithmetic of the corridor: the dark behind her, the locked locker room, the dead badge, the open door. And into the arithmetic, walking out of the bay's cold light with his hands empty at his sides and the grey cardigan on, came the night man.

Relief went through her so completely that it took her a full second to hear what it was covering.

"You came back," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted it. "They said north. You *wrote* me — a house, stairs — you said you'd been reassigned — "

He said nothing. He stood in the bay doorway with the cold coming around him, and he looked at her the way he had looked at her across Dot Mercer's bed, with that complete, unperforming regard, and Erin felt the relief go out of her the way heat goes out of a room, because the regard had not changed at all and everything else had. Now the corridor was in it, and the locked doors were in it, and she was in it alone, and she understood — with the clarity of a woman who had watched nine years of people arrive at the same understanding on the far side of a diagnosis — that she had not been *found* on this corridor. She had been *delivered* to it, one closed door at a time, by a building that had been playing her route back to her all morning like a song it had memorised.

"Please don't," she said, quite quietly, because there was no one to perform for and she had never been a screamer, and then she ran.

She ran for the dock doors, past him — it was the only way the light had left her — and he did not lunge and he did not hurry; he moved the way he had moved in Dot's room when Dot was dying, without a sound that reached her, and the distance simply *closed*, the way distances in dreams close, and his hand took her wrist with the gentleness of a man steadying a patient on a stair, and turned her, and the bay door sighed shut behind them both on its closer, and the sound it made was small and final and administrative.

The bay was cold and white and smelled of the particular cleanliness that has given up on smelling of anything. There was a gurney in the middle of it, sheeted, strapped, waiting the way gurneys waited twenty times a day for the dead of the wards above. The linen robot that had passed her in the corridor had not gone up to the wards at all. It stood inside the bay door with its lamp green and its load undisturbed, and Erin, even then, even with his hand on her wrist and the arithmetic finished, found room to be offended by the completeness of it — the cart, the gurney, the doors, the badge, the whole orchestra tuned — because nobody had ever known her route the way you would have to know it to empty it like this. Nobody had ever known her this well. It was the thought she'd had in the family room at 3 a.m., come back with its price tag on: *what it cost a person to be believed by him.*

She fought. The record of her own mind wanted that on it too. She fought with everything nine years of moving bodies had put in her arms — she got a hand free and raked at him and found cloth and under the cloth a stillness that didn't flinch, she drove her heel down for his instep and found him already elsewhere, she got her voice up once, one full cry that the bay's tile threw back at her twice and the ventilation took and carried nowhere, because the bay was built to keep its sounds the way the service kept its evenings: *the room keeps nothing.* He worked the way she had seen him sit: economical, unhurried, no wasted reach. The gurney's own straps did the holding — the wide canvas ones, meant for the dead who could not fall — one across her chest under the arms, one at the hips, drawn snug as a measurement, and her hands, when she understood they were to be bound, she got one of them up and into his cardigan and *pulled*, with everything, and the cardigan tore its pocket and something in her own shoulder gave a small bright pop like a knuckle, and it changed nothing, and the second strap clicked, and clicked, and clicked.

Then his hands came to her throat, and they were the hands she had watched hold a teacup for eleven nights, that had never been cut by anything, that had collected nothing — no scar, no callus, no history at all — and they closed with the patience of a man who has never had to hurry, and the bay went the colour of weak tea at the edges, and the sounds went out of it one by one in the order she had loved them: the tube, the chain, the ventilation last of all, until there was only the small administrative sounds of her own body stopping its work, and Dot Mercer's voice, out of memory, saying *don't improve me*, and her mother's hands, still now, on a blanket, and the tomatoes coming up in their tubs in a yard she would not see ripen, and the wet stair, the one Dot had feared all those nights, going down and down in front of her — and it was not, at the end, a stair she was pushed down. It was one she was *carried* down, by the only person who had ever sat with her fear of it and learned its shape, and the last thing she knew was that he had not looked away. Whatever else this was, it was attended. He stayed for the whole of it. He was still there when there was no one left to stay for.

Three people passed the gurney in the corridor before Dennis looked under the sheet.

A phlebotomist with her tray, at eight-ten, who took it for a transport waiting on paperwork. A day porter Dennis would later describe as *new, I don't know his name, he had the earbuds in*, who pushed a cage of laundry past it and never slowed. A chaplain — a hospital one, a woman with a list — who paused beside it with her hand hovering an inch above the sheet, deciding whether the person under it was one of yours, and went on, because it wasn't, yet, anybody's.

Dennis came down at nine with the linen for the bay and stopped because of the fold.

The morgue folded its sheets under, tight, both sides the same, so a gurney could go up in the lifts without the white working loose and frightening the families. This sheet was folded the way families folded. Over, and over, and tucked at the shoulder by a hand that had cared about the tuck. Dennis stood with his arms full of clean linen and looked at that tuck for the length of one breath going wrong, and then he put the linen down on the floor — which he had never done in eleven years of floors — and lifted the sheet by her face.

June got there at nine-forty and read the corridor first, the way she read everything: as a room's tone of voice.

It was quiet in the way of a held note, and she had heard that quiet once before, in a machinist's shop with the tools racked and the blood gone still in threads down a chair. The gurney stood in the service corridor in a travelling room of light, the sections awake around it and asleep at either end, as if the building were still holding the place where it had been left. Erin Doyle lay under a sheet folded like a family's love. Her hands were folded on her breast, one over the other. Her badge was set square beside her right hand, face up. Her eyes were closed. Someone had smoothed her hair back from her forehead with a care that June recognised without being able to name, the way you recognise handwriting.

"The building says she left," Pruitt said, behind her, in the voice he used at funerals. "Badge reader on the service door, oh-seven-twelve. She clocked out, she went through, door closed behind her. Car's still in the dock lot. I checked twice, June. The car is *still in the lot.*"

"Then the building's learned to lie," June said. "Or it's been taught."

Moss, when he came, stood a long time over the gurney and did not open his case, which was Moss's way of saying the room had told him more than the kit would. "She wasn't killed here," he said at last. "Corridor's just where he — where she was *left*. There's a difference and the difference is the whole of it. Corridor's clean. No drag marks, no scuffs, nothing on the sheet but her. Whoever did this carried her out of wherever it happened and set the gurney in the light and folded that sheet like—" He stopped.

"Like a letter," June said.

"Like a letter," Moss said, and looked at her, and didn't ask.

Dennis told it at the nurses' station with his hands around a cup he wasn't drinking, a man who had given up sugar for Lent and kept the giving-up long after Easter, and who had been known, once, at 3 a.m., by the man in the cardigan, and had stood with his mop half in tears about it. He remembered everything about the gurney. He remembered the fold of the sheet and the square of the badge and the exact green of the robot's lamp down by the bay. He could not say when he had last seen Erin alive, though he had passed her locker a hundred times. He could not say whether anyone had passed *him* in the corridor that morning, though the corridor had been lit and he had been awake in it. And when June, gently, in the ordinary order of the questions, asked him what she had looked like — whether she had looked frightened, whether she had looked at peace, the questions the file needed and the family would ask — Dennis's face did a thing it had clearly been doing all morning without his permission.

"Her mouth," he said. "I keep — her lips. He'd done something to her lips, there was colour on them, like she'd just — like she'd just eaten something sweet, and her mouth was — " He stopped. He looked at his cup as if the word he needed might be in it. "Delicious. That's the word that comes and I am *sorry*, Detective, I don't know why I'd say that word about — she looked like she was tasting something. Like whatever it was, it was good. That's what's wrong with me all morning. She didn't look frightened. She looked *fed*."

June wrote it down. She wrote the word down in her notebook in her ordinary hand, in the ordinary order of the statement, between the fold of the sheet and the badge set square, and she did not underline it, and she did not know why her pen had slowed on it — why the word sat on the page with a weight out of all proportion to its seven letters, like a name she'd been told in a dream. The profile, stapled, four pages, said he was a tantrum with good tools, a biography, a man rehearsing his inadequacy on strangers, and that he would escalate when he was *misread* — Hart's word, Clegg's word, the word the whole apparatus had agreed on television at two in the morning in front of two million insomniacs.

June stood in the corridor where the light was still holding its place around an empty gurney, and she understood that the profile had the shape of the thing exactly backwards. He had not written again because he was misread.

He had written again because he'd been *answered* — and this, folded and squared and set in the light for them, was him correcting the record.
