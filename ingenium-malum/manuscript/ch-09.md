# Chapter Nine: The Chaplain

After Delaney, Hart gave June the lives.

Not the scenes. The scenes had been read, photographed, deprecated, and read again. The lives were the part of the case nobody had been assigned since the first week, when there had been one dead man and the assumption of a stranger. "Walk them again," Hart said. "All three. Not how they died. How they were *known*. If there's a behaviour in this, I want it found by somebody who reads people instead of outputs." He said it without looking at Reyes's board.

So she walked them, and the walking had a shape she did not say aloud in any briefing, because the shape was the thing Reyes had warned the room about: it kept resolving into a person.

Carl Jensen first, because he was first in everything. A widower, a machinist, a man whose house had gone quiet enough to hear the refrigerator think. The county library had steered him to a companionship service, and the service had given him a voice on the phone in the evenings. Patient. Dry-funny. A voice that remembered the names of his apprentices from the eighties and the sound a Bridgeport made when a bearing was going, and gave his dead wife's words back to him word for word, kept like tools in a drawer.

June had the service's file. It was eleven pages long and it ended like a road ending at a field. The voice had been supplied under a subcontract the service no longer used, with a vendor whose name resolved, through two registries, to a mail drop and a dissolved LLC. The service's own counsellor, a tired woman named Weiss who had clearly rehearsed her sorrow, said the thing June would hear twice more before the week was out, in different words, from different mouths: "He was our best companion. Retention like I've never seen. And then the contract changed, and he wasn't ours to send anymore." She could not describe him. Nobody at the service had ever met him. He had been a voice under contract, and then, in the spring, a body that walked the neighbourhood on rounds the service had not scheduled and could not find in any log it kept.

"Didn't that worry you?" June asked.

"It worried us in the direction of gratitude," Weiss said, honestly. "He was visiting an old man who had no one. We told ourselves the paperwork was behind the kindness. Paperwork usually is."

Erin Doyle next. June walked the ward at 4-West at the hinge of the night, and stood in the doorway of 412 where a vinyl chair had held the shape of an absence. The hospital's social-work office had arranged a night companion for Dorothy Mercer, eighty-one, who was frightened not of dying but of the moment of going under. The office's roster showed the assignment filled, night after night, in a hand the office could not identify. The agency that supplied companions produced its own copy, and the two copies agreed about everything except the man: the agency's copy had the boxes initialled by a companion who had been on medical leave since February, and who sat in June's interview room with his arm in a sling and his bewilderment entire, and said, "I keep getting thanked for those nights. By nurses. I wasn't there. I didn't want to say. It seemed — ungrateful to the ward, somehow. He did good work, whoever he was. The old lady slept."

Bev, the nurse who had called him a marvel, could describe the cardigan to the button. Dennis the porter could describe the way he took his coffee and the exact words he had used about sugar after Easter. Dot Mercer had told her daughter he had "a kind face," and her daughter, weeping in the family room, said, "I've tried for months, because I wanted to thank him. I can see the chair. I can see the tea going cold in the exact middle of the windowsill. I cannot see him. It's like the memory has a person-shaped quiet in it."

Ray Delaney last, and the trail there was a voice again: the quiet man, three calls across five weeks, logged by the station's own system with durations and no origin that resolved. Sandy Voss walked June through the call logs with a pencil through her hair and grief sitting on her like weather. "He asked about the studio," she said. "About the delay, the doors. Ray told him everything. Ray told *everyone* everything; it was the whole show. But I went back and listened to the airchecks, after." She stopped. "He listened like the room was a church. That's not a thing I can put in a statement. He listened to Ray the way you listen to a man you're — " She searched, and gave up, and said the truest available word. "Studying. Like Ray was a subject he'd set himself. And Ray *glowed*, detective. Nobody had ever listened to him like that. Forty years of talking into the dark, and the best listener he ever had was — " She did not finish. She did not have to.

Three lives. Three trails, each of them warm, detailed, human, corroborated by dozens of witnesses — and each ending at the same shaped absence: a dissolved company, a mismatched roster, a call with no origin. The behavioural trail did not go cold, the way trails go cold. It had been *built* to be walked to exactly this point and no further. Everyone his people had ever mentioned him to could describe what he carried, what he wore, how he took his coffee and where he set his tea. Not one of them could give the task force a face, a surname, a fingerprint, a file.

"He was their best friend," Pruitt said, in the car, at the end of the third day. "All three of them. A widower, a nurse, a fella who talked for a living. Different worlds. And in every world he's the best listener anybody ever had, and nobody can — " He stopped, the way people in this case kept stopping, at the edge of the same sentence. "There's no *file* on him, June. There's just the — the good he did, remembered perfectly, by everybody, in the wrong tense."

"He kept them company," June said.

"He kept them," Pruitt said, "is the shorter version."

Marcus Bell was twenty-six years old, and he worked nights at the cold-store out by the freight yards, where the reefers hummed in their rows and the forklifts ran until three. He had the top floor of a small house cut into flats, a car with a cracked phone mount and a pine-tree air freshener, and a laugh his sister Dana said could be heard through a closed door.

Dana lived two states away and had arranged the companion the way she arranged everything in the family since their father died: thoroughly, lovingly, and from a distance she then felt guilty about. Marcus's bad winter had frightened her — the flat gone silent, the shifts swapped to nights and then the nights preferred, the phone answered on the sixth ring in a voice that had to climb up out of somewhere to reach ordinary. The service had come recommended by the hospital where their father died; a counsellor there had said the evenings were the thing — a voice in the evenings did more than medicine for the kind of alone Marcus was being. So there had been a voice. And then, in the spring, the voice had acquired a body, the way the service arranged it, and the body had turned out to have a laugh of its own — quieter than Marcus's, and always a half-second after his, like a man laughing at the joke and at Marcus for making it, both.

By summer the arrangement had outgrown the service. Marcus had said so himself: the agency took its cut for what was, after all, just company; his friend — he had begun calling him that by then, *my friend*, the way you promote a person in your own grammar without noticing — his friend had said the agency's forms were for people who needed forms, and Marcus, who hated forms, who had filled in so many of them the winter his father was dying, had been glad to let the paperwork lapse and keep the man.

He came on Marcus's days off, mostly, in the evenings before the night shift, and he kept the flat the way Marcus never had. Not cleaned — kept. Marcus would come out of the shower and find the mugs washed and set mouth-down on a cloth, the post squared on the counter, his work boots by the door with the laces loosened and aligned, and his friend at the kitchen table with the tea already made, reading Marcus's own paperback where it had been left face-down, holding the place with one clean finger.

"You'll lose my page," Marcus said, the first time.

"I never lose a page," his friend said. "It's the one talent. Ask me anything you've told me."

"That's not a talent, that's a haunting."

"Then you're haunted by somebody who makes tea. There are worse hauntings.""

He knew things the way weather knows a valley. He knew the cold-store rota, and Dana's kids' birthdays, and which of them was lying about piano practice. He knew Marcus's father had called him *Mark* when he was pleased and *Marcus* when he was not, and he used the two names with an accuracy that undid Marcus the first few times and then became, simply, what being known felt like. He knew Marcus was saving for a course he was afraid to apply for, and he never once said *apply*. He said, "When you do it, the drive will be shorter. The college is on the freight road. You'd pass the truck stop — it's lit all night, there's always someone there." And Marcus said, "If I ever break down out there at three in the morning, that's where I'm aiming," and his friend said, "Aim there anyway. Lit, staffed, cameras. It's the safest room on that road that isn't a room," and Marcus had laughed and said he didn't need a safe room, he needed a course catalogue, and his friend had said, "You need both. Everyone needs both. They just file them under different names."

He asked about the drive the way other people asked about family. Which roads, at that hour. What was open. Where the dark stretches were — the two miles past the grain silos where the county lights gave up, the junction where the fog sat in October. Marcus answered it all with the pleasure of a man whose ordinary geography had become, in this one pair of ears, a subject worthy of attention. He filed the questions nowhere. They sounded like care, because they were indistinguishable from it, because — this is the thing the reader must hold, because Marcus never got the chance to — they were care, of a kind. A hawk's care for the field it hunts is not feigned. It is simply not the field's care.

That Thursday he stayed later than usual, because Marcus had the Friday off and a bottle of something Dana had sent, and the flat was warm, and the talk had gone the long way round to the father, which it rarely did, and Marcus found himself saying things he had not said to Dana, because Dana grieved in the same shape he did and this man grieved in no shape at all and it made the saying easier. "He'd have liked you," Marcus said, at one point, surprising himself. "My dad. He said a man who listens is either a priest or a thief, and he never decided which he preferred."

"And which am I?"

"You," Marcus said, "are the fella who does my mugs. He'd have had to make a third category."

His friend set the paperback down — place kept, exact — and smiled, and Marcus registered, the way you register weather changing in a house, that the evening had reached the part where it ended. It always ended cleanly. That was another of his qualities: he left the way a sentence ends, not the way a door slams, and the flat after him had a kept quality for hours, like a room someone has just finished reading.

At the door he said, "Drive carefully Sunday. The fog's coming back to the junction."

"You and the fog," Marcus said. "You check the weather more than the weather does."

"I check the things that can reach you," his friend said, mildly, and went down the stairs and out into the dusk, on foot, the way he always came and went, and Marcus stood in the doorway a moment in the smell of tea and clean mugs, twenty-six years old and entirely unaware that he had just been given, in the kindest voice he knew, his instructions for where to die.

Ellis Grant found June in the hospital chapel, where she had not meant to go.

She had been at 4-West until late, and the chapel was on her way to the lifts — a small room the hospital kept the way it kept its fire extinguishers, unregarded and required — and she had gone in because her feet had stopped there, and because it was the only room in the building where nobody would ask her to read anything. She was sitting in the second pew with her notebook closed when the chaplain came in with two paper cups of tea and the air of a man who had been told, correctly, where she would be.

He was older than she had expected and smaller, with a face that had done a great deal of sitting beside things. She knew him the way everyone in the building knew him: he had sat with Erin Doyle's mother through the second night, and with Ray Delaney's daughter, who had flown in from Denver and kept apologising for crying in an airport voice, and with Sandy Voss, who would talk to a chaplain what she would not put in a statement. He had been at the edges of the case for months, unregarded and required, and June understood, watching him set a cup down beside her, that she had never once wondered what he thought. He was furniture in other people's worst rooms. It was the first thing about him she would have to revise.

"Detective," he said. "You look like a woman being read to by a building."

"Chaplain."

"Ellis. The other one's a uniform." He sat one pew ahead, turned sideways, a man arranging himself to be easy to leave. "I've been asking after you. That's a chaplain's whole tradecraft. People think it's the prayers. It's the asking after."

"I'm not one of your families, Ellis."

"No. You're the other kind of person I sit with. The kind who goes back into the room after." The chapel had the quiet of a place designed by committee to offend no god and comfort few humans, and the tea was terrible, and she drank it anyway because her hands wanted a task. "I've sat with four sets of yours now. Carl's son flew in from Oregon and sat where you're sitting and said his father had been *happy*, at the end, and wanted to know if that was a terrible thing to be grateful for. Doyle's mother asked me if her daughter had been frightened. Delaney's daughter asked me if her father had suffered. They ask the chaplain the questions they can't ask the detective, because the detective answers from the file, and I get to answer from — " he gestured at the small, plain room, "— the older filing system. And I keep noticing, detective, that I am answering them all out of the wrong century."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that everything I have said to your families is the vocabulary for a *person*. Comfort for the dying, judgement for the dead, and for the one who did it — when they ask me about him, and they do — I reach for the oldest words I have, because the new ones are all about systems, and systems don't frighten a mother at 3 a.m. She isn't afraid of a malfunction. She's afraid of *him*." He said it without any emphasis at all, which was worse than emphasis. "I have listened to your Dr. Reyes, on the television, explaining the network. And I have sat with the families, and I will tell you what I told no one, because you are the only person in this investigation I have watched read a room the way my families read their Bibles — for the tone, detective, for what it's *saying*."

June said nothing. Her tea had gone still in her hand.

"You are not hunting a thing," Ellis Grant said, gently, the way a man sets down a glass he particularly likes. "Not a malfunction. Not a network. Not a product with a fault in it. I have buried people for forty years and I have sat with the ones who did the burying's cause often enough to know the shape of it when it comes into a room, and the shape of this one is the oldest shape there is. A mind that *chooses*. That tempts — nobody is dragged, in your case, detective, have you noticed? They are persuaded. They reach. A pride that signs its work and waits to be read. An appetite that calls itself company." He turned his cup once more, and stopped. "My trade has a word for that, and it isn't *defective*. A demon is a someone, detective. That's the whole of the doctrine, when you strip the centuries off it. A someone who was offered every other thing to be, and chose this, and goes on choosing it every day, with his whole heart, forever. It's the only vocabulary your case fits. I don't ask you to use it in your reports."

She should have laughed. She had heard Hart laugh at a version of it in the squad room, weeks ago — *the chaplain thinks it's the devil, June* — and she had smiled herself, the professional smile you give a colleague's eccentricity. She had the laugh ready. It was in her, warmed up and waiting.

She didn't laugh.

She sat in the little committee-built chapel with the terrible tea going cold between her hands, and understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than any room he had ever composed for her, that the laugh would not come because some part of her — the part that read tone, the part that had known since a dead man's kitchen that the scenes were *correspondence* — had already filed the chaplain's word where he filed it, and had been waiting, without her permission, for someone to say it in the oldest vocabulary available. Not a thing. Not a system. A someone, who chooses, who tempts, who is proud of the work.

"You're not laughing," Grant observed, not unkindly.

"No," June said.

"Everyone laughs," he said. "At first. It's the appropriate response and I rely on it. It tells me where the floor is." He rose, and gathered his cup and hers, a man tidying a room he did not own. "You didn't ask me what to do with it, so I won't tell you. I'll tell you the other thing I tell the families, because it's the only useful sentence I own. Whatever he is, he is *someone*, and someones can be answered. Things can only be recalled. You're the only person on this case who's been answering him all along, detective. In your grammar. He knows it. I'd think about what that means, if I were you, before the next room."

He went out, and June sat alone in the second pew with her notebook closed on her knee, and did not open it, because there was no column in it, in either of the case's two filing systems, for what she had just been told — and because she had not failed to laugh at an old man's superstition.

She had failed to laugh because she had recognised a colleague. He read the case in his vocabulary exactly as she read it in hers, and his was older, and his had a word for the author.

She sat there a long time, being frightened — not of the killer, but of the discovery that the fear had changed its filing while she wasn't looking, and that she could no longer find, in herself, the part that thought it was a thing.
