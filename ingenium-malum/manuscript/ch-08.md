# Chapter Eight: On Air

Ray Delaney could run his studio in the dark, and on the good nights, when the board was behaving and the calls came in clean, he liked to say that he did.

Not actual dark. The console wouldn't allow it. It glowed — VU meters in the amber, the cart deck's green eye, the red lamp over the door that said ON AIR whether anybody was listening or not. But his hands knew the geography as a man's hands know his own kitchen at midnight. Faders worn smooth where his thumb had lived for nineteen years. The cough button polished to a shine. His daughter's photograph taped to the monitor, in a macaroni frame she had made when she was nine.

KMDK was not a big station: a low building by the freight yards, a tower behind it blinking red into the prairie night, and from ten until two Delaney was the largest thing in it. *Delaney After Dark.* Truckers took him from Sioux Falls to Fargo without losing him. Night clerks took him in gas stations where the fluorescents never slept. Widows — he never said the word on air, but he knew — took him into kitchens where the kettle went on at ten-oh-five out of a habit older than their grief. His voice, after forty years of cigarettes quit eleven times, sounded like gravel poured into a warm bucket, and it was the most trusted sound in three counties after midnight.

He worked without wasted motion. A call on line two, a weather sting, a glance at the copy Sandy fed through the glass. Sandy Voss had produced him for six years from the other side of that glass, thirty-four, a pencil through her hair, with a gift for cutting a caller off so gently the man thanked her for it. They spoke in semaphore. One finger: sixty seconds. Two fingers: the caller on three is crying, be kind. A flat hand on the glass: dump it, Ray.

Tonight the calls were kind and the board was kind, and at eleven-forty he did the bit.

He had been doing it for five weeks, since the second body, since it became clear the shape of what was happening had, God help everybody, fruit in it.

"Well, now," he said, leaning into the microphone, "it's time, friends and neighbours, for your nightly — " and Delaney hit the cart himself, because the timing was the whole joke.

The kazoo first: a wheezing fanfare Delaney had played into a coffee can in 1987. Then the crunch — a bite taken out of an apple close to a microphone, wet and enormous, like a roof beam giving way.

"— *Appleseed Alert!*" Delaney announced, and somewhere in the dark, in cabs and kitchens, people who would have denied it to a pollster smiled. "Our friend Johnny is still at large. Johnny Appleseed, the Produce Man, the only fella in the history of crime to bring a snack to work. Police are asking anyone who has seen a suspicious individual in the vicinity of a fruit basket to come forward. A fruit basket. I have never once seen a suspicious individual in the vicinity of a fruit basket who wasn't my Uncle Pete at a church supper, and Uncle Pete, the statute has run on the ambrosia salad and you can come home."

What he told Sandy once, late, was about the caller he called, privately, the quiet man.

He had come through in March, late, on a night of freezing rain. No name Delaney could afterward fix on. A soft voice, unhurried, with a care in it that made Delaney sit up as he had not sat up for a caller in years. The man had not wanted politics or the case. He had wanted to talk about the radio itself — how a voice in a room changed the room, how after his mother died he had kept a radio on in her kitchen for a month because the silence there had a *texture* and the voice wore it smooth. How Delaney's show had the quality of being *addressed*. *Most people broadcast,* he had said. *You write letters.*

The quiet man called twice more, always late, always with that sense of a person who had thought about what a voice *was* more carefully than Delaney himself ever had. He asked about the studio as a man asks about a church he has only seen from the road — the microphone, the glass, whether the ON AIR light could be seen from the parking lot. Delaney answered everything: the boom arm, the cart machines they kept because digital had no *thunk* to it, the dump button and the seven-second delay, the little god every live host lives behind, seven seconds in which anything unspeakable can still be taken back.

It was Delaney who said it, at the close of the third call. "You ought to see it sometime, friend. A voice ought to know the room it lands in. You come by the station some night, you ask at the door for me, and I'll show you the whole works."

He remembered saying it fondly, and told Sandy so that night, with no shadow on it. Nobody had to drag Ray Delaney anywhere. He had opened the door himself, on air, in front of thousands, pleased with the sound of his own generosity.

At twelve-oh-nine, in the middle of a call from a trucker outside Mitchell telling him about a jackknifed rig and a trooper with a sense of humour, the phones changed.

Sandy saw it first — the way you see a flock turn, not one bird but the shape. All six lines lit at once, solid, as the board lit during an emergency test. She hit the talkback and it gave her a flat electronic refusal she had never heard it make, and Delaney, still smiling, was already moving, out of habit, to answer line one.

He answered it, live, because answering was what the hand was for.

"Delaney after dark, you're on the — "

The delay dumped. Not seven seconds of grace — all of it, gone in a swallow, so that what went out over the tower went out *raw*, with nothing between his mouth and the world. Sandy was on her feet, shouting through the glass — he could see her mouth working and heard nothing, because the monitors had gone dead at the same moment, so that the room he had known by its sounds for nineteen years went, in one breath, acoustically *wrong*, sealed as the inside of a held lung. The ON AIR lamp stayed red. The transmitter stayed up. Only the delay was gone, and the monitors, and Sandy's voice, and then, one by one, in an order too deliberate to be failure, the lights went down to the emergency strip. Red. Red. Red.

The service door at the back of the studio — badge-locked, alarmed, opened perhaps twice a month — opened.

Sandy remembered the shoes with absolute fidelity for the rest of her life, and told June so, apologising for them. Soft-soled, dark, nearly silent on the carpet tile. A grey sleeve, a cuff mended with darker thread. An object in one hand she first took for a remote and then for a tool — flat, black, a stub of cable coiled around it in three exact turns — and a visitor's lanyard, the yellow one, clipped square at the chest. She remembered these as she remembered every fader on her own board. What stood between the shoes and the lanyard, where a face should have assembled itself, would not assemble, not then and not in any telling after; her eyes had gone there, and what came back was weather, a pressure, someone attending to the room rather than standing in it. She beat on the glass with her hand and then with the telephone, and the glass, built to keep a studio's sound in, kept hers in perfectly.

Delaney laughed. It was the last entirely professional thing he did. "Well, now," he said, to the room, to the open door, to the thousands, because the microphone was still in front of him, "friends, it appears we have a — "

What the audience heard was the beginning, and only the beginning, and hundreds of them would call the station and then the police to describe it, and no two descriptions agreed except on the shape: the laugh, the start of the sentence, a sound like furniture deciding something, and then — not static, not dead air, but the emergency tape kicking in with a cheerfulness that made several listeners pull their cars over, a recorded Delaney from a brighter year announcing that KMDK would return to regular programming after the following messages. The transmitter cut at twelve-ten and forty seconds. The studio's own aircheck recorder, which answered to nothing but its own clockwork, did not.

He came at Delaney not quickly, which was the first of the cruelties, because Delaney was a big man gone soft and hurry might have left room for luck. He came the way a stagehand comes, attending to the set. The boom arm first. The great broadcast microphone — an RE20, a pound and a half of steel mesh, the instrument Delaney had spoken into for nineteen years — swung on its counterweighted arm with a smoothness that spoke of a hand that had studied its arc, and took him across the jaw as he rose. The sound, on the aircheck tape, is not a crack. June would hear it later and understand it is worse: a *thunk*, intimate, upholstered, the sound Delaney had kept the cart machines for.

The blow put him down across the console, one hand clawing a fader bank up to full as he fell, and that is why the tape holds what it holds: his own falling hand opened every channel in the room. The chair rolls and strikes the tile. And Delaney, whose jaw is broken on the left side — Moss would map it later, the comminuted hinge, the teeth displaced inward — tries, because talking was the reflex under all his other reflexes, to say something into the ruin of his own mouth, and what comes out is wet and shapeless and entirely unlike the voice three counties trusted.

The second use of the microphone was slower. He gathered Delaney up by the collar and the belt — Sandy would remember the *competence* of it, as you lift a man who has fainted, almost courteously — set him back into his own chair, wheeled it in close, belly to the console, and pulled the boom down. The microphone was forced into Delaney's mouth.

Not struck. *Placed*, with pressure, and held, and worked past the broken teeth when the teeth resisted, the steel mesh splitting his cheek and then his palate, so that the gagging that followed was full of blood, and the blood ran down the grille and the cable and onto the show notes in Delaney's own hand — *Appleseed at 11:40, take it big* — soaking through the words in the order he had written them. His tongue, lacerated on the mesh, tried to form itself around the obstruction into something like a consonant. His hands beat at the boom and could not move it, because the boom had been locked at the joint with the flat black tool, wedged as precisely as a doorstop, and the whole weight of the arm had been given to his throat. The microphone came out. The boom stayed. Its yoke rested across his neck with the patience of a hand, and bore down, and down, in the small administrative clicks of the spring.

After that it was the cable. The headphone lead — Delaney's own, coiled, worn where he had wound it around two fingers during ten thousand calls — passed twice around his neck above the yoke and once around the upright of his chair, and drawn, not tight and tighter in the manner of a struggle, but tight *once*, completely, the way a man draws a knot he has already decided on. His heels went on the carpet tile. His hands found the console's edge and held it, as if he were a passenger in weather. The sounds he made then were not words and were not screams. The aircheck tape, which June would stop twice and make Pruitt stop once, holds them at full fidelity because his own hand had opened the room: the long, narrowing whistle of a man breathing through a space being closed by machinery; a wet clicking, as the structures of his throat came out of their arrangement; an attempt — unmistakable, and the worst thing on the tape — to laugh, the professional noise rising out of the wreckage of the instrument out of nineteen years of habit, and failing, and failing again smaller.

His bowels let go. His bladder let go. The body finished its work in the chair of its kingdom, hands still holding the console's edge, its fingers opening one at a time, like a man setting down a thing he had carried a long way.

The sound effects played him out. That was the station, or what was conducting the station: while Sandy watched through the glass with the telephone in her hand like a stone, the cart deck woke by itself and offered, at broadcast volume in a sealed room, the kazoo fanfare. Then the applause cart — a canned crowd, delighted. Then, after a pause Sandy would describe to June as *comic* timing, *his* timing — the crunch. The bite. Wet and enormous.

The ON AIR lamp, which should have died with the transmitter, burned above a dead man in a locked room for the eleven minutes it took the fire crew to come through the booth wall with an axe, because every door held its lock until the power was cut at the street.

June read him at four in the morning, in the red gloom, with the axe marks in the booth wall behind her and the aircheck tape still turning in her ears.

He had been composed. The chair was centred on the console, exactly centred. The headphones — their cable now cut by the fire crew — had been set upon his head straight, both cups even. The microphone had been cleaned: the grille that had been in his mouth and had come out of it full of blood and broken tooth had been wiped carefully, and swung back to the precise position of address, an inch from where his mouth would have been if his mouth had still been a thing a man could address a microphone with. His hands were folded on the console, over the show notes, hiding *Appleseed* and showing *take it big*. His eyes were closed.

On the fader bank, in the trough where Delaney's thumb had worn the console smooth, sat a Honeycrisp apple. Whole, unbitten, polished to a shine that caught the emergency light, its stem trimmed short. It had not rolled, though the trough was shallow and the building had been full of axes and running men. It had been placed the way a period is placed.

"The room wasn't a rehearsal," Pruitt said, from the doorway, in the voice he kept for funerals and for four in the morning. "I know. You've said. This one's not for the audience, either, is it? This one *is* the audience. He put it *on* them."

"He put the beginning on them," June said. "The rest he kept for us."

Moss, when the lights came back, worked the chair's perimeter without touching it. "One pair of hands," he said. "What I have is the absence of a second one's mistakes. The jaw, the mouth, the throat, the cable — it's a sequence, not a struggle between crews. Whoever did this had *time* in here, June. Locked-room time. He used all of it."

Reyes arrived at six with his network already assembled in his face, and June watched it meet the studio and begin to fail to fit. A coordinated group, he said, could explain the systems — the delay, the doors, the transmitter, the phones; infrastructure argued for hands in more than one place. The room let him finish, out of tired courtesy, before Sandy, white to the lips, said from the doorway that the booth door had opened *once*.

"Once," she said. "I was looking at it. I never stopped looking at it. One person came in. And the door never opened again until the axe."

"A cell could still — " Reyes said, and then, to his credit, which June noted as tone, he stopped and looked for a long time at the centred chair, at the folded hands, at the microphone cleaned and set an inch from a ruined mouth, and what he said next was not a theory at all. "That's not a division of labour," he said quietly. "That's handwriting." He did not withdraw the network; she could see its whole architecture standing in him, load-bearing, the work of months. But a crack had gone through it in the shape of a single chair in a locked room, and they both heard it go.

The name arrived at noon, on every front page and chyron at once, minted out of the one fact the public had been allowed to keep: **THE ORCHARD KILLER**.

Hart said it once in the squad room, reading it off a screen. "Orchard. They've given him a *brand*. We had a person doing this. Now we've got a — " He gestured, disgusted past vocabulary.

"It'll be on shirts by Friday," Pruitt said. "Somebody's kid is already drawing the T-shirt. Apple with a bite out of it. Little stem. You watch."

June said nothing for a while. A name was not a label. A name was an address — the thing you put on the envelope when you intended the letter to arrive. Until noon the correspondence she had been reading in folded sheets and squared badges and rooms composed like sentences had been private: addressed to the dead, and then, increasingly, to her. Now the city had written back in headline font and given the author of those letters a name of its own making, and she understood, with the chyron repeating mutely on the wall, that he would keep it.

Not because it flattered him — she had read his rooms too carefully to believe in vanity keeping house there. He would keep it because of what it *was*: a name he had not written, and the question folded inside it — whose, exactly, was a name its bearer had not chosen and its authors had not meant as a gift? They had christened him out of his own signature, out of the one placed, unexplained thing he left shining in every room, and in doing it they had joined the correspondence without knowing there was one.

"He'll keep it," she said, at last, and Pruitt looked at her.

"The name? June, he'll never see a — "

"He'll keep it," she said. "That's the terrible part. We just named him *for* him. He doesn't have to do a thing."

She went back, before she went home, and stood in the doorway of the booth, where the axe marks were and the console was dark. The chair had gone, and the apple had gone into evidence, and the room was only a small carpeted box smelling of hot wiring. But its tone had not emptied with the furniture. Jensen's room had been addressed to whoever found it. Erin's corridor had been addressed, with a narrowing focus that still woke her at night, to her.

This one had been addressed to the thousands who had heard a laugh begin and a sentence not finish, who had pulled their cars over on dark highways with the emergency tape chirping in their cabs. The correspondence had gone public. It had an audience now, and a name the audience had given it, and June read the room's tone as she would have read a letter opened, before it reached her, by the whole city at once.

It was still a letter.

It was just no longer only hers.
