At six-forty Saturday morning Sarah woke before the alarm.

Anne had answered after midnight. *After the appointment is fine. Call when you can.* Sarah read the message twice, then crossed out eight-thirty on the scrap beside her phone. She left Anne’s name.

Ellen had not answered the photographs of the notice.

David lay facing the wall. Sarah put a hand between his shoulder blades, feeling the warmth through his shirt.

“Are we late?” he asked without turning.

“No.”

He reached back for her hand.

For twenty minutes they were not late for anything.

At seven they got up.

David made toast while Sarah read the notice again. The assessment office was twenty minutes away by cab. She booked one for seven-thirty, using her own phone. The confirmation asked for a passenger name. She entered hers.

“Do you want me there?” David asked.

“Yes.”

“Inside?”

“If they let you. If I still want you.”

He put her toast beside the paper.

“That seems fair.”

She ate standing up, then sat because she wanted to. The distinction was becoming tiring. She wondered whether he made it every time he chose a chair.

At seven-twelve Ellen called.

“I’ve asked Saye to suspend the assessment,” she said.

“Has she?”

“No.”

“Can you?”

“My appeal jurisdiction concerns the holds. She issued the assessment under separate authority.”

“So your message last night doesn’t protect me.”

“It limits what can be done under my preservation request. It does not cancel her notice.”

Sarah pushed the plate away.

“What happens if I don’t go?”

“Midwest must account for its failure to make you available.”

“And what happens to me?”

“I don’t know what they would seek next.”

David sat opposite, listening with Sarah’s permission.

“Can you be there?” she asked.

“I can join by connection. Ask them to enter my objection before any examination.”

“What is your objection?”

“That the proposed assessment may collect material whose preservation scope is still disputed.”

“May?”

“I haven’t seen their procedure.”

Sarah asked Ellen to send the objection in writing. It arrived before the call ended.

The cab came at seven-thirty-two.

On the way Sarah watched shops opening. A man carried bread trays through a door held by his foot. Two women waited outside a pharmacy, neither speaking. The morning belonged to people who did not know she had an appointment.

David’s knee touched hers when the cab turned. She left it there.

At seven-fifty-six they reached a low building with a flag hanging wet against its pole.

Reception had a sign for deliveries and another for visitors. Sarah chose visitors.

The man behind the desk asked whom she was seeing.

“H. Saye.”

“Name?”

“Sarah Miller.”

He found the appointment, glanced at David and asked whether he was the custodian’s representative.

“No,” Sarah said. “He’s with me.”

“I need a category.”

“Visitor,” David said.

The man gave him a visitor slip.

He gave Sarah a different sheet.

*System presented by custodian.*

The presenting party field already read *Midwest Information Services*. There was no employee name.

Sarah set it down.

“I came in a cab.”

“That’s not the transport field.”

“Where is the person who presented me?”

“I only handle arrival.”

“Then record arrival.”

He called someone. They waited beside a rack of leaflets about service charges.

At eight-oh-four a woman came through the inner door.

“I’m Ruth Palmer, assessment technician.”

This was a different Ruth from the woman at the laboratory bench: a stranger to both Sarah and David.

“Can you change this?” Sarah asked.

Ruth read the sheet.

“I can add a note.”

“Will it change what the signature means?”

“No.”

“Then I won’t sign it.”

Ruth took the sheet rather than arguing.

“You can attend without acknowledging presentation. I’ll record refusal of the form.”

“Refusal of its statement,” Sarah said.

Ruth wrote that.

The assessment room contained three chairs, a desk and a reclining examination seat. Sarah took a chair.

David waited at the door until she nodded.

Ruth placed a sealed cable on the desk.

“What is that for?” Sarah asked.

“Diagnostic access.”

“What does it read?”

“The interface reports supported channels before we select a procedure.”

“So you connect before deciding what you’re allowed to read.”

“We enumerate first.”

“Does enumeration change anything?”

“It creates a session record.”

“Where?”

“Here, and in the device’s access history.”

Sarah laid Ellen’s objection on the desk.

“I want her connected.”

Ruth read it, then left the cable sealed.

At eight-eleven Ellen joined on the wall screen. Saye arrived in person two minutes later, carrying a folder without a terminal.

She greeted Ellen first.

Sarah waited until she finished.

“I have questions about the procedure,” Sarah said.

“That’s why you’re here.”

“No. I’m here because you required Midwest to make me available.”

Saye placed the folder on the desk.

“And you came.”

“I haven’t agreed to the cable.”

“No one has connected it.”

“Then tell me what it does.”

Ruth began explaining again. This time Sarah asked her to write each proposed step beside the record it would create.

Enumeration. Channel selection. Baseline capture.

At the third step Ellen spoke.

“What constitutes baseline?”

Ruth looked at Saye.

“Internal operational state sufficient to establish the listed system’s condition,” Saye said.

“State or structure?” Ellen asked.

“Whatever is necessary to distinguish subsequent alteration.”

Sarah looked from one woman to the other.

“That is the definition you told me not to use.”

Ellen said, “Yes.”

Saye opened her folder.

“Preservation without a baseline is an assertion by the custodian. We need an independent basis.”

“Then assess the assertion,” Sarah said.

“That requires assessing the system.”

“Me.”

Saye did not correct her.

Ruth set down her pen.

David’s visitor slip lay on his knee. Sarah could see his thumb smoothing its folded edge.

“What happens to the captured baseline?” she asked.

“It is retained as evidence,” Saye said.

“Who can use it?”

“Authorized examiners.”

“For what?”

“The proceeding.”

“Which proceeding?”

Saye closed the folder halfway.

“The condition determination. That is the determination within this assessment, under my authority—not a new name for Ellen’s appeal.”

“Not Monday’s appeal.”

“Not exclusively.”

Sarah felt David stop moving beside her.

Ellen asked for the answer to be entered verbatim.

Ruth wrote it.

At eight-twenty-one Sarah stood.

“I’m available,” she said. “I’m answering questions. I do not agree to capture.”

Saye looked at the sealed cable.

“The notice is directed to the custodian.”

“I’m the one you’d connect.”

“We can require the custodian’s cooperation.”

“Then require it.”

For a moment the room went quiet enough for Sarah to hear the wall screen’s cooling fan.

Ruth turned the procedure sheet so everyone could see it.

“Without the subject’s cooperation,” she said, “I need instructions for how this is to be performed.”

Saye looked at her.

“I’m asking for the procedure,” Ruth said.

Ellen remained on the screen. David remained in his chair. Neither spoke for Sarah.

At eight-twenty-three Saye wrote a new instruction on the sheet.

*Refer for supervised access authorization.*

Sarah read it upside down.

The cable stayed in its wrapper.

“Am I free to leave?” she asked.

Saye said, “This appointment is concluded.”

Sarah waited.

“Yes,” Saye said.

Outside the building, at eight-twenty-eight, Sarah called Anne.

“I can talk now,” she said.

David stepped away far enough not to hear.
