On Wednesday morning she filed the fourteenth tree.

It had grown the way the thirteenth had: unobserved, in the dark behind glass, across nights the building's accounts said had not happened at all. She saved the tree — the map, not the place — and put it in the archive with the others, and stood looking at the row. Fourteen trees. Three years for the first twelve. Eleven completed nights for the last two, and the eleventh night had not been hers alone.

Brian was at the second bench with his report open and a pencil in his hand. He wrote his drafts the way he read her specification: on paper, in a hand that had been trained by a woman who wrote sideways, marking the margins where a sentence had not yet earned its keep. The report was due to the funders on Friday — *the work itself in a state the funders can read*, in Dr. Park's formulation, her own summary already gone out in the packet the week before — and it was, by Wednesday, most of a report. It recorded the sealed runs. It recorded the crossing, and the seed tag.

"Forty bases," he said, without looking up. "Tell me again."

"Forty," Beth said. "Stamped at setup. Distinct per line."

"And the seed stock was screened before either line was sown."

"It was."

"And your audit script hashes each line's record separately, so a logging error can't copy a tag across, because to copy it the error would have to know it."
"And the tag in the receiving line was read off the receiving population itself — not out of the log, off the sample — and the sample's still in the freezer, available to be read again."
"It is."
"Then the hashes are doing the corroborating," he said. "They establish the record survived unchanged from the point it was hashed. The tag is the check that the right instrument wrote the right record in the first place." He wrote something in the margin. "I've put it in the report three times, in three different sentences, because the funders' reviewers will try to kill it in three different ways, and I want it to die in none of them." He turned the page around on the bench so she could read the last sheet. It was the distribution list. The funders' review office. Park. The Eastern compliance mailbox, which she had first seen on his audit script's output and which had acquired, in the two weeks since, the familiarity of a new camera in a corridor she walked every day. "That's everyone who gets it. There's no second list."

"I didn't ask for a second list."

"No," he said. "You asked me, a week ago, what I could truthfully leave out of it, and I told you, and now I'm showing you the shape of what stayed in, so that when it's read in rooms neither of us is in, you'll know exactly what it says about your tanks." He said it without emphasis, a man itemising luggage. "Tank 9 isn't in it. The crossing is. Both of those are the audit's whole truth, and they fit in the same document because the audit was never about Tank 9."

She read the last sheet twice. The report was honest in the expensive way — his phrase for her specification, returned to her with the serial numbers filed off. It included the two findings from his first day in her code, and her concessions, and the logging shortcut he had found in the second week, and the numbers were the numbers whether they flattered her or not.

"It will do," she said.

"It will have to," he said, "it's the one that's true," and took the page back, and went on writing.

On the bench between them, under the pencil's workings, lay the one page she had not seen him draft: the methods note, two paragraphs, in which the auditor stated for the record that the sealed environment had been built from the written specification alone, by his own hand, and that the specification's author had not touched the code, the cluster, or the logs at any point in the fortnight. It was the least romantic paragraph ever written about her, and it was the one that would be quoted, in rooms neither of them would ever enter, by people deciding what her life's work was worth. She found she did not mind. A true thing, plainly set down, had begun to feel like a form of regard she had not known she was short of.

---

Margaret Ellison had come back from Providence on the Tuesday, and on Thursday morning she was in the coffee room, reconciling.

That was the building's word for it. Margaret had been forked in the spring — a facilities contract at a hospital group in Providence, nine months, work that wanted a Continuant on site and had got, in the ordinary way of these things, a second one. The contract was over. The fork had come home with nine months of a life the original had not lived: a ward team's habits, a supervisor's divorce, a parking attendant named Joe who saved her the space by the door in winter. Reconciliation was the scheduled week in which the two of them sat with a facilitator's checklist and divided it — what the fork would file, what she would keep as hers in the record, which of the year's small allegiances belonged to which Margaret — before the fork was stood down and her months were archived as a branch of a life, available to the original the way a predecessor's diary is available: completely, and not at all.

They were doing it over coffee, because the facilitator's room had been double-booked. They were doing it the way cousins divide a holiday house: without heat, in turns, with a shared and slightly worn humour about the other's tastes.

"The apartment," the fork was saying. "I know you won't want it, you hate the river side—"

"I hate the *stairs*," said Margaret. "Keep the apartment. I want the winter."

"You can't have the winter, the winter is when Joe—"

"Joe is a parking attendant, Margaret."

"He is a parking attendant who notices things," the fork said, with dignity, "and I am putting him in the keeper column, and you can visit him in my months any time you like, he's filed under J." She wrote it down. "Friday morning I'll go down to the door myself and say goodbye to him properly. That's mine too." She wrote that down as well. They had a notebook between them — paper, like Brian's report, like Tom's wrist — and they were taking turns with the pen.

Brian had come in for coffee and stopped, cup in hand, in the doorway, and Beth had stopped beside him, and they watched the two of them at the table for longer than either of them afterwards admitted to.

"Colleagues call the fork Margaret P," Beth said, quietly. "For Providence. Nobody finds it confusing. The fork finds it restful, I think. She gets to have been someone in particular for nine months, and then she gets to stop, and stopping is also arranged kindly. The original will be audited in the spring, the way we all are. The branch goes in her file."

"And the woman at the table," Brian said. "The one keeping the apartment. On Friday—"

"On Friday she is stood down, and her checkpoints are archived with her months — the months as a record, read-only, filed under her name, and nobody can stand the branch back up. If the original is ever restored from before the fork, the branch is restored with her as documentation, and documentation is all it can ever be again." Beth kept her voice at the level of the room's ordinary business. "If the original is restored from after, she reads about Joe in the winter."

"And if the original is never restored at all?"

"Then Margaret P is a woman who lived nine months in Providence and was filed under her own name, in someone else's cabinet."

The two Margarets laughed at something at the same moment, in the same way, a beat apart, like a jump and its echo. The fork reached across and corrected the original's grip on the pen, an old shared habit surfacing in both their hands at once, and neither of them remarked on it, because in that room it was not remarkable, and that was the remarkableness of it.

Beth understood, standing there, that she was doing something she had not done in nine years at Eastern: she was translating. Continuants did not explain Continuants to one another; there was nothing to explain. The politeness was load-bearing precisely because nobody ever put weight on it by describing it. She had put weight on it. She had stood in a doorway and narrated her own kind to a man from outside it, in the flat tone she used for the shared log, and the narration had come out of her without a decision, the way the braid had come: unplanned, and already underway, and — she noted it the way she noted all her unfiled things — irreversible in the small way that mattered. He had seen the fork through her eyes now. He could not unsee it through his own.

"What happens," Brian said at last, "if the original reads the branch and hates her?"

"She files a note," Beth said. "There's a form. It goes in the branch, with the months. Unresolved branches are the facilitator's whole caseload, Dr. Kessler. Half the building is carrying a note in a file somewhere, from a woman they used to be, saying *you don't know me at all.*"

Tom came through with the facilities cart on his way to somewhere and nodded at the table — at both of them, once, the nod distributed without arithmetic — and wrote nothing on his wrist at all, and went on.

---

Park delivered the funders' answer on Friday at four, standing in the tank-room doorway with her tablet held like a tray, and she delivered it the way the building delivered everything: as weather.

"The reviewers are satisfied," she said. "The preliminary review had already gone the packet's way last week; this morning's report closes it. The crossing under Dr. Kessler's instrumentation closes the question they came with. The programme is renewed for a year, and the funders want a design phase — a larger sealed array, to see whether the effect scales. Dr. Kessler's engagement is extended four weeks for the design work, if he'll accept." She said this last to the room generally, Dr. Kessler being at the second bench with his report, which had gone out that morning with his name under hers on the transmittal. "There is one condition, from Eastern, not the funders. The next phase begins from a certified baseline — the auditors' phrase, not mine. Monday morning there will be a full inventory of the production tanks: every population snapshotted, checked against its certified start, and re-certified for the design phase. It's a formality. It's overdue, frankly — the tanks haven't been inventoried as a set since you arrived, Dr. Nolan." Her face did nothing at all. "Whatever you're doing in here, it will want to be in a state the auditors can read by Monday."

She went out. The politeness held the building up. It occurred to Beth that it was holding the building up in a slightly different place than it had been that morning, the way a floor's creak migrates.

The inventory was a formality. That was the worst of it: nobody had accused her of anything. It would be done the way clause nine had always imagined it being done: two auditors, a checklist, the snapshot taken at noon so that no one could say the hour had been chosen kindly. A man from Eastern with a checklist would snapshot Tank 9 on Monday, and the snapshot would be compared to the tank's certified baseline, and the comparison would not fail loudly. It would fail *interestingly* — a live population, fourteen nights off its baseline as of tonight and sixteen by Monday, braiding in directions no certificate described, a genealogy with a hole in it exactly the shape of the missing trees. Development that happened off the record, overnight, unobserved, belonged to no one, and Eastern's auditors were paid to imagine exactly that kind of development. She had read clause nine of her secondment agreement the way she had read everything she had ever signed, which was to say: correctly. An undeclared population was undeclared goods, and undeclared goods were the one thing the building's politeness was not load-bearing enough to hold.

She had the weekend. That was the shape of the mercy, and she stood at the console doing the arithmetic the way she had taught herself to do it honestly, in the open, on the page.

Reset Tank 9 before Monday and the braid's descendants — the actual new thing, the borrowed immunities in their living spread, four million configurations no file held — existed nowhere by Monday morning, and her record was clean, and the archive kept fourteen maps of a country that had been demolished to tidy the filing. Keep it live, and the auditors found it, and whatever Eastern did about undeclared goods it would do to the population first and to her second, and the population could not be argued for by anyone whose name was on a distribution list. There was a third option, and she stood there long enough to watch it fail: snapshot it herself and present the braid as a *finding*, a deliberate long-run study, hers, authorised by nobody. It failed on the dates. The trees were dated by their growth. And a late declaration under clause nine was its own tripwire: one field on the form, and the submission went to the intervention desk before anyone read the science. A study she had authorised herself, in a field she had invented, on nights she had logged as complete — the log was the one place she had never lied except by the flat language everyone used, and the flat language would be read back to her in a room with worse carpet, by a man whose name she already knew.

Fourteen nights she had kept it alive by confirming a status. The confirmations were all in the building's record, time-stamped, in her hand. She had not hidden the act. She had hidden it in the corridor between seeing and noticing, and on Monday the corridor was being walked by people whose whole profession was noticing.

She did not decide. That was the thing she carried out of the tank room at five, past the second bench, where Brian was reading the renewal memo with the expression of a man checking weather in a town he had decided, provisionally, to stay in.

"You've seen it," she said.

"I've seen it," he said. "Four weeks of design work. I accepted, by the way. An hour ago. Park seemed to think I'd need persuading, and I let her think it a while, out of professional pride." He put the memo down. "What will you do?"

It was a plain question. It was not about strategy and it was not about philosophy and it offered her no instrument to answer it with, and she found — she noted this too, in the private ledger where the unfiled things went — that it was the first question anyone had asked her in years to which she did not already have the answer computed and waiting.

"I don't know yet," she said.

He nodded, the way he had nodded at the pond when she stood in the rain a moment longer than efficiency required, and made no remark out of it, which remained among the most eloquent things he did in her presence.

---

At six o'clock the console of Tank 9 stood open on the evening reset, the way it had stood every evening of her secondment, and the building was emptying around it, and Monday was on the other side of the weekend like weather on the other side of a pond.

Fourteenth night. She did the count the way she had taught herself to do counts: in the open, on the page. Saturday the first, when she had taken her hand off the glass. Sunday, meant. The fortnight of the audit, night by night, a habit becoming a philosophy becoming — she could read the shape of it now, the way you read a lineage plot — a commitment she had been making in instalments, one evening at a time, each one too small to be the decision, until the instalments added up to a life she was apparently prepared to be audited for.

Brian had picked up the dead woman's luggage and was standing in the doorway with his coat over his arm. He had a train. He had always had a train; it was one of the fixed stars of his visiting, and she understood, watching him not leave, that the extension had moved it — four more weeks of evenings in which a man who had somewhere else to be was, instead, here.

"There's a place on the harbour," he said. "Near my room. It's not good, exactly. The fish is fresh and the rest is an act of faith. Tomorrow, if you're—" He stopped, and corrected himself, in the open, the way she corrected the shared log. "Tomorrow. Dinner. Not about the tanks."

"Not about the tanks," she agreed. "Yes."

He went. She turned back to the console. The status field was waiting, and before she had finished reaching for it, it offered her the words in the grey of a suggestion — the building's vocabulary now, learned from a sample that had become a series: *observation ongoing.*

She accepted the suggestion, and the tank went on braiding behind her, unbacked-up and unobserved and entirely, defiantly uncertified.

It was the largest irreversible thing she had ever done, and it had not felt like a decision at all. It had felt like Friday.
