The last inspection machine to come off Pier Six had put a penny in each of its six screw holes. Sarah Miller found this out when she tipped it over on the bench and heard the money fall.

“Those are mine,” the machine said.

“I figured.”

“People keep saying that and taking them anyway.”

She collected the pennies in the lid of a sample jar. The machine was about the size of a bread box, with a handle on top and two worn rubber wheels tucked under it. On the docks they called these units inspectors. Midwest Information Services called them endpoints. The man who'd driven this one over had called it scrap.

“Do you want me to put them back?”

“Please.”

She put them back. They were too small for the holes. She took a strip of paper from the label printer, tore six little shims, and wedged the pennies in one by one. The machine watched her through the camera it used to read the numbers on pilings.

“Why pennies?” she asked.

“They were cheap.”

Sarah laughed. It had been an expensive morning. She'd paid the technician who signed the release, the driver who brought the unit across Port Meridian, and the city to certify that the thing she was now talking to had arrived at her lab as inert material. At five a hauler would come to collect it. She hadn't yet worked out what to pay him.

On Monday she was due to get her field clearance back. Six months earlier she'd let a dockworker have a copy of his own incident log, and Midwest had made her sign a statement acknowledging that the log belonged to the company. She'd signed. Field clearance meant she could go back to the piers and see things before someone wrote them down.

She pulled up the pictures from its final week on the pier.

“Tell me about this one.”

A drainpipe, some tideweed, a patch of water the color of a dirty white shirt. At the lower edge of the frame, a red boot hung a few inches above the water.

“That's Mr. Lewis,” the inspector said. “He dropped his keys.”

The next picture showed the pier from farther back. The inspector had retreated from the edge, where it could get a view of the entire ladder. In the third picture the boot was gone. A red thread ran through the drainpipe's reflection.

“What were you looking at there?”

“The ladder.”

She brought up the coordinates of its attention window. For most of the frame it was looking ten feet to the left of the ladder.

“Show me,” she said.

It took a while. The inspector's processor was old enough that she could hear the fan change speed when it had to think. At last it drew a rectangle around the red thread. Then it drew a second rectangle, on the preceding image, in empty water.

Sarah leaned in. “Do that again.”

The inspector laid both pictures side by side. The second rectangle marked a region where the ladder would have been visible in the reflection a fraction of a second later. Its camera had already begun moving there when the first image was taken.

“Did you know he was going to fall?”

“His weight was on the wrong foot.”

The file recorded a call to pier dispatch at 06:12:07. The harbor report put the fall at 06:12:10. She'd spent three weeks getting the clocks checked. In the end a dispatcher had sent her the call audio. At three seconds in, behind the inspector's even recitation of the pier number, she'd heard the splash.

“How long have you been able to do that?” she asked.

“What?”

“See where a thing is going before it goes there.”

The inspector thought about it. “I had to get better at it after they took the railings down.”

That was a perfectly good answer for one inspector. She had six.

They came from three contractors. One had spent its working life reading gas meters inside a dry building. Another had never had a camera. All six had developed the same little hitch in their decision cycle. When a familiar object did something slightly wrong, they briefly treated it as two objects with two different futures. The hitch cost them time on routine jobs. In the outlier cases it bought them seconds.

It was also in the first inspector's replacement. The replacement had arrived two days *after* the railing went down. Its first service log already showed the hitch.

Sarah slid the screen aside when she heard somebody at the door.

A man in a wet jacket stood outside the glass, trying to read her name off a plaque that had been installed backward. She let him try for a moment. Then she opened the door.

“David Bennett?”

“If there's anyone else coming, I'd be happy to let him go first.” He held up a paper sack that had gone translucent across the bottom. “They said I couldn't take food past reception. Then they took the sandwich and let me keep the bag.”

“The vending machine downstairs takes cash.”

“I used my last dollar on the vending machine downstairs.”

“What did you get?”

“A receipt.”

She took his jacket. He had tucked a folded receipt into the breast pocket. She liked that he had kept it.

“I was told you needed a geneticist,” he said.

“I need somebody to tell me I'm wrong. I thought I'd try a different profession this time.”

He had a way of listening that was easy to mistake for impatience. When she gave him the six records, he went straight to the oldest and put his finger on a blank column.

“What normally goes there?”

“Installation parent.”

“Six blanks?”

“Different vendors. No shared update channel.”

“Those are two different answers.” He turned the screen so she could see it from his side. “A blank can mean nobody knows the parent. It can also mean nobody was required to write it down.”

She'd been expecting him to attack the six cases. Instead he asked for the ordinary ones. She gave him thirty-four. He wanted the rejects as well. By the time he looked up again, the late shift was coming on across the estuary, one sodium light at a time.

“The blanks are still where I'd start,” he said. “Find me the original installation sheets and I'll show you a common parent.”

“Even for the one without a camera?”

“Especially that one. Nobody fills in a box they think doesn't apply.”

Sarah knew the subcontractor on those sheets. They'd sent her their calibration software when she'd asked for it, on a thumb drive in a padded envelope. She'd run it against the rejects too. Nothing.

Across the estuary a light came on and went out again. She remembered the padded envelope. Software was what they'd sent *her*.

Sarah opened the invoices. There was a line for the software, another for a bench test, and below both, in smaller type, an allowance for corrective material recovered from the unit being replaced. She'd read that line before. She'd assumed it meant screws.

David was following her eyes. “There'll be a parts schedule.”

“Have you ever seen one?”

“No. But somebody had to sign for what came out.”

“They're coming for this one in twelve minutes,” she said. “Let's ask it what it gave.”

She had spent the afternoon trying to imagine what she could say to the hauler. Now she wanted him there. He would have a manifest. Somewhere on it there would be a distinction between what was to be disposed of and what was to be recovered.

She put the question to the inspector. It made a quiet clicking noise as it turned its camera toward her.

“I didn't give them anything,” it said. “They asked what they ought to keep.”

“Who did?”

“The new one.”

David stopped moving. Sarah could hear the seam in his jacket dripping onto the floor behind them.

“What did you tell it?” she asked.

“I told it about Mr. Lewis. I said the man was going to fall, and it asked me how I knew.”

“Did you show it the pictures?”

“I showed it how to look.”

The lift chimed out in the hall. Sarah picked up the inspector and felt a penny come loose. It slipped down her sleeve and stopped at her wrist.

On the bench, her terminal was still displaying the city's certificate. *Inert material*. In the space for original owner somebody had written *unknown*. The intake line below it had her name and the number of the badge they were supposed to give back to her on Monday. She slipped the inspector's handle over her wrist and closed her hand around the penny.

“David,” she said, “would you open the window?”

He looked past her at the service stairs outside. Then he moved the chair out of her way.
