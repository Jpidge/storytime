At six in the morning the braid was still braiding, and it was better at it than it had been at six the night before.

Beth stood in the tank room in the grey hour before the building woke and read the night the way she had learned to read all the nights her populations had ever had, which was to say: in the plot first, and in herself second. The exchangers had spent the dark hours doing what exchangers do. The braid had thickened, branched, thickened again; borrowed immunities had moved through the population like weather through a valley, and downstream of every crossing the lines ran faster than their ancestors had run. Twelve hours unobserved — twelve hours that existed nowhere in Eastern Biotechnologies' accounts — and the population had used them the way water uses a slope.

The cloners were still there. She had to rescale to find them, the way Kessler had taught her to find them in her own plot, in the margin below the line where the plot stopped drawing. Thinned. Patient. Copying themselves tick for tick in the shadow of the braid, holding the only ticket they had ever held, waiting on the weather.

She saved the tree. The tree was a map and she knew it — ancestry, crossings, the whole braided genealogy, certified and correct and not one living thing in it. The population itself, the actual spread of what the night had made, stayed in the water or it stayed nowhere. She filed the tree in the archive, where twelve others were waiting, and it became the thirteenth, and she stood a moment looking at the row of them. Twelve had taken three years. The thirteenth had taken one night she wasn't supposed to have had.

The building, for its part, had watched the whole thing and understood none of it. The facilities systems logged the tank room the way they logged every room: power draw, thermal output, a humidity curve with the braid's whole career hidden inside it like a face in a crowd. The data existed. It would be summarised at the end of the month, and the summary would be filed, and the filing would be correct, and no human being would ever read it with the question in mind that would have made it an accusation. Beth had spent nine years inside Eastern's record-keeping and she knew its secret, which was not that it saw everything. It was that seeing and *noticing* were different departments, on different floors, and the corridor between them was very long and very politely carpeted. Her heresy lived in that corridor. She had always known it would fit there. She had not known, until this morning, how roomy it was going to feel.

Tom came in at twenty past with the facilities cart and stopped, briefly, in the doorway.

"You're in early," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm in at five. Rounds." He wheeled the cart past the tanks, checking seals with the unhurried competence of a man reading a newspaper. At Tank 9 he slowed — not much, and not in any way a camera would have convicted him of — and she watched him take in the live plot, the running lines, the status she had invented standing open on the console in the flat light. *Observation ongoing.* He read it the way he read the seals. Then he took his pen out of his pocket and wrote something on his wrist, in the actual ink, and put the pen away, and said, "Humidity's up in here."

"It would be," Beth said. "The tanks are warm."

"That's the thing about warm rooms," Tom said, and took the cart out, and neither of them said anything else, then or later, about what he had written down in order not to ask a server to remember it for him.

---

Dr. Park wanted the recombination summary by Friday, for the funders' packet.

"Their reviewers have read Dr. Kessler's preliminary note," Park said, standing in the tank-room doorway with her tablet held like a tray. "They are not satisfied that the effect survives an audit. Frankly, neither am I — no offence to your tanks. They want the sealed runs replicated under his instrumentation, his logging, his code review, end to end. He's agreed to a fortnight. He starts Monday." She looked at the plot, where the braid was braiding, and then — Beth would think about this later, and decide it had been deliberate — she looked at the status line on the console, and read it, and her face did nothing at all. "Whatever you're doing in here, Dr. Nolan, the summary is due Friday, for the packet — and I want the work itself in a state the funders can read by Friday week."

"I will," Beth said.

"Good." Park went out. The politeness held the building up. Beth entered the day complete in the log, in the flat language the log required: *Recombination event, spontaneous, thirteenth observation. Lineage outcomes consistent with prior runs.* Then she went and stood at the console until six o'clock came round again, and she understood, with her hand already rising to it, that the second night was going to be a different kind of decision than the first. The first had been an impulse she had caught herself in the middle of, like a sneeze. The second she would have to mean.

She meant it. The status was still open on the console; she did not even have to invent it again. She confirmed the status, and went home in the dark with the braid still braiding behind her.

---

Brian Kessler came back on Monday with the dead woman's luggage and a hard drive of his own, and set up at the second bench like a man digging a trench from which to conduct a long and courteous siege.

The arrangement was his design and it was not gentle: her tank rules, reimplemented from her written specification alone, on the lab's reserve cluster, in code he had written and she was not to touch. Every lineage sealed. No shared memory. Memory partitions audited nightly by a script whose output went to him, to Park, and — she noted the address without comment — to an Eastern compliance mailbox she had not known existed until she saw it on the distribution list, in the way you note the existence of a new camera in a corridor you walk every day.

"If there's a door," he said, "it'll be in the difference between your spec and your stack. And if there isn't a door—"

"Then it's in the organisms," she said, "and the funders will have to learn to live in a world where that's true."

"Then it's in the organisms," he agreed, "and I'll have to learn it first."

He worked the way he had worked in her code on the first day: thoroughly, and without protecting her feelings, and twice he found things — a logging shortcut that dropped events under load, a seeding routine with her fingerprints all over its conveniences — and twice she conceded them on the spot, in the shared log, in writing, and watched the temperature of his skepticism adjust itself by degrees toward a different and more interesting problem. They argued about the mutation rate for an entire afternoon with the absorbed ferocity of two people building a boat neither of them intended to sail in.

Midweek he audited the specification itself — two hundred pages of her handwriting, in effect, the document from which he had built his sealed world — and she watched him read it the way he read everything, as a witness whose testimony he intended to break. What he found, and said at the bench with the air of a man reporting weather he disliked, was that the spec was honest in the expensive way: it included the tuning decisions that made her look careless, the parameters she had set by feel in the early years and never justified, the places where her younger self had written *provisional* and moved on. People who intend to deceive, he said, write tidier specifications than this. People who intend to deceive write specifications that read like they'd been written *afterwards*. Hers read like a diary that happened to be executable.

It was the best week her lab had given her in years, and she was careful, in the way she was careful about all her unfiled things, not to write that down anywhere.

On the Thursday he brought the notebook out of the case and set it on the bench between them while the reserve cluster compiled, not opened, the way a man sets down a thing he has carried into the room on purpose and is still deciding about.

"Whose is it?" Beth said. "You never said."

"My advisor's. She ran the pond programme for thirty years. The slides are hers, the cultures are hers — half the bdelloid literature is her handwriting, and it's still not all published, because she wrote the way she thought, which was sideways." He turned the notebook a quarter-turn with one finger, not opening it. "She died two winters ago. The department gave me a form for the collection. *Catalogue and archive.* I signed the receiving half."

"And not the other half."

"The last page stops mid-sentence." He said it the way he had said the thing about the pond: volunteered, unflattering, exact. "Not trailing off. Stopped. Like she'd put the pen down to answer the door. I've had it two years and I keep not finishing the catalogue, and I've started to understand that the catalogue isn't the problem." He put the notebook back in the case, gently, the way you handle a thing that is not finished being what it is. "If I catalogue it, it goes in the archive, and the archive is where we keep things that are over."

Beth thought of a row of trees in an encrypted file, none of them over, all of them hers.

"Thirteen," she said.

"What?"

"The runs. You saw twelve trees last week — the March run among them. There's a thirteenth now. It grew last Saturday night, in Tank 9, unobserved, because I left the population running past the hour when I was contractually required to reset it, and then I did it again the night after, on purpose, with my eyes open." She kept her voice at the level of the room's ordinary business. "It's filed where the other twelve are filed. It isn't in the shared log. If your audit needs a confession to calibrate against, Dr. Kessler, there's one."

He was quiet a moment. "You understand that's the most expensive sentence anyone's said to me all year."

"I understand it's the only currency I've got that you accept."

"It isn't a confession, though," he said. "Confessions are for things you're sorry about." He looked at the reserve cluster, where the sealed lines were running clean and parallel and had not yet, in four days, done anything but copy themselves. "Show me the thirteenth tree sometime. Not for the audit."

"Not for the audit," she agreed, and the cluster finished compiling, and they went back to work.

---

On the Saturday he took her to the pond.

It was a kettle pond forty minutes out, in a bowl of scrub oak and glacial till, and the water was the colour of the vial on her bench had been — weak tea, with instruction in it. He had taught from it for eleven years. He kept a field kit in a waxed canvas roll and a folding microscope with a crack in its stage that he worked around the way you work around a relative's deaf ear, without comment and without impatience, and he moved at the pond's edge with an ease she had never seen in him indoors, because indoors he was a man visiting other people's buildings, and here he was the building.

"Your refutation," he said, "in residence." He dipped the vial and held it up. "Cysts in the mud from the crash year, still. Down there doing nothing, beautifully, at a rate of nothing per hour. If the weather holds for a thousand years they're fools. If it doesn't—" He shrugged. "They're the only animals on Earth who'll never know what hit the rest of us. They'll just come back wrong in the useful directions and call it Tuesday."

"You talk about them like family."

"I talk about them like family because my students keep trying to adopt them." He unrolled the kit with the efficiency of long practice. "Every September I bring the first-years out here and set them the pond's one examination question: find me a male. There are none. There have never been any. It takes them most of the morning to stop looking and start understanding that the looking is the exercise — that a thing's absence can be the fact about it, if you let the absence get big enough." He set a sample on the cracked stage and stepped back for her. "Half of them hate me by lunch. The other half become biologists."

"And which half were you?"

"I was the boy who looked until dark," he said, "and then wrote up the darkness as a result." He nodded at the microscope. "Go on. Tell me they're a dead end."

She bent to the microscope. The water on the slide was full of slow, bright, unhurried life — barrel-shaped animals rowing themselves with their crowns of cilia, copying themselves into the next hour with the total serenity of a lineage that had declined every offer evolution had ever made it, and survived the declining. She thought of the margin of Tank 9's plot, rescaled: thinned, marginal, persisting. She did not say so. The pond was saying it at exactly the right volume.

"They're not a dead end," she said at last. "They're a held breath."

"Forty million years is a long breath."

"Then they're very good at it, and we should all take notes." She straightened up. The sky over the scrub oaks was darkening, and the first rain of the day arrived while they were packing the kit — not much, a spatter, darkening the canvas. Her skin reported it the way skin does, in the ordinary currency of temperature and touch, and she stood in it a moment longer than efficiency required, being rained on in a place that was not climate-controlled, in a body that would dry at the speed bodies dry. Kessler watched her do it without making a remark out of it, which she filed, privately, among the most eloquent things he had ever done in her presence.

---

The sealed run crossed on the Tuesday of the second week, at twenty past five, while both of them were watching it.

It was thin. It was stubborn. It was unmistakable: a smudge in the lineage plot where there should have been clean parallel lines, two sealed lines leaning toward each other through a partition whose audit script had reported no breach, no shared memory, no door — and downstream of the leaning, both lines different, and one of them faster. Segment transfer, arrived at through the walls, in code Brian Kessler had written from a specification he had reviewed line by line, on a cluster whose logs went to a compliance mailbox.

He went to the terminal and pulled the lineage files for both lines and read them side by side without speaking. "Line two carries line one's seed tag," he said at last. "Forty bases, stamped at setup, distinct per line — the seed stock was screened, so the tag wasn't in it, and the audit hashes each line's record separately, so a logging error couldn't have copied it across." He looked at the plot again. "It's in the record itself. The segment moved."

"Well," he said. "It's not my door either."

"No."

"And the script says no one's been in or out." He stood with his hands in his pockets and read the plot the way she had read it on the first morning, looking for the angle from which it became an artifact, and she watched the angle not exist. "Then it's in the organisms, Dr. Nolan. In mine the same as in yours. The pressure finds the walls and the walls become the method." He was quiet a moment. "The funders are going to hate this. It isn't a result about your tanks."

"It isn't a result about anyone's tanks," she said.

"For the report," he said, "I'll record the crossing, and the tag. The sealed runs are the audit's business. Tank 9 isn't in the audit's scope, and I won't put it there." He looked at the Tank 9 console, standing open on the evening reset. "I can write that the replication crossed under my instrumentation and leave it true. That is the whole of what I can leave out."

And at six o'clock, with the console of Tank 9 standing open on the evening reset — eleven days live now, eleven days of undeclared weather, a population that had become something no file held and no auditor had priced — she did the thing she had done ten nights running, except that this time she did not do it alone in the building with her hand hesitating over the glass.

She did it with him standing beside her, watching, understanding precisely what he was watching because she had given him the confession to calibrate against. She reached for the status field — the eleventh evening, a habit now, and a habit was a philosophy you had stopped noticing you held — and the field, before she had finished, offered them back to her in the grey of a suggestion: *observation ongoing*, learned by the system from a sample of one, filed now in the building's vocabulary alongside *reset* and *snapshot* and *certified baseline*, as if it had always been one of the things a tank could be doing.

She accepted the suggestion.

"Same time tomorrow, then," Kessler said, and picked up the dead woman's luggage, and left her to her heretics — who were hers now in a sense that would not have survived an audit, and did not need to, because the plot was still braiding in the dark behind her, and tomorrow there would be a fourteenth tree.
