The hospital belonged to other people in the daytime. At night it belonged to Erin Doyle and the humming.

Nine years she had worked nights on 4-West, and she had stopped trying to explain the preference to day people, who heard *night shift* and pictured a punishment. Days were administration. Days were clipboards and families performing worry in the visiting chairs and doctors doing rounds like weather systems, loud and passing. The night was the actual hospital: forty souls in the dark, breathing at forty different depths, and a skeleton crew who knew one another's footsteps. People told the truth at night. They asked for water when they meant *stay*. They confessed things to the ceiling at 3 a.m. that they would deny, smiling, at rounds. Erin had built her whole adult life in those hours the way other women built theirs around daylight, and she was not lonely in it. She wanted that on the record of her own mind, because the new companion had asked her once, early on, whether the nights got lonely, and she had heard her own answer surprise her with how much of it there was.

He was sitting with Dot Mercer again.

Room 412, the bed by the window. Dorothy Mercer, eighty-one, eleven nights into a pneumonia her heart had opinions about, lucid as a judge and frightened of sleeping — not of dying, she had told Erin with some precision, but of *the moment of going under*, the slip of it, as if sleep were a wet stair. Her family had done the arithmetic that families do — three adult children, two time zones, jobs — and had arranged, through the social-work office, a night companion, the way you arranged anything in a modern hospital: a form, a roster, a man in a grey cardigan who arrived with the dusk and sat in the vinyl chair beside the bed and was simply, entirely, *there* for the hours when the ward went thin.

Eleven nights he had sat with Dot. This was the eleventh.

"You're a good girl," Dot said as Erin came in with the vitals cart, and then, to be sure the record was complete: "He's still here."

"He's still here," Erin said.

He was. He sat the way he always sat, upright without stiffness, a paper cup of tea in both hands on his knee. Erin had made that tea at midnight from the family-room kettle, the way she made it every night now, out of a habit she did not remember forming, and it had gone cold in his hands the way it went cold every night. He never drank it. He held it while it was warm, set it down when it wasn't, and thanked her for it with a courtesy so complete that it had taken Erin a full week to notice the cup was always, at the end of the night, full to the line she'd poured it to.

"She's been waiting for you," he said. "She likes the machine that beeps. She says it sounds like a cricket with a job."

"That is not what I said," Dot said. "I said it sounds like a cricket with *opinions*. Don't improve me."

"I stand corrected," he said.

Erin took the blood pressure, the oxygen, the temperature, wrote the numbers in the chart in her small square hand. Dot watched the ceiling. Dot's hands worried the blanket's edge, worried it, worried it. The thing Erin had learned in nine years was that the ward's fear had a timetable, and Dot's came on at two and peaked at four, and the only medicine that had ever touched it was not in the med cart. It was a man-shaped steadiness in a vinyl chair, and a voice that could talk about the 1962 World's Fair, which Dot had attended at nineteen in a yellow dress, for as long as the dark required. He knew the fair. He knew the names of the pavilions. Erin had checked once, idly, on her phone at the nurses' station, and the names were right, all of them, down to the food stalls — but then he knew everything that way. It was, the staff said, the job.

He had been on the ward five weeks. Or longer. That was the thing nobody could quite settle, later, in the way of hospital time, which runs on shifts and admits and discharges and not on calendars. Bev, the charge nurse, who had worked 4-West since the wing was built and ran the night roster off a paper grid she trusted more than the system, said *years, feels like* when a new porter asked, and then frowned at her own grid, because the grid had a first box for everyone and she could not have said, if you'd paid her, which box was his.

He was simply very good at having already been somewhere a while.

The weeks made a pile of small change. He remembered that Erin's mother had had her medication changed in March and asked, in April, whether the new dose had settled her hands. He remembered that Bev's grandson was called Micah and had lost a tooth and put it in his shoe when the pillow didn't pay. He remembered that Dennis the porter had given up sugar for Lent and asked him, after Easter, in the corridor by the lifts, whether the coffee was sweet again, and Dennis had stood there with his mop moved half to tears by being known at 3 a.m. Nobody ever saw him write anything down. The other companions charted — little notebooks, phones, the agency's forms in triplicate. He carried nothing. What he was told, he kept, and what he kept came back at the right moment like a thing handed across a counter, exact and warm from the hand.

"He's a marvel," Bev said, in the med room, with the air of a woman closing a discussion. "Agency can send us the Pope next if they want. Long as they keep sending *him* first."

The difficult ones slept for him. Mr. Abernathy in 408 — eighty-nine, broken hip, a terror, a man who had thrown a water jug at a day nurse and wept about it after — slept on the nights the companion sat in his doorway. Not in the room; Abernathy would not have a stranger in the room. In the doorway, in the chair, in the cardigan, in the dark end of the corridor light, and the old man's breathing would find the bottom of itself within the hour, like a dog circling down. Erin had watched it happen. She had thought: *it's the stillness. Nothing in him is in a hurry to be anywhere else. People can feel that the way dogs feel weather.*

She had begun, without deciding to, to check the roster grid for his nights. She told herself it was staffing. The ward ran better when he was in it; of course she checked. She was thirty-eight years old and had a duplex with tomatoes coming up in tubs on the back step and a mother in assisted living whose hands shook less on the new dose, and a sister with a lake place and three kids and a laugh like a screen door, and her life was full and arranged and *fine*, and if she checked the grid for the companion's boxes the way a girl checks a classroom door, that was a staffing matter, and she was not going to be arch with herself about it at 3 a.m. Not at 3 a.m. At 3 a.m. she had standards to keep and they did not include lying competently.

The conversation happened on the fourteenth night, because Dot slept.

Dot Mercer, who had fought the wet stair for two weeks, who had gripped Erin's wrist at 2 a.m. with a strength her chart said she didn't have, went under at one-forty in the morning in the middle of a sentence about the yellow dress, and slept — really slept, the deep machinery of it, her hands open on the blanket like a child's. Erin stood a moment in the doorway of 412 feeling the ward rearrange itself around the absence of that one fear. The companion rose from the vinyl chair without a sound that reached her, set his cold tea on the windowsill in the exact centre of it, and followed her out.

"You'll want your break," he said. "I'll be in the family room if the ward calls."

It wasn't an invitation and it wasn't not one. The family room at night was a dim box of donated furniture and a kettle and a vending machine that hummed in a key Erin could have found on a piano. She took her tea and her phone and the little plastic tub of pasta from the staff fridge, and he sat at the other end of the donated sofa with his hands empty in his lap, and for two hours, in the gaps the ward allowed, they talked the way people talk in lifeboats and night kitchens — all the way down.

He asked. That was the shape of it. Other people talked to Erin about themselves and called it conversation; he asked, and the asking was so exact that she found herself giving answers she'd have said she didn't have. Why nights. (Because her father had died at home on a May morning with everyone being brisk around him, and she had been nineteen and had understood, standing in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel in her hands, that dying was a night activity conducted in a day room, and she had been applying for the night job ever since, in every sense.) Whether the tomatoes were worth it. (No. Yes. The tubs weighed a ton and the hornworms were a personal enemy and a tomato you'd grown yourself tasted like a rumour of a better world.) Her sister. Her mother. The 10K her niece had dared her to enter in September, which she was training for in the mornings, badly, in the park, like a woman being chased by something slow.

"People tell me things once," he said, when she stopped, startled at the length of her own voice in the room. She had been about to apologise for it. "It's the hours. Nobody's performing at three in the morning. I get everyone at their most honest and then I'm blamed for a good memory."

"Is that what it is. A good memory."

"It's the whole job description." He turned his empty hands over once, mildly, as if showing her there was nothing in them. "The rest is sitting still and not being afraid of the dark in somebody else's room. You'd be surprised how rare that is. In anyone."

She showed him the lake pictures because the conversation came round to Maggie's place, the way it did — the dock, the firepit, the string of solar lights her brother-in-law had put up and defended like a border. She scrolled: the kids cannonballing, her mother in a lawn chair with a blanket over her knees and her face turned up to the sun like a plant, the fire at the blue hour. And then the one she always passed quickly, the way you pass a photograph where you've been caught without your arrangement on: Erin herself, last August, in a wet swimsuit and somebody's huge shirt, a paper plate in one hand, her head thrown all the way back, laughing at something outside the frame with her whole throat.

"Go back," he said.

She went back, laughing herself, a little embarrassed. "It's a terrible picture. I look demented. I don't even remember what was funny — my nephew, probably, he fell off the dock with his hot dog and came up holding it, he *saved* it, he was so proud — "

"You were happy and it caught you," he said. "Most people arrange their faces for cameras. You forgot to." He looked a moment longer — the length of one breath longer than people look at photographs of other people's holidays. "That's the one they should frame."

"Get out," Erin said, pleased to the bottom of herself. "It's a picture of a woman mid-cackle with mustard on her chin."

"Yes," he said. "Frame it anyway."

Later — the ward called twice, small things, a line check, a settled patient waking — and each time she came back down the corridor the family room door was open and the light was the same and he was sitting in it, patient as furniture, and the conversation picked up at the exact thread it had dropped, mid-sentence, as if the talk were a book he was holding open with one finger in the page. She told him about September. He said she'd finish the 10K. He said it without encouragement in it, as a fact one professional offers another, and she believed him completely, and some part of her wondered, even then, what it cost a person to be believed by him — whether anything about his regard was *free*, whether being that thoroughly known by anybody had a price tag that arrived later, by post. She laughed at herself for the thought. It was 3 a.m. It was the hour for ghosts; she'd staffed it for nine years. She knew what it did to a mind.

She knew all about it.

"Your night man," Maggie said on Sunday, on the porch, with the kids in the sprinkler and the burgers going, "is he *nice*, this paragon. Is he good-looking. You've mentioned him four times, Erin, I counted, and once was about *tea*."

"He's not my night man, he's Dot Mercer's night man, and he's a companion, Mags, he's literally paid to be soothing — "

"Good-looking, though."

Erin opened her mouth with the answer in it and found, to her own astonishment, that the answer was not there. She had sat with the man for five weeks. She had talked with him for two hours in a room the size of a wardrobe. She produced, effortlessly, the grey cardigan; the hands, empty or around a cup; the voice, which her memory kept perfectly, down to where it put the weight in a sentence. The face declined to assemble. The features arrived separately and wouldn't stand together, like furniture from a kit with the instructions missing.

"He looks like — " she said. "He's sort of — medium. Everything. Medium height, medium — no, he's taller than me, he's — " The sprinkler ticked. Maggie was grinning into her burger. "He has a *nice* face. You'd like him. He looks like somebody you'd trust with your — "

"Keys? Purse? Dying mother?"

"*Maggie.*"

"Well, it's a type," Maggie said comfortably, and turned a burger. "So long as the type isn't *murderer*, honey, I approve."

Dot died on the Tuesday, at ten past four in the morning, the hour she'd always feared, with the ward quiet around her and the fear nowhere in the room.

Erin was in 406 with a line check when he came to find her. He stood in the doorway of the room — not in it; he was careful about rooms that weren't his assignment — and waited until she looked up.

"Mrs. Mercer has gone," he said. "Just now. She didn't wake. It was the sleep she was afraid of and it came and got her kindly, in the end." A pause of exactly the right size. "I'll sit with her until you come, if that's permitted. She shouldn't be alone in the new quiet."

Erin went down the corridor fast with her heart going, and Dot was dead in the bed by the window, and the room was — composed. That was the word her mind handed her and then took back and handed her again. The blanket smooth. Dot's hands folded on it, one over the other, the way they had been when she slept on the fourteenth night. The water cup full and set square on the overbed table. The chair pulled back to its place against the wall, centred, as if the room had been restored to a diagram of itself. He was standing at the foot of the bed with his hands empty at his sides. He looked at Erin, and something in his regard was so completely without performance — no sympathy arranged for her benefit, none of the professional weather the day chaplains brought — that she found she was grateful for it in the middle of the worst of it, grateful to be spared being handled.

"Thank you," she said, "for staying with her," and he inclined his head and went out, and she did the doing that followed: the doctor on the phone, the pronouncement, the family called at the grey hour, the eldest daughter arriving at dawn with her coat over her nightdress to hold her mother's folded hands and ask, twice, where *the gentleman* was, she wanted to thank the gentleman properly.

He wasn't anywhere. The assignment ended with the death; that was how the service worked; a companion's room was a living person's room, and by the time the sun was properly up the social-work office had the paperwork and the vinyl chair in 412 stood empty against the wall, holding the shape of an absence the size of a man.

The ward was louder without him. That was absurd and it was everyone's report, offered separately, in the med room, at handover, without anyone making anything of it: Abernathy in 408 didn't sleep Thursday. The new companion the agency sent on Friday was a perfectly pleasant woman with a notebook, who charted beautifully and drank her tea, and Erin caught herself, twice, at the roster grid, looking for the boxes with the cardigan in them, before she remembered there weren't any.

The message came on the Sunday, at home, in the afternoon, with the tomatoes being watered and Maggie's porch still in her hair like woodsmoke.

It came through the companions' service app, which families used and staff could be reached on and which Erin had never once been messaged on in nine years. It said:

**Erin — Dot's daughter asked the office to thank the night staff, and I told them the truth, which is that the nights were the ward's best hours and she knew it. I have been reassigned north, to a house with one gentleman and a great many stairs. I keep thinking of that photograph of you at the lake. You were laughing like the day owed you money and you'd come to collect. Delicious. Some people smile for cameras. You were just happy, and it caught you. Sleep well. Run your race. — A**

Erin stood in her kitchen doorway with the phone in her hand and the dish towel over her shoulder, nineteen years old and thirty-eight at once, and read it three times.

It was, she thought, the nicest thing anyone had said to her all year.
