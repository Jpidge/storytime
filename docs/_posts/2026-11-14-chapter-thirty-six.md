The man at the door rang once. Betty got as far as the passage before he knocked.

“I was coming,” she said when she opened it.

He took a step back. “I couldn’t hear it out here. I should have waited.”

“It works from the kitchen.”

He had a case with two catches and a folded sheet in his top pocket. He asked if she was Betty, then put a finger on the sheet where the name was printed. Sarah, who had come to wait with Betty, could see the finger through the gap between the door and Betty’s arm.

“What does it say about the entrance?” Sarah asked.

He drew the sheet out. “Front door. Ring once.” He lowered it a little. “It does say to wait.”

Betty held the door open for him. “You can put that down in here.”

He set the case on the floor and asked where the first test had been done. Sarah brought him the receipt. He took a small pair of glasses from behind the folded sheet and looked at the two lines she showed him.

“Which one did they send you for?” she asked.

He read the reference from his sheet. It was the original one. Sarah put her finger on the matching line. “That’s the one dispatch sent you under?”

“That’s the one they gave me this morning.”

“Can you see this other number anywhere?”

He looked on the back. There was a box with three lines of type in it. He read the print time to her: eight twelve that morning. Sarah checked the receipt. It was after Helen’s read-back. The route line ended at number twenty-six. The third said to return the sheet to the office.

“I haven’t got it,” he said.

Sarah wrote *eight twelve, original reference on visit sheet* beside the instruction Betty had given. She left the replacement on its own line.

Betty brought him a chair. He sat to undo the catches, one at a time, so the lid wouldn’t spring against his knee. Anne came out of the kitchen with the envelope in her hand.

“I heard both,” she told Betty.

“Both what?”

“The ring and the knock.”

Betty moved the cup on the hall table to give her room. Anne made a mark in each of the boxes she’d drawn. She looked down at the flap, then wrote the time underneath. She had to ask Sarah what time it was.

The man lifted the top tray out of his case. There was a sheet of brown paper underneath it, cut to the size of the case. He put it on the floor before he took anything else out.

“Do I sign your sheet when you’re done?” Betty asked.

“If you want a copy, I can leave you one.”

“Does it say what you did?”

He turned the sheet round on his knee. Below the return instruction there was a blank wide enough for a name. He looked at it for a moment, then took his pen out.

“That’s for your name,” he said. “I can put the work on the back.”

Betty brought the envelope over and laid it beside his sheet. She held the flap down with one finger so he could read the time.

“Before I sign,” she said.

When he was done, he turned the sheet over and wrote what he’d tested and what he’d left in place. He copied the two lines onto the sheet for Betty. She set them side by side and read both before she picked up his pen.
