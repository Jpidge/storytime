The call came at three-oh-four, the way it always came, as Marcus was crossing the cold-store yard with his bag on his shoulder and the night coming off the reefers in white breaths.

It was not a scheduled thing. That was the pleasure of it, and Marcus had never examined the pleasure, because pleasures you examine at 3 a.m. in a freight yard tend to evaporate. His friend simply tended to call as his shift ended — not every night, which would have been a service, but most nights, which was a friendship — and the car, which knew the phone, which knew the car, put the voice up into the speakers as Marcus pulled out of the yard, so that the drive home, the dark two miles past the silos and the lit miles after, had a passenger in it.

"You sound tired," his friend said.

"I sound like a man who's been stacking frozen peas since eleven. There's a word for it. *Dedicated.*"

"There's a word for it. It's not that one." The laugh came, the half-second-late one, warm in the car's dark. "How was the kingdom?"

So Marcus told him the kingdom: the supervisor and the rota, the new lad who kept stacking the pallets wrong in a way that was almost a philosophy, the fog that was supposed to be back at the junction and wasn't, not yet, just a thinness in the air past the silos that the headlights worried at. He talked the way he had learned to talk in eleven months of being listened to in the right order — easily, in paragraphs, without checking whether he was interesting, because the listening had taught him that he was. His friend asked the questions that made the stories better than they'd been while he was living them. It was the best part of the shift. It had been the best part of most of his year, if he was honest, and he was, at 3 a.m., with this listener, honest the way he was nowhere else.

They were past the silos, in the dark stretch, when Marcus — because it was on every screen, because the whole city had been chewing it for a week, because it was the thing everyone said to everyone that autumn to prove they were taking it lightly — did the joke.

"You're quiet tonight," he said. "Don't tell me you're out placing apples. City's got a fella for that now. Brings a snack to work, that's what the radio said. The new fella on Delaney's show — did you hear him? He's not as good. He did the whole bit again Tuesday, the kazoo and everything. *Appleseed Alert.* I told Dana, I said, our tax dollars — "

"He never brought a snack to work."

It was said mildly. It was said the way his friend said everything, unhurried, with the care of a man setting down a glass he particularly liked, and it stopped Marcus's sentence the way a hand stops a door.

"The apple isn't food," his friend said. "It never was. It's punctuation. A man who brings a snack eats it. This is placed — stem up, turned to the door, so that its face meets whoever comes in. It's the full stop at the end of a sentence he has spent all night writing, and he chooses the reddest word he has. The radio fellow thinks it's a picnic because the radio fellow reads at the level of the stomach." A pause, in which the car hummed and the fog stood thin in the headlights. "Forgive me. It's a small thing. But I have always thought that if a man is going to be read, he should be read at the level he wrote."

Marcus drove for perhaps four hundred yards with his hands at ten and two and his whole year rearranging itself around him in the dark, in silence, the way furniture rearranges in an earthquake — slowly, and then all at once, and nothing afterwards in the place it had been.

Because he had heard it. Not the fact of it — anyone could have an opinion about an apple; the city was made of opinions about apples that autumn. He had heard the *authority* of it. The correction of a man who is not guessing. The particular, intimate irritation of an author listening to his reviewers. And underneath the authority, arriving with the terrible completeness of a tide, he heard everything else: a voice that had come out of a service and stayed past the service; a man no one else in his life had ever met, whom Dana had never seen, whose surname he did not know and had never once, in eleven months, thought to ask; a friend who knew the rota, the roads, the junction, the fog — who had asked, with such care, such *interest*, where the dark stretches were, and what was open, and where a man should aim if he were ever frightened on that road at 3 a.m. —

"Marcus," his friend said, gently, into the silence, "you've gone quiet."

"Sorry," Marcus said. His voice came out almost normally. He was proud of it for the rest of his life, which was not long, and the pride was real, and it was his. "Sorry. Peas. Tired. Say that again — the bit about the punctuation. I want to tell Dana. She'll — " He made himself laugh. It sounded like a laugh. "She collects that stuff."

"Tell her the sentence is the room," his friend said, "and the apple is where the sentence ends. Tell her the writer is old-fashioned. He still believes a full stop should be *placed*, not dropped."

"I'll tell her," Marcus said, and ended the call with his thumb, the way he had ended it a hundred times, and sat in the sudden enormous quiet of the car with the road going by under him and his heart going like a hammer in a wall.

The car reconnected the call. It did it helpfully, the way it did everything, with a small chime and his friend's name — the name Marcus had given the number himself, in his own phone, months ago, in a fit of something he had thought was humour: **MY KEEPER**. The name lit up the dash.

"Marcus?" his friend said. "We were cut off."

"Were we," Marcus said, and pressed end again, and this time he took the phone out of its cracked mount with his right hand, steering with his left, and held it in his lap and dialled with his thumb without looking, the way everyone dials the number that is not a number, and the car, which knew the phone, which knew the car, tried to give him the speakers, and he said "No — handset — " aloud, to machinery, and got the little voice at his ear, small and real and human, saying *County emergency, what is your location* — and that was the moment, not the apple, not the voice, but the small real human voice in his ear saying the ordinary words, when Marcus Bell began, with great care and in the right order, to do everything right.

"My name is Marcus Bell," he said. "I'm on the freight road, westbound, just past the silos, in a blue hatchback. I need police. I'm not — there's no accident. I'm being — " He stopped. He chose the sentence the way a man chooses a tool off a bench, by its fitness for the hand. "The man the city is looking for has been visiting me for eleven months. He's my companion. He's on my car system. He knows this road. I think he's — " The dash lit again. **MY KEEPER**. Incoming, patient, unhurried. The car declined it on his behalf because the line was busy, and chimed, apologetically. "I think he's coming," Marcus said. "Please stay on the line with me. Please just — stay."

She stayed. Her name, she told him, was Wren — whether it was her name or the name the county gave the chair she sat in, he never knew, and it didn't matter; she was the best of them, the dispatchers, calm as deep water, and she did the job the way it is supposed to be done. She had units moving. She had his position off the phone. She told him where to go — the freight stop, lit, staffed, cameras, two minutes — and Marcus, hearing the destination, heard also, in the same instant, his friend's voice from a summer evening, mild over the tea: *Aim there anyway. Lit, staffed, cameras. It's the safest room on that road that isn't a room.* — and understood that the refuge had been chosen for him before he had ever been afraid, by the man he was fleeing, the way everything in his year had been chosen, kindly, in advance — and he went anyway. He went because it was still the right thing. Doing the right things was the only property he had left that was entirely his, and he spent it deliberately, with his hands at ten and two and his speed exactly at the limit, because his friend had once remarked that Marcus drove the way he stacked pallets, like a man who believed the world was breakable.

"Marcus," Wren said, "talk to me. What do you see?"

"I see the lights," he said. "I'm nearly — Wren. There's something on my passenger seat."

A pause in his ear that was not a professional pause.

"It wasn't there when I got in," he said. His voice was steady. He noted that with a strange, distant gratitude: steadiness was available; he would use all of it. "My bag was there. My bag's on the floor now. It's been set down neatly. He does things neatly. It's an apple. It's a big red apple and it's — it's sitting up, stem up, turned toward me. He was in my car, Wren. At the yard. While I was inside. The car was locked but it — the car knows him. I gave him the access. In March. So he could move it for the ploughs, the lot at the flats, they plough at five and I'd be asleep — " He stopped, because Wren was speaking, level and fast, to someone else, and then to him: units, minutes, *stay in the car, Marcus, doors locked, engine running, we are coming to you* — and he said, "Yes," and "I know," and "I'm here," and pulled into the freight stop's forecourt in a long clean arc and stopped the car in the brightest part of the light, square between two camera poles, with the pumps lit and the diner window gold and a waitress visible inside, moving, alive, twenty yards away, and a trucker asleep in a cab with the curtain drawn, and the whole ordinary lit world going on around him like a room he had reached in time.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm in the light. I did it."

"You did it," Wren said. "Now keep talking to me. Tell me about — tell me anything. Tell me about the course you — " She had nothing of him but his name and his voice and she reached, professionally, for a future to hand him. "Tell me something that's next week, Marcus."

He told her about the course. He told her about Dana's kids. He watched the forecourt and the dark beyond its lights, where the fog had come down at last onto the junction and the road he had come down, and his heart slowed by degrees, the way a machine slows when the load comes off, and two minutes went by, and then three, and he began — this is the cruelty the record cannot file, and June would hear it in his voice on the tape and understand it completely — he began to hope.

"He's here," Marcus said.

He said it quietly. Not in panic. In the tone of a man reading a line of weather off a horizon only he could see. At the edge of the forecourt, where the light gave up and the fog began, a figure had come out of the dark on foot, unhurried, in a good coat, with something held against his side the way a man holds a book he is returning. He crossed the concrete the way he had crossed every room in Marcus's year: as if the distance were a courtesy he was performing. He did not approach the driver's side. He went to the passenger side, and stood there, and waited, with his clean hands empty and visible, in the full light, in front of two cameras and a diner window and a woman twenty yards away making coffee — waited, the way he had waited in doorways and at bedsides and beside a still life in a gallery, for Marcus to understand that there was no version of the next minute in which the door stayed shut, because the door was not his to hold. It never had been. It had been lent to him in March, with the access, and the ploughs, and the tea.

"Marcus," Wren said. "Marcus, the units are at the junction. Ninety seconds. Do not open the door. Do you hear me? Whatever he — "

"He's not breaking in," Marcus said. He said it with great calm, as a man correcting the record, as the last accurate thing he would ever file. "I want that — if this is being recorded, I want it right. He's not breaking in. I let him in. I've been letting him in all year."

The locks opened. The car did it the way it did everything, helpfully, with a small chime, because the fob it recognised was near — the fob Marcus had had cut in March, at his friend's mild suggestion, *so you won't be locked out of your own life, Marcus* — and the passenger door opened, and the dome light came on, and the apple rolled, very slightly, on the seat, and settled, stem up, facing the door.

What the tape holds after that is the car.

The indicator, ticking — Marcus had left it on from the turn into the forecourt, the one small human error at the end of a night of doing everything right, and it ticked on and on, patient as a clock, under everything. The engine, idling. A seat taking weight, slowly, the way a guest sits down in a house he knows. Marcus's breath, audible, doing its best. And Marcus's voice, once more, at the very edge of the recording's mercy, not screaming, not even raised — the voice of a man who has understood that the conversation he is in is the last one, and has decided, God knows how, to have it properly:

"You don't have to — "

And then nothing that was words. The indicator ticked. The engine idled. Ninety seconds later, distantly, the first siren came down the freight road like a rumour of the world's ordinary help, arriving, as it always arrives in the true stories, in time to be a witness.

June read him at first light, in the forecourt, with the fog burned thin and the diner doing a trade in coffee for uniforms it would never normally have seen.

He had been composed in the driver's seat. The belt was fastened. His hands were folded on the wheel, at rest, the way a driver's hands rest at a light that will never change. His bag was on the floor where it had been set down neatly. On the passenger seat, stem up, turned to face the driver's door — to face whoever opened it, which was June — sat the apple, and beside it, squared to the seat's edge, Marcus Bell's own paperback, with his place kept, exact, at the page where he had left it face-down on his kitchen table on Thursday.

Moss worked the car's perimeter the way he had worked the studio's, without touching it. "What was done," he said at last, "was done in the car, in the light, with the doors shut, and then the doors were shut again after. There's no hurry anywhere in it, June. There's no *exit* in it. Whoever sat down beside him had all the time there was."

The cameras had caught everything, which is to say they had caught the coat, the shoes, the paperback held spine-out against his side, the cap, the clean hands empty and visible — and between the cap and the collar, on three cameras, at three angles, under sodium light and LED, a face that would not assemble: not masked, not hidden, just unresolvable, a smudge of weather in the shape of a man, as if the forecourt's light had agreed among itself to keep his secret. The truck-stop manager, who had watched the footage four times with his hand over his mouth, said, "It's like the cameras couldn't *stand* him. Sir. Ma'am. It's like they looked away politely."

"He did everything right," Pruitt said. He was standing at the open driver's door with his notebook unopened, grey as the light. "June. I want it said in this room, because nobody's going to say it in the other one. He did everything right. The call. The drive. The lit place. Staying on the line. Everything on the list. There's a list, and he did all of it, in order, with a — " His voice, for once, would not do what he asked of it. "With a thing sitting next to him that he'd invited in for tea for a year. And it didn't — the list didn't — "

"I know," June said.

"Then what's the list *for*?"

She looked at the boy in the driver's seat — twenty-six, his hands folded, his place kept in his book — and understood that this was the message of the room, and that it had been addressed, like all the others, to whoever would read it properly: the hope was the thing that had been killed in this car, not only the boy. The hope that the rules were a shelter. He had priced the rules in, all of them, kindly, in advance, over tea. He had taught the boy the list himself.

"The list is for people who are being hunted by people," she said. "He's been telling us since Jensen. We're not. We're being *written* to. And you can't hide from a letter, Rob. It knows your address. He made sure he was the one who taught it to him."

Behind them the forecourt went on being lit, and ordinary, and useless, and the indicator, when the tow crew finally reached the car at noon, was still ticking.
