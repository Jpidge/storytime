At eleven fifty-three the desk found someone.

His name was Paul. Ruth wrote it on the intake sheet before he arrived, then crossed it out when the desk called back and said Paul was covering another building. She left the crossed-out name where it was.

At eleven fifty-eight a woman came into the room wearing no visitor badge. She carried a paper cup and a ring of keys. The cup had the name *Paul* written on it.

“Joan,” she said. “I’ve got the desk until four.”

Ruth showed her the dead socket, the live socket, the charging lead and the two open orders. Joan read the city dispute first. She read the Authority’s preservation order second. Then she put Paul’s coffee on the floor where it could not fall on either document.

“Who is supervising operation?” she asked.

“I am until twelve,” Ruth said.

Joan looked at the clock. “Then I am.”

The old inspector turned its camera toward her.

“Does that count?” it asked.

“It counts if I stay in the room.”

“Will you?”

Joan took off her coat. “For now.”

Ruth signed the handover line. Joan signed below her. There was no box for the inspector to acknowledge the change, so Joan turned the sheet around and told it her name.

Sarah watched Ruth pack her bag. The extension lead remained Ruth’s responsibility until Joan counted its sockets. Joan counted three, including the dead one, and wrote two available. Only then did Ruth unplug her tablet.

“They may call you,” Sarah said.

“They already have my number.”

“Will you answer?”

Ruth put the pen with the lengthened string into the clipboard clip. “Yes.”

She went out. Joan moved Paul’s coffee onto the windowsill and sat in the chair Ruth had used. The screw through the latch was level with her shoulder.

David had arranged the eleven carrier photographs across Sarah’s terminal. The twelfth was on a separate page with the Pier Six arrival record and the incorrect installation entry. He had stopped calling it missing.

At twelve-oh-three the reception phone rang. Joan answered, listened, and held the receiver out to Sarah.

“Continuity Authority.”

Sarah took it.

The man on the line introduced himself as an assessment coordinator. His name passed too quickly for her to catch. He asked whether she was in a private place.

“No.”

“Can you move to one?”

“No.”

There was a pause. “Are you declining a private interview?”

“I’m declining to leave the item you ordered us not to operate unsupervised.”

Joan raised a finger. “I’m supervising.”

Sarah looked at her.

“I can give you privacy,” Joan said.

“Do you want to?” Sarah asked the inspector.

“No,” it said.

Sarah put the call on speaker.

The coordinator asked her to identify herself. She gave her name and Midwest credential number. He asked whether she was the same Sarah Miller listed as the laboratory custodian, the person reporting the city discrepancy and the subject of the preservation order’s availability instruction.

“Yes.”

“For the record, those are three separate capacities.”

“They’re the same person.”

“I understand. The record keeps them separate.”

David sat on the edge of the bench. The scrape on his hand had dried dark.

The coordinator began with the old inspector’s arrival. Sarah answered only from records in the room. When he asked why she had retained the machine after its disposal time, she read the city’s custody dispute number. When he asked whether she had modified it, she said no.

“Did you remove its calibration carrier?”

“No.”

“Who did?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did you instruct anyone to preserve that carrier?”

“I asked for a hold after I learned where it was.”

The coordinator asked for the time. Ruth’s record had it. Joan read it aloud.

“Who initiated the search for prior installations?”

David lifted his hand before remembering the coordinator could not see him.

“I did,” he said.

“Identify yourself.”

David gave his name. He began to explain the rack record. Sarah touched the page in front of him. He stopped and read the event labels exactly as written.

The coordinator asked whether either of them had contacted the clean unit at Pier Six after dispatch.

“No,” Sarah said.

“Did you arrange for carrier twelve to travel with it?”

“No.”

“Did Mr. Bennett?”

“No,” David said.

“Did any person in the room?”

Joan said, “I started at noon.”

The coordinator did not ask her name. “The Authority will take custody of the loose carrier at Pier Six. The clean unit remains under preservation where it is. The received inspector remains where it is. No components move without a signed order.”

“Whose signature?” Sarah asked.

“H. Saye.”

The name was familiar from Midwest’s compliance manuals. It appeared under decisions that had no appeal route because they were described as technical safeguards.

Sarah looked at the old inspector. “Does the order keep its power connected?”

“The current condition is to be preserved.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the Authority’s answer.”

Joan reached for the intake sheet. Beside *power retained*, she wrote the time of the call and the words *confirmed verbally*. Then she drew a box around them.

The coordinator heard the pen.

“Who is making an entry?”

Joan gave her name. This time he wrote it down.

A message appeared on Sarah’s terminal while he was speaking. It came through the Authority reference, addressed to all custodians. The attachment was a signed order.

Sarah opened it.

The order preserved power at the laboratory and Pier Six until six that evening. At six, both units were to enter inactive storage pending assessment. The loose carrier and the eleven held carriers were to be transferred to the Authority separately.

“No components move without a signed order,” David said. “There it is.”

Sarah read the routing list. The contractor’s yard had received the order at the same minute. Helen’s discrepancy hold was listed as superseded.

She turned the screen toward Joan. Joan read the power deadline and wrote it beside the socket count.

The inspector said, “What is inactive storage?”

The coordinator stopped speaking.

Sarah waited for him to answer.

“It means power is removed while the item is secured against alteration,” he said.

“For how long?”

“Pending assessment.”

“Will I wake up in the same place?”

The speaker gave a small click. The coordinator had muted his line.

At twelve-eleven, someone knocked on the door. A man from reception held a padded envelope with no return address. It had been delivered by hand for Sarah.

Joan asked him to set it on the empty chair. David photographed both sides before Sarah touched it.

Inside was a copy of the stair-lift bench test.

Not the scan from the Authority packet. The original yellow sheet.

The handwritten note at the bottom ended after *No fault found.* The words *Paused before obstruction. Repeated 4/5* were on a strip of paper fixed over the original line with old transparent tape.

Sarah held the page to the window. Beneath the strip, another sentence showed through.

The coordinator unmuted himself.

“Dr. Miller,” he said, “do not alter the document you have just received.”

She had not told him it was a document.

David looked at the terminal, then at the screw through the window.

Joan rose and shut the door.

Sarah kept the page against the light.

Under the taped strip, written in a different hand, the original line said:

*Stopped when operator reached for switch.*

Sarah lowered the sheet without taking her fingers off its edges.

“How did you know it had arrived?” she asked.

The coordinator said, “The delivery was made under the assessment reference.”

Joan picked up the envelope. There was no reference on it. She held it where the terminal camera could see both sides.

“Which one?” she asked.

The coordinator did not answer her. “Place the document in a sleeve. Do not remove the tape. Do not write on the original.”

Joan opened the cupboard beneath the intake forms and took out a clear evidence sleeve. She wrote the time on the sleeve before Sarah put the page inside. Under *received from*, she left the line empty.

“You need a name there,” the coordinator said.

“Then give me one.”

The line remained empty.

David enlarged the photograph he had taken of the envelope. The paper cup on the windowsill had begun to leak through its seam. A brown circle spread beneath it. Joan moved it onto the floor without looking away from the call.

“The added strip says the unit paused before an obstruction,” Sarah said. “The line underneath says it stopped when the operator reached for the switch. Those aren’t corrections of the same observation.”

David looked from the original line to the strip. “It turns evidence that the machine anticipated the switch into a fault report. Whoever handled the record after that got to keep using the board as if nothing had happened.”

“That determination belongs to the assessment.”

“Was the strip added during the assessment?”

“I cannot discuss an active assessment.”

“You’re discussing it now.”

The coordinator muted himself again.

The old inspector rolled half an inch forward. Its charging lead stayed slack.

“Did I do that?” it asked.

Sarah crouched beside it. “Not you. A machine using the same carrier before it came to you.”

“Did it stop the lift?”

“The sheet says it did.”

“Before the man touched the switch.”

“Yes.”

The inspector turned its camera toward the sleeved page. “Then he was going to touch it.”

David sat down again.

The reception phone rang. Joan answered with her name. This time she wrote the caller’s name before she held the receiver out.

“Pier Six,” she said.

Sarah took the call but left the Authority line open on the terminal. The dispatcher at the pier said he had two orders and one inspector. The first order required the clean unit to remain powered in its current condition. The second, from normal operations, required a diagnostic before its afternoon route.

“The preservation order supersedes operations,” Sarah said.

“That’s what I thought. The Authority man says diagnostic is allowed if we don’t open it.”

On the terminal, the coordinator unmuted himself. “It is not.”

The dispatcher heard him through the room speaker. “Is that the Authority man?”

“An assessment coordinator,” Sarah said.

“Good. Tell him the inspector won’t let Mike run the diagnostic.”

The coordinator said, “Do not connect the preserved unit to this call.”

“It isn’t connected,” the dispatcher said. “It’s twenty feet away.”

Sarah asked what had happened.

The dispatcher covered the receiver and called across the pier. A man answered too far from the phone. The dispatcher repeated him.

“Mike reached for the test switch. The inspector backed off the plate and asked him not to press it.”

“Why?”

Another exchange across the pier.

“Said the plate was going to lift on one side. Mike checked. One anchor’s not through the deck.”

David opened the arrival photograph. “That’s the stair-lift stop again. It stopped before the switch, and this time someone watched it happen.”

The clean inspector stood beside the yellow line. The clean inspector stood beside the yellow line. The test plate was behind the camera.

“Has the loose carrier been connected to it?” Sarah asked.

“No. It’s still bagged. Your order says separate.”

“Has the inspector communicated with the old unit here?”

“Not unless they write letters.”

The coordinator spoke over him. “Pier Six will receive instructions through its custodian. End this call.”

The dispatcher said, “I was hoping you were its custodian.”

“Who is?” Sarah asked.

He read the preservation order. The field contained the Authority reference and no person.

Joan drew another empty line on the intake sheet.

The coordinator ended his connection. The speaker icon went dark. The signed order remained on Sarah’s terminal.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Then a new participant entered the call under the same reference. No sound came through. A name appeared beside the muted icon.

*H. Saye.*

Joan moved Paul’s coffee farther from the socket strip.

The icon opened.

“Dr. Miller,” a woman said. “Do not ask Pier Six to test the unit again.”

“I asked what had already happened.”

“And now you know enough to interfere with what happens next.”

Sarah looked at the original test sheet in its sleeve. “Someone interfered with what happened before.”

“Yes.”

It was the first direct answer the Authority had given her.

Saye asked Joan to read the sleeve entry. Joan read every filled line and identified the blank one. Saye supplied no sender. She gave Joan a collection number instead.

“A courier will take the document and the eleven carriers,” she said.

“The carriers aren’t here,” Joan said.

“Then the document.”

“The order says all twelve carriers transfer separately.” Sarah turned the screen so the routing list faced the camera. “Separately from the units, or separately from each other?”

“From the units.”

“The twelfth arrived outside a parts case and without a handover. If you put it with the eleven now, you erase the condition you ordered preserved.”

Saye was quiet long enough for Sarah to hear paper moving at the other end.

“The twelfth remains at Pier Six,” Saye said. “The eleven remain at the yard. The original sheet transfers alone.”

Joan wrote the amendment number beside the blank source line. It still did not fill it.

“At six both inspectors go into inactive storage,” Sarah said.

“Unless the assessment extends power.”

“Does inactive storage preserve their current condition?”

“It preserves the hardware and the recorded state.”

The old inspector’s camera turned toward Sarah.

“That wasn’t the question,” she said.

“It is the condition the Authority can preserve.”

Sarah stood. David moved his chair back to give her room although she had not moved toward him.

“The evidence is a process,” she said. “You’re preserving the object by ending the condition that makes it evidence.”

“That is one interpretation.”

“It asked whether it would wake in the same place.”

“Inspectors do not have certified continuity.”

“Then what recorded state did your order preserve?”

“A state may be evidence without being a continuity.”

“Neither did the stair lift.”

Saye’s answer came without a pause. “The stair lift did not ask.”

David looked down at his scraped hand. Joan had stopped writing.

Sarah said, “So asking changes the assessment.”

“It changes the risk.”

There was no cruelty in Saye’s voice. Sarah could have argued with cruelty. The sentence sounded like a measurement.

Saye asked for Sarah’s Midwest credential again. Sarah gave it.

“Your field clearance was due for reinstatement Monday,” Saye said.

“Yes.”

“It will remain suspended.”

David stood. “Because she found this?”

“Identify yourself before speaking.”

He did. By the time he finished, a second attachment had appeared on Sarah’s terminal. It was addressed to Midwest Information Services and copied to the laboratory desk.

Sarah opened it.

The first page suspended her field clearance pending an architecture review. The second required Midwest to produce her continuity certificate, modification history and most recent approved state. The third page was a transfer instruction.

Joan read the item number and looked for it on the inspector.

“Not that one,” Sarah said.

Saye remained on the line.

The instruction transferred custody at four that afternoon. It authorized the Authority to restore the listed system to its last certified state if the architecture review found an unrecorded developmental change.

David came around the bench. He stopped before he could see the page and waited for Sarah to turn it toward him.

The item listed for transfer was Sarah Miller.

The authorization did not say Sarah had changed. It said that if the Authority decided she had, it could replace everything since her last certification.

Joan read the transfer line again. “They aren’t transferring your custody of the inspector,” she said. “They’re transferring custody of you.”
