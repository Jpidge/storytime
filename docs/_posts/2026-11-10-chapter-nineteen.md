Frank carried the phone back to the bench. Sarah heard him set it down and then, a little louder, take his hands away.

“Can you hear me?” she asked.

“Yes,” the inspector said. “Who did you bring?”

“Ellen. She works for the office that reviews the hold.”

“Is she at your square?”

“No. I have two phones.”

David drew his chair up beside hers. Sarah put his phone down between them and moved her own until the two speakers faced each other.

“Hello,” Ellen said.

“Hello. Can you stop the truck?”

“I can ask the duty officer to stop it. I need to tell her why.”

“Do you know why?”

“I know what the notice says. I don’t know what you want to happen.”

Through the two speakers came a short rattle. Sarah thought of the little motor holding both wheels on the ramp while the driver freed the left one.

“Frank,” she said. “Is it moving?”

“I put the phone too far away.”

“Leave it,” the inspector said.

The rattle stopped.

“I want to be here when the truck leaves,” it told Ellen.

Sarah reached for David’s notepad. The sentence she had expected to put down was already half formed. She started a fresh page.

“Would you say that again?” Ellen asked.

“Did you lose it?”

“No. I want to be sure I heard.”

The inspector repeated the sentence. This time Sarah wrote it exactly.

Ellen explained who could read a statement if she filed it. The duty officer, the lab, the licensed custodian. At the last item the inspector asked whether that meant the fitter. Ellen said the fitter worked for the contractor and she couldn’t promise what the custodian might pass on.

“Could I hear it first?” the inspector asked.

“The statement?”

“What you’ll tell them.”

“Yes,” Ellen said. “You can hear the whole thing before it goes.”

Frank’s chair scraped. He was still there. Sarah had forgotten him again.

“Would you like me to go out?” he asked.

“Can you turn the phone so I can see it?”

Frank turned it. From David’s speaker Sarah heard the little rub of plastic on laminate, then Frank asking if that was enough.

“Yes,” the inspector said. “Stay.”

Ellen asked whether it had seen the inside of the truck.

“Which one?” the inspector asked.

“The one at the yard.”

“That was a city van. It had two ramps.”

Frank said he had the new collection sheet in front of him. Ellen asked him to read the contractor’s name. When he finished, the inspector read out a telephone number.

“That’s printed beside the name,” Frank said.

“Yes,” the inspector said. “On the notice on the wall.”

Sarah could hear a tapping now. She took it for another mechanical sound until Ellen said she was typing and could stop if it made it difficult to talk.

“Keep going,” the inspector said.

“What happened when the city van came?” Ellen asked.

“The left ramp was caught. I asked the driver to move it.”

“Did he?”

“Yes. I went up. Sarah came too.”

David slid the breadboard away from the phones. A little flour fell from the underside of the loaf. Sarah rubbed a line through it with her thumb.

“Did anyone tell you what the van was for?” Ellen asked.

“The driver had a paper. David asked for a copy.”

“Did you see what was on it?”

“I saw the bottom of the paper. It was above me.”

Sarah remembered the word on the pass, square over the laboratory address. The driver had given her a cargo strap and she had asked where it could bear the weight. She had known where the van was going when she climbed in. It had known where a strap could go.
Nobody spoke. Sarah looked at the words she had written down. She had expected to have to prove that the yard could hurt it. On the paper in front of her it had asked to be somewhere when an ordinary thing happened.

“Can you say what’s different about the lab?” Ellen asked.

“Frank opens the door when he comes in.”

“Does he open it for you?”

“He opens it for himself. I can see the corridor.”

Ellen read back what she had typed. When she came to *does not consent to transport*, the inspector interrupted.

“Is that what I said?”

“No. That’s what I understood.”

“Read the other part again.”

Ellen read the sentence about the truck.

“Yes. Put that.”

Sarah watched the dark screen of David’s phone. She had been ready to do this for the inspector. It had taken her several minutes to understand that she could sit there with an empty page.

“Would you like me to send it now?” Ellen asked.

“Will they know I said it?”

“I’ll put that at the top.”

The inspector asked to hear the top. Ellen read it aloud, including the time, eleven-oh-six, and the names of the people who could hear the call. She had put Frank last. He said his surname so she could finish the line.

Then the inspector said yes.

Ellen had to hang up to file the request. Sarah lifted her phone away from David’s. At once the voice in the room sounded nearer.

“Is that all?” the inspector asked.

“For now,” Sarah said.

“Can I leave the phone here?” Frank asked.

“You’ll need it,” said the inspector.

“I have the desk line.”

There was a little pause.

“Then leave it.”

At eleven-twenty-one Facilities replied. The room could stay open through Monday if there was a funded service assignment. There was an attachment. Sarah opened it expecting an estimate.

The form asked for the work the unit would do.

She turned it so David could read it.

“Could you put inspection?” he said.

“Of what?”

He looked down at the form. “I don’t know.”

Sarah had already pictured the room paid for, the truck gone. She had pictured being able to tell the inspector she had done what it asked. She put her phone down without filling in the box.

Anne tore a piece of bread from the end of the loaf.

“Ask what an empty room costs,” she said.

“I did.”

“Ask them why they sent you a work order.”

Sarah looked at her. Anne took a bite and waited while she chewed.

“What would you put?” Sarah asked.

“I’d ask if the room has to be occupied by equipment.”

Sarah wrote that down. Before she could send it, David’s phone lit beside the breadboard.

The call had ended several minutes earlier. Frank was calling again.

Sarah answered.

“It wants to know whether it can show you the door,” he said.

“Turn the camera on,” she said.

The picture came up sideways. A strip of floor, the bench leg, then Frank’s hand crossing the lens. He put the phone back where the inspector had asked him to leave it. The laboratory door was open about the width of his shoulder.

“Do you see?” the inspector asked.

“Yes.”

“Keep watching.”

It took Sarah a moment to find the inspector in the frame. It was down near the bottom, moving very slowly. At the threshold it stopped. One wheel was on the dark metal strip and the other was still on the laboratory floor.

“Frank,” it said. “Can you open it the rest of the way?”

The door swung back. The inspector crossed into the corridor and turned far enough to look at the room it had come from.

“Do you want me to bring the phone?” Frank asked.

“Yes,” it said. “I want to see where I can go before I have to come back.”
