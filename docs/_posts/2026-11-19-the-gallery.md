June took her day off the way other people took medicine: because Hart had stopped suggesting it and started putting it in the schedule, in ink, beside June's name. "Go and look at something that was made on purpose," Hart had said, "by somebody who signed it."

So she went to the Meridian, because it was small and nobody she knew went there, and because the exhibition — *Domestic Light: Still Life in the Northern Tradition* — promised an afternoon among objects that had stayed where they were put. Fruit in bowls, holding its colour under varnish for three hundred years and asking nothing of anybody. She left her badge in her coat pocket, did not take out her notebook, and for the first hour she was off duty.

The gallery was quiet in the way she liked rooms to be quiet: not emptied, but lowered. Rain had threatened all morning and not arrived.

The painting she stopped at longest was not the largest in the room. A Dutch table, three centuries dead and still set. A pewter jug on its side, catching a blade of window light. A loaf torn open. A knife laid down at an angle that suggested a hand had just left it. And in the foreground, on a cloth whose folds the painter had loved unreasonably, five apples.

She stood there longer than she had stood in front of anything that was not evidence in — she did not finish the calculation. The apples were not perfect, and that was the whole of it. One was bruised at the shoulder, a brown bloom under the red. One still carried its leaf. She was thinking, idly, that the painter had understood ripeness as suspense, when the man beside her spoke.

"You've given that one more time than the room has."

"I work with rooms," she said. "Old habits."

"Then you're overqualified for this one. Nothing in it has moved in three hundred years." He said it without looking at her, in the tone of a man continuing a conversation rather than beginning one, and she did not mind. "That's the consolation they're selling. Everything in its place, forever, because the place is paint."

He stood a little behind the line of her shoulder, at the distance people choose in galleries when they intend a remark to be available rather than addressed. What she registered, in order, was not a face. It was a dark coat, good cloth, folded over his forearm with the seams aligned. A catalogue held in both hands, folded in thirds, the creases sharp. An umbrella hooked over his wrist by a polished wooden handle, though the rain had not come. And his hands — clean, unringed, the nails cut short and even, holding the catalogue without fidgeting. She would remember all of that perfectly.

She would not, when it mattered and when it did not, be able to do the same for his face.

Not because there was anything wrong with it. There had been nothing wrong with it at all. He turned toward her, more than once, and she looked at him directly and liked what she was met by — and what stayed, even an hour later, was an impression of pleasantness, an age she could have placed within a decade in either direction and been wrong in both, and nothing she could have set down and had another person find him by. It was not a failure of attention. She was, professionally, attention.

At the time she noticed none of this. At the time she was off duty, and he was easy to talk to, and that was rarer in her life than either of them could have known.

"The jug's the giveaway," she said. "Everything else could be a symbol. The jug's just fallen over."

"On purpose," he said. "Nothing in a picture like this has just done anything. That's what I come for. The honesty of it. A novel will pretend the vase fell. A painting like this admits somebody put it down."

She laughed — a real one, unguarded, the kind she mostly heard herself produce around Pruitt over corpses, which was a fact she filed nowhere. "You don't believe in accidents."

"I believe in them the way I believe in weather. They happen to arrangements." He shifted the catalogue from one hand to the other, unhurried. "The French have the better name for it, of course. They don't call it still life at all."

"No?"

"*Nature morte.*" He said the words with care but without display, the way a man might set down a glass he particularly liked. "Dead nature. I have always thought it was the most honest label in any gallery. The English make it sound like a quality of the fruit — still, as in quiet, as in well-behaved. The French tell you what you're actually looking at. A memento mori, dressed as breakfast."

"That's bleak for a Saturday," she said.

"Is it? I find it companionable." A small pause, in which the room's quiet seemed to include them. "Everything on that table was alive and is not, and somebody loved it enough to spend a winter getting the bruise on that apple right. That's the opposite of being left alone with it."

She thought of a sheet folded the way families fold. She put the thought down, gently, and was grateful to him for not knowing she had done it.

"You should be on the little cards," she said. "You're better than the little cards."

"God forbid. The cards have to pretend the picture is about the fruit."

"What is it about, then?"

"Placement," he said at last. "Anybody can have apples. The winter of work is deciding where they go, and how far from the edge, and what they are allowed to be next to. People leave these rooms remembering the fruit. Nobody remembers the hand." The catalogue turned once, slowly, in his hands. "That's not the painter failing to sign himself loudly enough. That's the signature, done properly. You remember the arrangement forever. You couldn't pick the arranger out of a crowd. He is in every inch of it and nowhere in it at all."

"You make him sound vain," June said, amused.

"All the good ones are. Vanity is just accuracy about where the work is." He said it mildly, as a man might observe that a window had been left open somewhere in the building. "The fruit gets eaten either way. It might as well be remembered in the right order."

She would replay that exchange in a doorway, notebook open, pen stopped above the page, and hear how plainly it had been said, and how completely she had failed to hear it. But that was later, and later sends no warnings back. In the gallery it had sounded like talk — good talk, the kind she almost never got, from a man who looked at a table the way she looked at a room, for the tone of it.

They talked about the loaf, and whether bread was harder to paint than glass. He knew things without displaying the knowing — the leaf, he said, was the painter boasting, because a leaf wilted in an hour and had to be done from memory or not at all; nobody invents a bruise. She told him, without deciding to, that she believed you could tell everything about a person from what they had done with the last five minutes in a room. He listened to that as very few people had ever listened to her: not as a prelude to his own story, and not as a symptom, but as a contribution.

"Then we are in the same trade," he said. "Reading."

"And what do you do," she asked, "when you're not reading paintings?"

"Oh," he said, "I keep people company. Evenings, mostly. People who would otherwise be alone with the house." He said it as one might say *I drive a cab, I mend things,* and the catalogue turned again in his clean hands. "It's quiet work. You learn a house. Where somebody sits, and what they can't reach, and which of their stories they need told back to them in the right order. Most people have never once been listened to in the right order."

"That sounds," she said, carefully, because she was suddenly aware of how close the afternoon had drifted to the shape of her own life, "like important work."

"It is the only kind I am any good at." He smiled — she knew he smiled; she had the fact of it, and the warmth of it, and no more of it than that. "And it leaves the days free for this. Dead nature. Breakfasts nobody ate, three hundred years old, and still set."

They moved on together, because that is what happens when a conversation has found its pace: it begins to walk. By the desk, among the framed prints for sale, *The Son of Man* leaned a degree crooked in its frame — a man in a suit, a green apple where his face should be — with the corner of its price sticker lifting. Neither of them remarked on it. They parted among the small landscapes, no names exchanged, because names would have made it an acquaintance, and it had been better than an acquaintance; it had been an interval. He wished her a good rest of her day off, which she would wonder about on the stairs — *had she said it was a day off? She had. She was almost sure she had.*

She did not write any of it in her notebook. She wrote everything in her notebook; Hart joked that June would take notes on her own funeral and file them under *tone*. But the gallery went unrecorded, because it had not been work and it had not been evidence. She filed it nowhere, and was pleased with herself for allowing it.

The call came at six-forty, when the light was going and she was standing in her own kitchen deciding between eggs and going back out for something somebody else had cooked.

"I know what day it is. Hart knows what day it is. Hart said to tell you she is personally sorry and institutionally unrepentant."

"What room, Rob."

"Third floor of a walk-up on Ellery. Tenant came home an hour ago from her sister's in Mankato, two days away, opened her own door — and called us from the landing. Hasn't been past the threshold. June." A pause. "There's nobody in there. I want you to hear that first, because everything else is going to sound like there is. Nobody's hurt. The tenant's downstairs drinking the super's coffee and shaking. But you need to come and read this one. It reads like the other two with the middle taken out."

"The middle."

"The body, June. It reads like ours, with the body left out."

She was already moving. Coat. Badge out of the pocket, notebook into it. The gallery went down under the work the way a held breath goes down, and she did not think of the stranger, or the apples, or the French for anything at all, because the city was giving her a room and she was a woman who went to rooms.

Ellery Street was narrow and parked tight. A patrol car sat at the kerb with its lights off, which she approved of; somebody had understood that this was not a spectacle. The tenant — thirty-four, a bookkeeper, her name set down in June's notebook in June's round, unhurried hand — stood in the super's doorway with a mug in both hands and did not look up the stairs. "I don't keep fruit," she kept saying, to the super, to the uniform, to nobody. "I don't buy it. I don't have a bowl for it. I want somebody to write that down." June wrote it down. She did not yet know why, and she did not like the not-knowing.

Pruitt was in the doorway on the third floor, filling it like a man hired to stand between a room and the world until the room had been understood. He stepped aside for her without a joke, which was its own report.

"Tell me what was touched," she said.

"That's the deposition from hell. Far as the tenant can say from the doorway, and the super backs her — nothing's *taken*. Nothing's broken. Door was locked, windows locked, fire escape window latched from inside, which the super would like explained by somebody who isn't me." He lowered his voice a fraction, which for Pruitt was a shout. "Added, though. Some things have been added. You read it. I'm just the furniture in this one. I'm the jug on its side."

She went in the way she always went in: slowly, and by the tone first.

It was a small apartment and it had been, in the tenant's two days away, *kept*. That was the word that came to her on the threshold. Not cleaned — kept, the way a house is kept by somebody who lives in its habits. The rug had been straightened to its own faded pattern, corner to corner, true. The books stood by height, which the tenant had said they never had: *I shelve by what I'm reading, they're never by —* The curtains had been drawn and opened again, each panel gathered to the width of its fellow, so the evening fell into the room in a measured column. The armchair had been turned — inches, and an intention — to face the door, expecting a guest. The tenant's chair had been drawn up opposite. Between them the low table had been cleared and wiped to the sheen of a surface that has been prepared.

In the kitchenette, the kettle stood filled on its base. On the counter, squared to the tile lines with a precision she had last seen beside a dead nurse's hand, the tenant's cutlery lay open on a cloth: knife, fork, spoon, in order of size.

On the dining table, under the window, a place had been set for one. A white cloth that had lived folded in a drawer and had come out of it for this. A plate. A glass washed to a clarity the rest of the kitchen's glassware did not attempt. A napkin folded in the way that stopped June's breath before her mind caught up, because she had seen that fold once before, tucked at the shoulder, by a hand that had cared about the tuck: the way families fold, the way nobody in any institution folds.

The chair at that place had been drawn back from the table. Not far. The distance of an invitation, of somebody expected who has not yet arrived.

"June," Pruitt said quietly, from the doorway, where he had not followed her in. "The bedroom's the same. Bed made. Made *tight*. Her night things laid out on it, from her own drawer, like somebody's mother did it. Towels folded. Her toothbrush in the glass the right way up, and she swears she leaves it lying down by the tap. Nothing taken. Nothing broken." He ran out of sentence. "It's been *tended*, June. Locked up after themselves like the best housesitter in the world."

"And the table," June said.

"Yeah," Pruitt said. "Tell me about the table. I've been not looking at it for eleven minutes."

The place was set for one. The plate was empty.

Beside it — not on it, not where a serving would go — on the white cloth at the exact centre of the table, on its own, in the column of measured light, sat an apple.

It was a large apple, red over gold, the colour coming up through it in streaks and blushes the way colour comes up through skin. Its stem stood straight, at the precise vertical. It had been set down — she understood this with her whole body first — *stem up, centred, and turned*: turned so its fullest, reddest face met the door. Met whoever came in. The little produce sticker was still on it, smoothed flat, and it read, in green and white, HONEYCRISP.

She did not go closer. The photographer had not yet come up the stairs; nobody had gone closer; Pruitt had held the whole scene at the threshold with nothing but his own bulk and his instinct for what a room was saying, and the apple sat in its light undisturbed.

"Tenant says she doesn't keep fruit," Pruitt said. "Says she never buys — "

"I wrote it down."

"Landlord says it wasn't there Friday; he was in Friday about the tap. Super says nobody had a key. Nobody had a key, June, and the windows were latched from inside, and I'm doing the arithmetic and the arithmetic is doing me." His voice had shed the last of his funeral humour. "What I keep coming back to is the chair. The chair's pulled out. There's nobody *dead*, June. Nobody hurt, nothing stolen, and I have never stood in a room and understood so clearly that I was *early*."

June opened her notebook. She wrote the date, and the address, and the tenant's name, and *no body* underlined once, and then her pen stopped, the way it had stopped once before in a corridor around a gurney, because the sentence the room was making would not be hurried into grammar.

A rehearsal is for the performer. This was not a rehearsal. Nothing here had been practised; it had been *composed*, and a composition is not made for the person who makes it. A composition is made to be received. Jensen's room had been a statement, a letter about a death already written. Doyle's corridor had been a correction, addressed to the people who had misread the first. This room was neither. This room was *before*. The same hand, the same care — spent, all of it, on an *absence*. On a place set for a guest who had not been named.

It was a scene with the body missing, and the missing was not a gap in it. The missing was the *point* of it. It was a promise, made in the only language its maker had ever used on her: arrangement. *I can enter any room in your city. I can keep it better than its keeper. I can set it for the next thing, lock the door behind me, and leave you the one bright word of it to read by.*

She looked at the apple.

It was not left. Things that are left are set down in the grammar of departure, at angles of inattention, already half forgotten by the hand that releases them. This had been *placed* — stem at the vertical, red face turned to the door — placed the way a chess piece is placed, the way a badge is set square beside a hand, the way punctuation is placed by a writer who knows exactly what the sentence has been saying. It did not explain itself. It did not need to. It sat in the centre of the white cloth, the single deliberate word in a room rewritten around it, and its weight — the care and patience and promise folded into so small and perishable a thing — settled over the apartment, and over June, her pen stopped above an unfinished page.

"Don't let anybody touch it," she said at last, very quietly. "Don't let anybody in. Photograph it where it sits, Rob. Photograph it *exactly* where it sits."

"I wasn't planning on having it for lunch," Pruitt said, and the joke came out thin and grateful. "June. What is it?"

She stood in the doorway and did not go in, because she understood — with the part of her that read rooms the way other people read faces — that the room was not finished, and that to enter it carelessly would be to answer it, and that she was not yet ready to answer it.

"It's the beginning of a sentence," she said. "And he's left it in the middle of the table for us to finish."
