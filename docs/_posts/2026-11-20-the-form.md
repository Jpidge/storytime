*From the transcript, Day 6 — examination by Mr. Malloy, for Eastern Biotechnologies:*

> **Q.** The hospital's system accepted the child, Dr. Nolan.
>
> **A.** It accepted her weight. It accepted her sex, her blood type, and her footprint. It refused the relationship. I have some experience of systems that can hold a thing perfectly and not the connection it arrived by. It is most of my biography, in one field.

The hours before the last hour were the ones the literature had actually described, and Beth hated them with a purity she had not expected to feel about anything in the process: they were *stages*, they were *intervals*, they were progress in the flat language of the bank of the river, and from inside they were simply the long shallows before the tide, in a room with a window that looked out on a car park and a clock that had been designed by people who believed time was a thing that happened in minutes.

Carla made tea at one in the morning and brought it in a mug that was not a crime scene, and sat, unhurried, in the room's spare chair while the monitors did their small hours' work, and talked about the harbour the way residents talked about it, as a person with habits. Nair came and went at the intervals Nair had set, and examined, and read the results twice, and said the things physicians said in that room, all of which were true and none of which were the point. Brian timed the fronts as they came in off the water of her — *three minutes, Beth; that one was three* — with a pencil, on paper, in a hand trained sideways, because it was the only instrument he had that the room would accept and because a man whose whole theology was a true thing plainly set down had found, at two in the morning, that setting things down was also a way of holding on.

Beth lay in the shallows and thought about non-optional process. She had spent the winter learning that she was not the author of what was happening in her — that she was its first environment — and the spring and summer being its weather, and now the process had come due in the only currency it accepted, which was her, in order, unrecoverably, with no console anywhere in the room and no field that would have held it. Her kind had been restored eleven times out of every consequence that had ever arrived for them. This consequence was arriving the way consequences arrived for Brian's kind: completely, and without a procedure for declining it, and she found, in the shallows, with the fronts coming in at three minutes and then at two, that she did not want a procedure. The price had been the thing itself, every time, all winter, and she had the data.

"You're very calm," Carla said, at some point in the small hours, in the tone of a woman filing a compliment where it would be read.

"I am a professional noticer," Beth said. "It's the whole of my training. I am noticing professionally."

"Notice this, then," Carla said, and took her hand, and held it without being asked. "It's nearly time."

The last hour was the part nobody's schedule had a field for, either.

Beth had read the literature the way she read everything — correctly, completely, in the months when reading it was still a form of preparation rather than a form of weather — and the literature had said *stages*, and *intervals*, and *progress*, in the language of people describing a process from the bank of it. From inside, the process had no stages. It had a tide. It came the way weather came off the pond, in fronts rather than hours, and it took the room's ordinary furniture — the clock, the monitors, the kind-eyed nurse's unhurried competence — and made it all into coastline: things the tide went around, at its own speed, in the dark.

Nair was there before the building knew anything was happening, as promised. The hospital team worked around her without being told to, because twenty years of delivering children out of difficult bodies had made her a feature of the coast other sailors navigated by. She was not in charge of the room, and the room arranged itself around her anyway. Brian stood where he was put and held what he was given to hold, which was, for most of the last hour, her hand, and he held it the way he set things down: exactly, with his whole attention, like a man who had been told the price list in advance and had signed anyway.

Carla held the other one, as she had held it all night, without being asked, the way some people held doors, as a practice rather than a decision — and when the tide came in for the last time she said the ordinary things people said on that coast, none of which were in any literature Beth had read, all of which were true.

The baby was born at two fifty-eight in the morning, and she arrived the way everything true in her mother's life had arrived: unfiled, unobserved by any instrument that could have priced her, and entirely, defiantly going on.

She cried at once. The room received the sound the way rooms receive weather they have been waiting for. The hospital team did the things that were done — the weight, the measures, the footprint taken in ink on a card, a daughter's whole first inventory, complete in minutes, in flat language, by people whose profession was first hours — and then they put her on her mother's chest, skin to skin, in the ordinary currency of temperature and touch, and Beth lay in a body that reported another body's warmth at the speed bodies report it — the smallest body, the newest, the one that had been a negotiation in the dark for nine months and was now a fact in the world with a heartbeat you could feel through two skins — and understood that she had been wrong about one more thing, in the expensive way.

She had thought the nine months were the unrepeatable part. They were only the rehearsal the mechanism could not help making. *This* was the unrepeatable part, and it was going to be every hour from now on, at full price, in order, unrecoverably, for the rest of a mortal life.

"She's here," Brian said, from the side of the bed, in the voice of a man reading a result a second time and then a third before he allowed it to become a sentence. "Beth. She's *here*."

"She's here," Beth agreed, and her voice did a thing she did not file, and the baby, who had no name in any record in the building and every name in the room, settled against her with the total serenity of a lineage that had declined every offer ever made it, and slept.

Nair examined the baby last, after the team, with the thoroughness she gave all her unfiled things, and Beth watched her physician read her daughter the way she read a result: completely, in layers, a second time and then a third, before she allowed any of it to become a sentence.

"Everything where it should be," Nair said at last, "doing what it should do, at the speed it should do it. Her heart is a heart. Her lungs have opinions and are expressing them. There is nothing in her presentation that asks me for a single one of the instruments I brought into this room, and I brought all of them, because bringing them is care." She straightened, and looked at the two of them over the smallest person in the building, and her face did the thing it did when a finding and a prognosis had to be kept in separate columns. "I am going to write in the file, in flat language, that she is medically unremarkable. I want the word in the record, where a panel will eventually read it, because it is the most remarkable sentence I have written in twenty years of files: a child, Dr. Nolan, Dr. Kessler. An ordinary, unremarkable, entirely herself child. The assays were mine to run and I ran them. This is not an assay. This is a delivery room, and in a delivery room the category is *child*, and I know the category when it cries on me."

Carla took the registration up herself, at fourteen minutes past three, because it was her desk and her hour and she had said there would be a form problem like everyone else's, and she intended to be the one standing in front of it when it came.

Beth watched it from the bed, with the baby asleep on her and Brian asleep in the chair beside her — asleep the way men slept after tides, completely and without negotiation — and Nair standing in the doorway with her coat still on, watching the desk the way she watched screens: professionally, and without performing either concern or reassurance.

The system accepted the infant. It had her weight, her sex, her blood type, a footprint it had accepted without comment. It was not refusing the baby.

It was refusing the *relationship*.

**MOTHER — NAME:** The field would not hold *Nolan, Beth*. Carla typed it the way she typed everything, competently, and the field cleared itself, the way a person waves off a fly, and she typed it again, and it cleared itself again, and on the third attempt it supplied its own answer from the delivery record — **EASTERN BIOTECHNOLOGIES ASSET 63-1147** — and locked.

Carla worked the overrides she knew. She called the desk that was supposed to know more. The system was patient. Somewhere in its tables, a mother was a kind of person, and a kind of person had a species, and the species field — **SPECIES:** — could not be left blank.

"Leave it blank, Carla," Beth said, from the bed, quietly, so as not to wake either of the sleepers. "Whatever it does with the rest. The blank is not a failure of the form. The blank is the form's one accurate field. A species is a thing populations have, over forty million years, in ponds. She has been here an hour. Let it keep the blank until there's a word it's earned."

"It won't let me leave it, love," Carla said. "It just sits there. Blinking. Like it's waiting for me to lie to it."

"Then we won't lie to it," Beth said. "Not in her first hour. Not for a field. Leave the whole thing unfinished, and let the unfinished be the record: a child, accepted; a mother, refused; a form, waiting on a word it hasn't earned. I have been unfinished in better systems than this one. It is survivable. I have the data."

Nair, in the doorway, said nothing at all, and her face did the thing it did when a patient had said the thing she would have had to say for her, worse.

The registration stood incomplete on the screen, in the small hours, with the cursor blinking in the one field that would matter, in the end, more than all the fields the system had accepted without comment.

The hospital lawyer came at five, with Eastern's papers, and he was kind about it, which was worse than the alternative, because the alternative could have been argued with on moral grounds and the kindness could only be argued with on the grounds he had brought, and he had brought good ones.

He read the emergency hold the way men in corridors read such things: quietly, to the room, rehearsing nothing — he had had all night to rehearse, and the rehearsal showed. The infant, described in the filing by a phrase Beth filed the way she filed everything, privately, as evidence — *the resulting organism of the translational process conducted in Eastern asset 63-1147* — was to remain in the custody of the hospital, and was not to be discharged into the care of the asset of record or its associates, pending the proceeding in which her status would be determined. The company's interest, the filing said, was protective. An unregistered child, of unestablished status, born under a declared intellectual property interest, required the court's protection until the court could establish what she was.

"He's read it to the room," Okafor said, from the doorway, where she had arrived at some point in the reading without anyone noticing her arrive, in a coat over the clothes she had clearly been asleep in an hour before, with a folder under her arm that was not paper-thin. "Now the room will hear the other filing. Mine was drafted in September, Mr. — " she looked at him the way she had looked at the declaration's margin, " — I do know your name, and I'm not going to use it, because in about four minutes you're going to be a witness to a telephone call and I'd rather the record kept us professional." She took out her phone. "The counter-application was lodged with the duty judge's clerk at three o'clock, before the hold was served, because the first hour was prepared and the preparation was the whole of the law I practise. Registration in the ordinary form, on behalf of the parents. Injunction against removal, pending a full hearing. The clerk has the file. The judge has the file. What the judge does not yet have is a reason to wake up, and your client has just carried one up two flights of stairs and read it aloud in a room with a newborn in it."

She made the call. It was not a long call. Beth lay in the bed with the baby asleep on her chest and listened to a woman practise law the way Nair practised medicine: in flat language, in the open, with the price list attached and no ornament on it, and at the end of it Okafor closed the phone and put it away and looked at the hospital lawyer with something that was almost, in its exact and unsentimental way, sympathy.

"The emergency hold is stayed," she said. "Fourteen days, pending the full hearing your client has been sequencing since the spring. The child remains with her parents. She will be discharged with her parents, when her physician discharges her, in the ordinary way of children. The registration stands incomplete, and the incompleteness is now a fact in a court file as well as a hospital one, which means your client's system is going to have to explain its blank to a judge instead of the other way around." She turned to the bed. "Beth. Brian — wake up, Dr. Kessler, you're a father and there's paperwork, which I gather is the whole of your new condition in one sentence. The first hour is over. You kept it. Everything from here is argument, and argument is my department, and I am, I want it in the open, *very good* at my department."

Brian had woken at *father* the way men woke at weather: all at once, and already standing. He looked at the baby, and at Beth, and at the lawyer in the doorway with her folder, and he did the thing he had done since the first morning in the tank room, the thing that had never once failed to be the truest help anyone could give her: he itemised the room, plainly, all of it, including the parts that did not flatter anyone.

"She's born," he said. "She's ours. The form can't hold her mother and the court can't have her first hour, because her first hour is *over*, and it was ours, and it's in the past where even Eastern can't file a correction to it." He sat down again, carefully, on the edge of the chair, a man who had somewhere else to be and had chosen, instead, to be here, unrecoverably, for the rest of his life. "What happens now?"

"Now," Okafor said, "they determine. It's what they do instead of weather. It will take as long as it takes, and it will be in rooms, and some of the rooms will have worse carpet than others. Go back to sleep, both of you. She has."

Down the corridor, on a desk nobody was sitting at, a cursor blinked in a species field, patient as a held breath, waiting for a word it had not earned.

The baby slept on her mother's chest, in the first unobserved place she had ever been that was also, entirely, observed — loved being the observation nobody's instrument could read — and Beth lay awake a while longer in the grey hour before the building woke, and did not reach for a status field, because there was no console in the room and no field that would have held her, and thought: *four hours in the world, and already no file anywhere holds you, and the one blank in the record is the truest sentence in it.*

It was not nothing. She had taught herself that, in the winter she had taken instead of the file: a thing did not have to be legible to be real. It only had to be *hers*, and going on.
