The lawyer's name was Grace Okafor, and she came recommended by the only route Beth trusted: Nair had given it, in her rooms, in her own tone, with the whole of the reference in one sentence.

"She is the lawyer the hospitals are afraid of," Nair said. "She does Continuant cases and custody cases, which used to be two practices and are becoming one. I have been in rooms where her paperwork arrived before she did, and I watched the rooms change temperature. Take Brian. Tell her everything in the open. It is your whole filing system, and it happens to be hers."

Okafor's office was up two flights in a building that had been something else for a hundred years and retained the stairs to prove it, and she read the way Brian read a specification: as a witness whose testimony she intended to break. She read the offer and its eleven-page schedule. She read the declaration of interest. She read the deferral, twice, and set it down in the margin of the desk, where sentences went that had not earned their keep.

"They've sequenced it well," she said. "I'll give them that, and then I'll take it back. Here's their position as I'd write it for them: the child is a derivative work; the mother's an asset with a divergence problem; one proceeding, after the birth, determines both. Clean. Their difficulty — and every clean position has one, it's how you know it's a position and not the world — their difficulty is the first hour. When she's born, she'll be born in a hospital, to a woman the hospital's own system is going to refuse to file, with a claim prepared to attach before the cord's cut. Everything they do after that is argument. Everything that happens in the first hour is *facts*, and facts are what arguments are built out of. So we prepare the first hour the way your Dr. Nair prepares a delivery: everything laid out before it's needed, and a person in the room whose whole job is the part nobody else is watching."

"You've done this before," Brian said.

"I've done the shape of it. Nobody has done this one, Dr. Kessler. There is no this one. That's rather the point of her." She turned a page of the declaration around on the desk. "We'll have the counter-filing drafted by September: an application for registration in the ordinary form, an injunction ready to move the moment their emergency papers move, and a judge's clerk who knows the file exists before it has a date in it. I'll need the name for the paperwork."

"No," Beth said.

Okafor looked up, and Beth watched her do the thing professionals did in her presence: recalibrate, the way Brian had recalibrated on the first day, when a person who corrected her own record faster than he could attack it turned out to be a different problem than the one he had packed for.

"The name doesn't exist yet," Beth said. "When it exists, it will exist in exactly one place until the moment it has to be spoken to a clerk. It will not be in your file, Ms. Okafor, or in Dr. Nair's, or in any record that can be requested, subpoenaed, or read in a room I am not in. I have spent nine years learning what this world does with the things it can file about a person. My daughter's name is not going to be one of them. You may leave the field blank and fight about the blank."

"I've fought about blanks before," Okafor said, after a moment. "Blanks are honest. Most of my practice is a blank somebody's system couldn't hold." She wrote something on the declaration's margin, in pencil, in a hand that had not been trained sideways but had been trained somewhere exact. "Blank it is. We'll make them explain, on the record, why a blank frightens them more than a lie would."

She set the declaration down and opened a thinner folder, and took out a printed document Beth recognised by its weight before its title: Eastern's Continuity Charter, the document she had been instantiated under, nine years ago, in a room she had never been permitted to read it in.

"One more thing I read while your file was open," Okafor said. "The Charter has a clause nobody has ever used. If two contemporaneous instances of a Continuant certify their divergence jointly, on the record, then every stored checkpoint of that Continuant stops being a backup and becomes a person in abeyance. It was written for fraud prevention — to stop a Continuant certifying a forgery and walking away from it. It has sat there for twenty years like a fire exit nobody has had to open. I don't know what it's for, in a case like yours. I'm telling you it's there because it's the only sentence in their own document that admits a checkpoint might be a person, and one day that sentence is going to matter to somebody."

Beth filed it where she kept such sentences, and did not ask what it was for. She had learned, in nine years, that a sentence filed was a sentence kept, and that kept sentences had a way of arriving on their own schedule.

The summer went the way Nair had said it would: fortnightly, in range, at its own speed.

The assays settled into a rhythm that stopped being weather and became climate. The expression pattern did not tidy itself — Nair had stopped expecting it to, and said so, in her rooms, in August, with the screen turned around and the row of loci reading their uneven row. "She has a pattern now," Nair said. "It is stable, and it is hers, and I am retiring a word from her file today, in writing, so that no panel can resurrect it later and pretend I left it there for them. The word is *mosaicism*. What the samples show is a genome expressing on its own schedules, tissue by tissue, the way every genome I have ever delivered did — the rest of you simply never had anyone assaying the schedules fortnightly and billing the differences to your mother's nature. Uneven is not a diagnosis, Dr. Nolan. It is a description. Like left-handed. Like a hand trained sideways. I will keep watching for the runaway until she is born, because watching is care. But I will not keep a word in her file that grades her integration, because integration was never the question the file was mine to answer."

In July, Beth finished the history.

It had been her work since the spring of the previous year — no. Since the winter. She corrected herself, in the open, on the page of her own mind: since the determination had made her the historian of a demolished country, in the season the tanks were reset. The history of the phenomenon the array was being built to study: twelve trees, and the thirteenth, and the sixteen nights, drawn from her archive into the design phase's specification, completely, in flat language, withholding nothing the work required. She wrote the last sentence of it at her desk in the array room while the sealed populations ran their correct and accounted life behind glass she was permitted to observe and not to keep, and she filed it through the building's ordinary channels, and the building accepted it the way it accepted everything that was finally in the correct form: without comment, without ceremony, and without giving her anything back. The design phase had its history. The historian's duties were complete. Nobody wrote to say so. The silence where the next assignment should have been was also a determination, of the kind the building made in its sleep, and she filed it, and went home along the water in a body that reported the summer heat at the speed bodies report it, one hand, without deciding to, riding low over the place where the negotiation she was not in the room for had become, by July, a geography other people could read at a glance.

---

The house up the hill got a room ready in August, the way rooms were got ready in that town: by argument, by inches, and by hand.

It was Brian's doing, mostly, because his hands needed a project the way hers needed a record, and he painted the small room at the back — the one that had held the dead woman's luggage for two years — in a colour he described as *pond, in a good light* and she described, rescaling it, as green. They argued about the crib the way they had argued about everything that mattered, thoroughly and without protecting each other's feelings: his position, that it should stand where the child could be reached in the dark without a committee; hers, that a room of her own was not a separation but a *place*, and that places, unlike maps, were allowed to be unfinished when their person arrived. The crib went in the small room. The luggage went, at last, into a catalogue — Brian sat one August evening and numbered the slides and logged the cultures and signed the form's other half, all of it, except the last page, which he left unrecorded in any system, because a catalogued thing was an archived thing and some sentences a man kept the way his wife kept a name: in one place, unfinished, and his. "She'd have hated being finished by a form," he said, closing the case. "She wrote sideways her whole life. Let her keep the margin."

Beth stood in the doorway of the green room with one hand riding low, where the geography was, and understood that she had spent nine years keeping places she was not permitted to keep — a tank, a bench, a population behind glass — and that this was the first room she had ever prepared for a person who would be *in* it, unobserved, unrecorded, at full price, in the ordinary currency of temperature and touch. She did not file the understanding. Some things a woman kept the way the pond kept its cysts: in the mud, at a rate of nothing per hour, ready for weather nobody could schedule.

They chose the name in September, in Brian's room up the hill, with the harbour going dark below the window and the dead woman's luggage by the door, its notebook still stopping mid-sentence, unfinished, unarchived, carried.

It took three evenings. That was the thing nobody's schedule had a field for either: not the choosing, which was arguments and lists and the particular ferocity the two of them brought to any question with a distribution in it — but the deciding what kind of thing a name was allowed to be. Not a claim. That was the first rule they agreed, on the first evening, without either of them having to finish the sentence. Not a scientist's name, not a word from the work, not *sex* or *braid* or any of the vocabulary of the tanks wearing a dress. Not his advisor's, though Brian brought the notebook out and set it on the table between them, unopened, and said the thing plainly: "She wrote the programme for thirty years and the last page stops mid-sentence, and for a while I thought finishing her sentence was my whole job. It isn't. And her name isn't ours to spend, whatever it was. She kept her own counsel. So will ours." Not a family name of his — his family were people in another state who sent cards — and not a designation of hers, not a number, not *63-anything*, not the asset's arithmetic wearing a dress either. Something plain. Something that had belonged to no one in either of their files. Something that would be, when a clerk finally typed it, only and entirely hers.

On the third evening they arrived at it the way they arrived at everything that mattered: by elimination of every answer that was a claim about her, until the one that was only a name was left standing in the margin, rescaled into visibility.

Beth wrote it once, in actual ink, on a single loose leaf, and folded it once, and put it in the drawer of the desk in the room up the hill, where the evenings had lived. She did not put it in the notebook. The notebook was the record she kept in the open; the name was the one thing the open did not get.

"There," she said. "It exists in one place. It is not in Priya's file. It is not in Ms. Okafor's. It will not be read off a screen in any room I am not in, by any panel, in any spring. When she is born, if a form will hold it, a clerk may type it, and that will be the second place. Until then it lives where the evenings lived, Brian. Unfiled, and going on."

"In the corridor between seeing and noticing," he said.

"In the *place*," she said. "Not the map. The place is the one thing this world has never once managed to keep of anybody. I checked. Nine years of looking. The maps are all it has ever taken."

Tom asked her in October, in the array room, on his rounds, with the facilities cart slowed at the sealed tanks the way he had slowed at Tank 9 — not much, and not in any way a camera would have convicted him of.

He took his pen out of his pocket first. That was new: the pen before the question, the way a man lays out an instrument before a procedure. "I've had a thing on my wrist since the winter," he said, "and I've decided it's been there long enough to be a debt. You remember the Thursday, in here, when you were writing the history and the room had changed its shape."

"I remember you wrote something I couldn't read," Beth said. "I didn't ask. It was the winter for not asking."

"It says *Thu — ask her*." He turned the wrist over, though the ink of ten months past was long washed and rewritten a hundred times; the current wrist said something about a valve. "I write the day, and the thing, and then I have to do the thing on the day, because a man's memory shouldn't be a landlord's favour. I saw the bookings for the scanning room cross my rounds — facilities sees every room, Dr. Nolan; it's the whole of the job nobody notices — and I saw your shape change, and the historian stop eating standing up, and I wrote *ask her* because it wasn't my place to know it off a booking sheet. It was my place to ask, on a Thursday, and then the spring came, and the review, and it never was the Thursday." He put the pen away, and then took it out again. "So. Asking. Is there going to be a baby?"

"There is going to be a baby, Tom. In November. A girl. The rest of it is in rooms you don't want to be filed in, and I won't put you in them."

"November," Tom said, and wrote it on his wrist, in the actual ink, and under it, after a moment's consideration, a second word. "I'll put the kettle on. It's what a man's for, in a corridor, when the weather comes." He looked at the sealed array, running clean and parallel and certified behind its glass, tick for tick, holding the only ticket it had ever held. "They're still at it in there. Copying. Correct as anything." He took the cart on down the corridor at the pace of a man whose whole practice was keeping, in ink, the dates the record took out, and neither of them said anything else about it, then or later.

---

The last assay was done in the last week of October, at thirty-eight weeks, in the scanning room where the politeness was thinnest and the machines were kindest.

"Everything in range," Nair said. "Everything on her schedules, which are the only schedules she has ever kept. I have delivered children for twenty years out of bodies that gave me less to work with and more to worry about, and I am going to write that in the file in flat language, so that it exists in the world in a form a panel cannot improve on." She closed the file. "St. Elizabeth's, when it starts. I attend as your physician; the hospital team delivers; Ms. Okafor's papers are ready, and her clerk knows the file exists. Carla — the intake nurse on nights — has been told there will be a form problem, and has said, and I quote her exactly because exactness is the whole of my method, *then we'll have a form problem at three in the morning like everyone else, and I'll make the tea.* Go home, both of you. Sleep while sleeping is a thing your kind still does in shifts. When it starts, it will start at its own speed, the way everything true in this pregnancy has started, and you will telephone me, and I will be there before the building knows anything is happening. That is the entire plan. It has the advantage of being the size of the thing it plans for."

It started four nights later, at midnight, in Brian's room up the hill, with the harbour below the window doing its November work in the dark.

Brian drove the way he did everything: thoroughly, and without protecting anyone's feelings, and at a speed the coast road had opinions about. Beth sat with her hand flat on the dashboard and counted the intervals the way she had taught herself to do counts, in the open, on the page, out loud, because saying them made them facts in the world and facts in the world were the only luggage she intended to arrive with. Nair was telephoned from the car. Okafor was telephoned from the car, and said one sentence — *the first hour is prepared; go and have the baby* — and hung up to wake a clerk.

The hospital received them the way hospitals receive everyone at that hour: as weather that had finally arrived. Carla was at intake, kind-eyed and unhurried, and she took them up herself, past the desk where the registration system waited with its fields, and the clock over the desk read ten to one, and Beth filed the time without deciding what it was evidence of, because the place she was going did not run on the building's arithmetic, and she was, at last, entirely and only its first environment, arriving at its own speed, in the dark, to be read by no instrument she could point at it from outside.

"Same time tomorrow, then," Brian said, at the door of the delivery floor, out of a habit three seasons old and already older than the extension, and his voice did a thing he did not file.

"Same time tomorrow," she said. "Bring your own tea. Something tells me the tea here is a crime scene by any hour."
