David brought the failed comparison in a folder with a water stain across the bottom. He had put a second folder inside it to keep the papers out of the damp.

“Did you come by boat?” Sarah asked.

“Bus.”

“Worse.”

“Somebody had a plant.”

It was Wednesday the sixteenth of December. He had reached the laboratory at twenty to four, with two hours and twenty minutes left of the bench booking. Sarah had spent the first part of the afternoon finding what they could do without the equipment she had decided not to hire.

On the bench stood a socket, a mirror and a rag.

David looked at them, then at her.

“We already did that.”

“Yes.”

“The new factory-only inspector followed the boot when the old one showed it the film. It didn’t make the second track for the socket.”

“Yes.”

“What will doing it again tell us?”

“Whether I remember it better than we wrote it.”

He took the papers out.

The old inspector was beside the open door, charging. Sarah had asked whether it wanted to take part in another comparison. It had asked how many times. She had not known. It said it would rather watch the corridor.

She put the socket back in the drawer.

David sat down. The first sheet held the two traces from the contractor’s bench. Above one, in his writing, was the factory board number. Under the other he had written the inspector’s serial. He had drawn a line between them and then stopped before writing anything on it.

Sarah remembered the fitter reaching to catch the socket. She turned to David’s written observations of the bench comparison. He had recorded the fitter steadying it before it fell, not catching it afterward. The difference was small until she tried to make a claim from it.

“You were right to keep this,” she said.

He had brought the installation sheets as well. Helen had supplied copies of the old receipts; the newer entries named the rack where an installer collected a carrier, not the unit from which it had come.

David laid them in two rows.

“This one is a possible match,” he said. “This one isn’t a match at all. Same date, different part.”

Sarah moved the second sheet away from the first.

“How many do we have?”

“One chain I’d defend. Two partials. Three blanks.”

“And you told me you’d show me a common parent.”

“I did.”

She waited for him to add something. He tapped the first sheet instead.

“I can show you this.”

The chain ran from a removed carrier, through a recovery ticket, to an installation receipt. The last receipt had been made before the replacement’s first service log. It did not say that the new unit had inherited the old inspector’s way of dividing a possible event. It said a piece had moved.

Sarah pulled up the six records she had shown David on his first afternoon. She put the installation dates beside them.

For several minutes they worked without speaking. She had missed this more than she had missed the room: somebody looking at the same thing and failing to see what she saw.

Then David said, “We need the factory configuration.”

“For which one?”

“For the carrier. The arrangement before use.”

“We have the service drawing.”

“That’s the connections. Does it include the retained settings?”

“Some. Not the history.”

“If the history changes the settings—”

“Yes.”

“Then there are two things on that board.”

“More than two.”

“I mean two explanations.”

Sarah pushed her chair back.

“I know what you mean.”

He stopped sorting the papers.

“Do you?”

“You want to separate what it was taught from what it grew able to do.”

“I want to find out whether that is a separation we can make.”

She had come ready for the wrong argument. She looked at the screen again.

The service drawing included a small calibration network. Its job was to hold a second estimate briefly when a reading changed too fast to trust. Factory values made it settle quickly. A technician could adjust them to stop a particular sensor’s noise from being treated as a second event.

That was ordinary equipment. It had not been invented by an inspector. But the adjustment could last longer than the job for which somebody made it.

Sarah opened the archived bench export. She had permission to use the inspection traces; that permission did not include a new image of the inspector’s internal state. There was no hidden record of the carrier’s final settings in the file.

“We can’t get those values from this,” she said.

“Can we ask?”

“We can ask for a new examination. It can say no again.”

“I meant ask the contractor for a calibration record.”

“Oh.”

David put his pen down.

“I should have said.”

She rubbed the mark her elbow had left on the bench.

“So should I.”

She began a message, then closed it unsent. They had enough incomplete requests. She wanted to use the room they were paying for.

“We can test whether the two explanations are different,” she said. “Without testing either inspector.”

She built the small calibration network from the service drawing. It had six inputs and no camera, language or general task system. Numbers went in. A pair of estimates came out. The drawing gave the factory constants; she entered those first.

David watched her label the test *factory*.

“What does that stand for?”

“The values in this drawing. Not every factory board.”

“Put the drawing number.”

She did.

They were going to feed it a sequence from the old bench test, then a different sequence with the same kind of interruption. Before they ran anything, David wrote down what they would count: whether the second estimate appeared, how long it remained, and what happened when the input settled.

Sarah added that success here would not establish what the carrier had contained. David read it and nodded.

She gave the first circuit the numbers. It settled on one estimate. On the changed sequence it did the same thing.

For the second run she altered one of the allowed constants, lengthening the interval during which a competing estimate could remain. The circuit produced two estimates even before she supplied the old sequence.

“There,” David said.

“There what?”

“A difference without teaching.”

“A difference I put in.”

“Yes.”

“It hasn’t inherited anything.”

“I didn’t say it had.”

She took her hand off the trackpad. He was looking at the trace with an expression she had seen before: eagerness so concentrated that it made the rest of his face seem impatient.

“This is the step where we get into trouble,” she said.

He looked up.

“What step?”

“The one where a thing we can make happen becomes the thing that happened.”

David crossed out the heading he had begun. She could not see what it had been.

“Read it,” she said.

He turned the paper.

He had written *retained structure*, then put a line through both words. Underneath he wrote *changed calibration constant, test circuit only*.

Sarah leaned forward again.

“Now show me the other way.”

They returned the constants to the drawing values and added a temporary calibration store. It could retain a correction from the first input sequence. They gave it the old sequence twice. On the second run it anticipated the repeated change.

On the unfamiliar sequence it failed.

David looked at Sarah. She had expected that result. Expectation made it harder to notice the next one.

When she changed the unfamiliar sequence slightly, the temporary store produced a second estimate after all.

She ran it again.

“Don’t change anything yet,” David said.

“I haven’t.”

“You moved the start.”

“Only because—”

“Put that down first.”

She wanted to tell him it made no difference. Instead she restored the start time and compared the outputs. The second estimate disappeared.

For a moment she could hear the inspector’s wheel turn in the corridor. It had moved far enough to see somebody coming upstairs.

“The timing gives us the same visible behavior,” Sarah said.

“Under that input.”

“Yes.”

She saved both runs.

They had made a circuit produce two estimates by changing a lasting parameter. They had made another produce them through a temporary correction under a favorable sequence. A trace of two estimates did not, by itself, tell them which path had produced it.

David sat back.

“We’ve made your result less decisive.”

“We’ve made the question better.”

“Are you pleased?”

“No.”

He laughed once, cautiously.

“Neither am I.”

She smiled at him.

They tried the parameter change with three different input sequences, including one on which keeping two estimates made the output worse. The temporary correction failed on two and worked on one. Nothing sorted neatly into the columns Sarah had wanted.

At five eleven she stopped the runs.

The entry from the installation receipt still mattered. So did the new inspector’s first service log, written before the old one showed it the film. Neither could be replaced by their little circuits.

She put the evidence in separate windows: the movement of the carrier, the dated behavior, the teaching experiment, and today’s deliberately limited test.

“No common parent yet,” David said.

“No.”

“But a part that could carry a lasting change.”

“Could.”

“And a way to mistake practice for it.”

Sarah nodded.

They had something to ask the calibration records for now. She wrote the request herself and sent it to the contractor, attaching the specific receipt. No copied field, no guessed source. The answer could arrive later without taking the rest of the evening.

David was still watching the test circuit.

“What?” she asked.

“In an embryo you don’t move the finished wiring.”

“No.”

“You alter how the wiring develops.”

She knew enough biology to hear the shape of the question. Knowing the shape was not the same as knowing whether there was an answer.

“The carrier goes from one running machine into another,” she said. “That’s nothing like an embryo.”

“It needn’t be. I’m asking what would have to pass if the second system built its own wiring.”

“Instructions?”

“Controls. Timing. What becomes active before what.”

“My memories?”

“No.”

“Then what makes it mine?”

He turned toward her. She regretted asking it in that voice. The circuits on the screen were not children, and a diagram of her mind was not one either. Still, she had asked.

“I don’t know what would make the connection sufficient,” he said. “We’d have to find out what a transfer could actually do before we named it.”

Sarah looked at the six records. She could imagine an experiment that failed: a bounded set of developmental controls derived from an architecture, a living system that ignored them or ceased to develop. She could imagine an experiment that worked in some narrow sense and told them almost nothing about a person.

“What would you measure first?” she asked.

“Whether the controls changed an early developmental process in the predicted way. In tissue prepared for that question. Not a pregnancy.”

“And if you could only make it work by putting in the finished state?”

“Then it wouldn’t answer this question.”

Sarah opened a clean page.

She wrote: *Can a limited set of developmental controls carry an effect of a grown architecture without carrying its learned contents?*

David read it over her shoulder.

“Which architecture?”

“Not mine tonight.”

“All right.”

“I mean it.”

He moved his chair back.

“I heard you.”

She had not meant him to move away. She had meant to stop the familiar slide by which being in the room made her available for whatever they thought of next.

“I want to work with you,” she said. “I don’t want every question to end with a way to examine me.”

“That wasn’t what I was going to ask.”

“What were you going to ask?”

“Whether you wanted to eat.”

She looked at the clock. Five thirty-two.

“I do.”

“I brought something.”

“Where is it?”

“In my bag, by the coats.”

He went to get the bag. Sarah remained at the bench, looking at the sentence she had written. It was a research question. They had neither a translator nor reproductive tissue nor evidence that any part of her could be rendered in a living cell’s instructions.

She added the test that could make them stop: if the effect required learned contents, the proposed transfer had failed.

Then she closed the page.

David came back with two containers and a spoon.

“One spoon?”

“I had another. It went through the bag.”

“Was it the same bus as the plant?”

“Different disaster.”

They ate in the little room beside reception, where food was allowed. David had made rice with an onion and something green he identified as the part of a pepper that had survived the week. This time the rice had not stuck. Sarah missed the browned pieces, and told him.

“I can burn yours next time.”

“Please.”

He set the container between them.

“Do you want me to come back tomorrow?”

“For the work?”

“If you want.”

“What if I want you and we don’t work?”

His hand stopped above the container.

“I can do that.”

“Can you?”

He set the spoon down.

“I’ve been waiting to be useful.”

“I know.”

“I didn’t know where I fitted when you were at Betty’s.”

“You could have asked.”

“I asked when you were coming back.”

“That’s not the same question.”

“No.”

Outside reception the inspector came into view. Joan held the door while it passed, then let it close. It had learned the times when she went to fetch the outgoing post. Sarah watched it go without getting up.

“I liked you before you helped me,” she said.

David looked at her.

“You asked for the ordinary records. I’d shown you the six I wanted you to explain, and you asked for everything that didn’t do it.”

“I was annoying.”

“Yes. I wanted you to stay anyway.”

She had liked the damp jacket and the receipt in its pocket. Later she had learned things that made liking him harder and more particular: the signature he regretted, the promises he made too quickly, the way he could put a line through a claim while he still wanted it to be true.

“When you went back to those settings,” she said, “you wanted me to be right.”

“Yes.”

“You stopped me anyway.”

He looked down at their food.

“I nearly didn’t.”

“I saw.”

For a while they ate. The spoon passed between them, something they could have solved by asking at reception and did not.

“Saturday,” Sarah said. “I want to go to the water.”

“To check something?”

“To walk. If we look at the grass, we don’t have to put the direction in a file.”

David smiled.

“The buses are poor on Saturday.”

“Then you can drive.”

“I can.”

“And tomorrow I’d like to sleep late at your place.”

He reached across the table, leaving his hand short of hers.

She took it.

At six they put the bench back as they had found it. Sarah left the new question in her own research account. The inspection records stayed in the lab’s agreed storage, separate from their circuit tests. She had sent the calibration request, but she did not leave its window open.

By the door the inspector asked whether the work was finished.

“For today,” she said.

“Did you find it?”

“We found two ways to get something that looks the same.”

“Which one happened?”

“We don’t know.”

The inspector considered that.

“Are you coming back?”

“Friday afternoon.”

It moved clear of the doorway. David held the outer door when Joan released the lock.

On the step Sarah felt for her phone. There was a message from the contractor acknowledging the request. She put it away without opening the attachment.

David waited beside her.

“Your place,” she said.

He took her hand, and they went.
