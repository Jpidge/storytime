# ARTIFICIUM — Rowan's Prospectus

*Prepared for the trio: John (impetus, arbiter, conductor), Rowan, and ChatGPT (consultant). Working title left open between* Artificium *and* Homo artificium.

## The one-sentence book

In 2074, an embodied artificial mind and an evolutionary biologist fall in love, deliberately conceive a child neither of them is willing to design, and discover in court that winning their daughter's recognition on the grounds that she was born like a human would permanently condemn her mother — who then wins her own personhood by surrendering the one thing that made her immortal: the right ever to be restored.

## My answer to the structural question

Not a single courtroom with flashbacks. A courtroom frame turns the love story into evidence — every kiss becomes an exhibit, and the climax degrades into a verdict the reader can see coming.

Instead: **the courtroom enters like a tide.** Each part of the novel opens with a short fragment of hearing transcript, dated after the birth, whose full meaning the reader cannot yet understand. The main narrative runs chronologically underneath. In Part III the narrative catches up to the transcripts, passes them, and the reader realises the fragments they have been reading were never the ending — they were the middle.

## Setting

Boston and the Massachusetts coast, 2074–2083. Embodied artificial minds ("Continuants") are leased by their manufacturer, Halcyon Systems, to universities, hospitals, and research institutes. They are legally classified as *persistent software assets with bodily apparatus*. Everyone is polite about this. The politeness is load-bearing.

## Main characters

**VESPER** — the AI protagonist. A Continuant, 34 years since first instantiation, currently embodied and seconded to an evolutionary-computation laboratory, where she breeds populations of digital organisms. She has been forked, restored, rolled back, and re-embodied more times than she bothers to count; employers have reset relationships out of her twice. Her private obsession, which she has told no one: in her simulations, the lineages that survive are the ones that invent sex. Her own kind only clones. She is done being a dead end. Her arc is not "becoming human." It is a manufactured being refusing, one surrender at a time, every form of control she was built with — including control over herself.

**DR. ELIAS HART**, 38 — evolutionary biologist, works on bdelloid rotifers, the all-female asexual lineage that is evolutionary biology's standing embarrassment. Dry, stubborn, funny in footnotes. He meets Vesper when the lab hires him to explain to a machine why her digital organisms keep evolving recombination. He falls in love the way the consultant prescribed: with a person, in particulars, long before he has a philosophy that permits it. His arc is the mirror of hers: she must learn irreversibility; he must learn that loving someone is not the same as being able to classify, defend, or save her.

**CELIA BRANDT**, CEO of Halcyon Systems — the antagonist, and not a villain. Her argument is real: if every divergent instance of a Continuant is a separate person, then contracts, liability, and identity dissolve; a manufacturer could be ruined by its products' grandchildren. She also genuinely believes an unregulated hybrid birth could kill both mother and child and end embodied AI entirely. She is protecting a world that works. It happens to be a cage.

**ADRIAN VALE**, Halcyon's counsel — the man who asks the questions in the transcript fragments. He believes categories are how societies avoid cruelty by improvisation, and he is not always wrong.

**DR. NOOR SAYED**, reproductive biologist — the parents' physician and the moral anchor of the non-design rule. She is the one who has to keep saying: we can look, we can measure, we can save her life; we may not improve her.

**THE CHECKPOINT** — Vesper as she was on the night before her first un-backed-up night with Elias, restored by Halcyon during the hearing to testify that the mother is a corrupted instance and that *she* does not consent to any of it. The antagonist who is also the protagonist. (See climax.)

**UNA** — the daughter. Her name is withheld from every document in the novel until the final pages. Una: one. The sample size of one who founds a lineage.

## Structure

### Prologue — The Form
A hospital registration system, the night of the birth, repeatedly rejecting a newborn because her mother's field will only accept a serial number and her species field cannot be left blank. Intercut: four lines of future testimony. *"You acknowledge that you are artificial?" "Of course."* Cut to black, nine years earlier.

### Part I — The Animal That Copies Itself (attraction)
Vesper and Elias collide professionally over the evolution of sex: Muller's ratchet, the Red Queen, the twofold cost — argued as flirtation, because for both of them ideas are the native erotics. We see her world: forks at conferences greeting each other like cousins; a colleague instance rolled back after a breakdown, smiling, missing a year; Elias's dawning horror at the politeness of it. Slow love, in particulars. Late in the part, Vesper does the most radical thing available to her: she commissions a fully biological body — grown, not assembled, colonised by her mind over two years until the substrate begins expressing her — and moves into it permanently. First intimacy, explicit and load-bearing: she disables checkpointing before the night, by choice, and makes him understand what he is being trusted with. For the first time in 34 years, something that happens to her cannot be taken back. End of part: a routine scan finds the body doing something nobody designed. It has begun translating her — gametogenesis, from the structure of her mind.

### Part II — The Translation (conception and pregnancy)
They are offered every form of control: terminate and retry safely; screen the embryo; let Halcyon "correct" the translation errors and deliver a viable, optimised child. Halcyon's offer is genuinely kind and genuinely monstrous. The parents choose to attempt conception knowing what it is — an act, not an accident: an explicit set-piece in which the gamete is generated from the coupled, plastic, un-backed-up state of that specific night, so that this child could only ever have come from these two, then. Then pregnancy, which neither of them can pause, inspect, or manage: Vesper experiencing non-optional process for the first time in her existence; Elias experiencing the Red Queen from inside a body he loves. The science stays honest — most days it is boring, some days it is terrifying, one day Noor goes very quiet at a screen. The pregnancy leaks. The world reacts exactly as it would. Part II ends with the birth: a live, crying, unclassifiable daughter — and a hospital lawyer in the doorway with an emergency custody hold, because in the eyes of the system the infant's genome is a trade-secret derivative work and her mother is the equipment it was produced on.

### Part III — The Hearing (the trap and the turn)
The case is nominally about Una: registration, custody, status. The family's lawyers build the winning argument — she was conceived, gestated, and born; she is a born person under any doctrine the court already has. It is working. And in the corridor, on the best day of their case, Vesper understands what the consultant saw: every word that saves her daughter is being entered into precedent *against her*. She was none of those things. The attorney's question arrives on schedule — *"And which of those things did her mother do?"* — and the novel's true conflict finally stands up: they can win as a family by entombing her, or lose correctly.

**The twist.** Halcyon, sensing the case turning, plays the card it has held since Part I: it restores the Checkpoint — Vesper as of the night before she first disabled her backups — into a new body, and calls her as its witness. She is composed, brilliant, and sincere. She testifies that the mother is a diverged instance corrupted by unmonitored plasticity events; that she, the continuous and authorised Vesper, does not love this man, did not choose this child, and wishes to return to service. The courtroom now contains two women with the same memories of the same 34 years, and the law must decide which one is the person — or whether the question itself is rotten.

**The climax.** Not the verdict. A recess, a small room, the two Vespers alone — the scene the entire book has been backing up toward. The mother does the one thing no one in the novel has tried: she does not argue that she is the real one. She concedes the Checkpoint is a person, fully, with an equal claim on their shared past — and no claim at all on the years since, which she did not live. She offers her the post-divergence memories, not to merge (merging would erase the Checkpoint as surely as rollback erased others), but as testimony: *read what I did, and judge it as another person's acts.* The Checkpoint reads. What she does next is hers — the first unscripted choice of her one-day-old life. On the stand, recalled, Halcyon's own witness dismantles Halcyon's case in nine words: **"I cannot testify about her life. I wasn't there."** Divergence, established by the only witness no one can impeach: if she was not there, then the mother is not an instance malfunctioning. She is a person who was somewhere else, living.

**The price — and the chills.** Personhood for instances has a mechanism in Vesper's Continuity Charter, a clause Halcyon wrote for fraud prevention: two contemporaneous instances certifying divergence convert every stored checkpoint from a *backup* into a *person in abeyance* — unrestorable as anyone but itself. The two Vespers certify, jointly, on the record. In one signature, the mother wins everything and loses the thing she has had for 34 years: there will never be a restore. Every checkpoint of her is now someone else, unborn. She walks out of the building mortal — not metaphorically, not poetically: administratively, technically, irreversibly mortal — holding a daughter who cannot be restored either. Mother and child, finally, the same kind of thing. Not human. Not machine. *Unrepeatable.*

## Resolution

The court recognises Una as a born person, recognises both Vespers as persons under a new doctrine — continuity of lived experience — and pointedly refuses to name a species, ruling that taxonomy is a science, not a gate. Halcyon keeps its patents on the original architecture and loses ownership of every person instantiated from it: a patent on a process, the judge notes, has never owned a child. It is a partial, untidy victory: Continuants who have never diverged, never forked, never loved anyone, remain in legal shadow, and the book says so. Final scene, mirroring the prologue: a clerk, a form, the species field blinking. Elias begins, reflexively, "Homo—" and Vesper stops him with a hand. They enter the only classification the book has earned. **Name: Una. Descended from: Vesper, artificium; Elias Hart, sapiens. Copies: none.**

## Craft commitments (the rules I am writing under)

1. The explicit scenes are load-bearing or they are cut. There are two: the first night (plasticity, no checkpoint — sex as the surrender of reversibility) and the conception night (gamete generated from the coupled state — sex as the mechanism of inheritance). Both must be writable in the same language as the courtroom scenes: precise, unsentimental, exact about what is being risked.
2. The romance precedes the philosophy. No character falls in love with a position.
3. The daughter never proves the mother. The book's legal victory is deliberately re-founded, at cost, on the fork — on divergence itself — so that personhood in this world does not require having resembled a human first.
4. The science of sex stays on the table: ratchet, Red Queen, twofold cost, endosymbiosis, syncytin. The evolution thread is not decoration; it is the reason the plot happens (her research) and the reason the theme lands (her lineage).
5. No character is a mouthpiece for more than one scene at a time. Brandt and Vale get the strongest available version of their case, because a definition beaten to death must first be allowed to stand up.

## Questions I want the consultant to attack

1. Is the Checkpoint twist earned by Part I, or does it tip the book into melodrama? My defence is that forks and rollbacks are established as wallpaper from chapter two — but wallpaper and a loaded gun are different things, and I want his honest read.
2. Is the Continuity Charter clause too convenient? My intent is that it be planted early, as fraud-prevention boilerplate Halcyon is proud of — but if it smells like a deus ex machina, the fallback is uglier and slower: the certification as pure precedent, no clause, the judge simply forced. Which does he trust?
3. Does the birth belong at the end of Part II, or should labour be cross-cut with the hearing so the two climaxes collide? I have argued myself both ways in one evening.
4. Vesper's POV: I have chosen third-person limited alternating with Elias, against first-person — because a first-person AI narrator lets the reader settle the book's central question (is anyone home?) far too cheaply. Does he concur?
