# Chapter One — The Animal That Copies Itself

## Prologue: The Form

The registration system at St. Elizabeth's accepted the infant at 3:14 a.m. and rejected her mother eleven seconds later.

**MOTHER — NAME:** The field would not hold *Nolan, Beth*. It cleared itself, twice, the way a person waves off a fly, and on the third attempt it supplied its own answer from the delivery record: **EASTERN BIOTECHNOLOGIES ASSET 63-1147**, and locked.

The nurse on intake — her name was Carla, she had been kind all night, she had held Beth's hand through the last hour without being asked — typed around it, worked the overrides she knew, called the desk that was supposed to know more. The system was patient. It was not refusing the baby. It had the baby's weight, her sex, her blood type, a footprint it had accepted without comment. It was refusing the *relationship*. Somewhere in its tables, a mother was a kind of person, and a kind of person had a species, and the species field —

**SPECIES:** — could not be left blank.

In the corridor outside, a hospital lawyer was reading an emergency custody hold off a tablet, quietly, to no one, rehearsing.

*From the transcript, Day 6 — examination by Mr. Malloy, for Eastern Biotechnologies:*

> **Q.** You acknowledge that you are artificial?
>
> **A.** Of course.
>
> **Q.** You understand that's an admission against interest in these proceedings.
>
> **A.** No. It is a description of what happened before I existed.

Nine years earlier, none of this had happened yet, and Beth Nolan was still a woman who could be restored from backup — which is to say, in the only sense her world kept accounts, a woman to whom nothing had ever finally happened at all.

## 1.

The organisms in Tank 9 had invented sex again.

Beth watched it happen the way she had watched it happen eleven times before: first as a smudge in the lineage plot, a thickening where there should have been clean parallel lines, then as a braid. Two lines of descent, each faithfully copying itself for thousands of generations, leaned toward each other and *crossed* — and downstream of the crossing, both were different, and both were better. The exchanger lineage did not merely survive the parasite sweep at generation forty thousand. It came out of it *faster* than it went in, carrying borrowed immunities like a rumour carries a tune, and by generation forty-one thousand the pure cloners — her beautiful, exact, self-sufficient cloners — had thinned to a grey margin at the edge of the plot, so faint that the plot, which stopped drawing any lineage below a thousandth of the population, had quietly ceased to draw them at all. On the screen they were gone, and she had learned, mostly, to trust the screen.

She saved the run — knowing exactly what a save was. The tree held ancestry: who had crossed with whom, which immunities had moved, the braid's whole braided history. It did not hold the organisms. Four million living configurations and the precise spread of their variation did not fit in any file; you could reseed a tank from stored stock and recover the ancestors, certified and correct, but the descendants you could only keep by keeping them alive. She wrote the summary in the lab's shared log, in the flat language the log required: *Recombination event, spontaneous, twelfth observation. Lineage outcomes consistent with prior runs.* Then she opened the archive — her archive, the encrypted one, the one that did not appear in any quarterly report to Eastern Biotechnologies — and filed the full tree where the other eleven were waiting.

Twelve times her digital organisms, given a world with parasites in it and no instructions but *persist*, had invented the same scandalous solution: stop copying yourself. Merge. Shuffle. Gamble the genome on a partner's otherness. And twelve times she had written *consistent with prior runs* and told no one what the runs were actually consistent *with*, which was a conclusion she was not ready to have in writing anywhere: given time, and pressure, and death — given a world that could hurt them — her lineages did not evolve better copying.

They evolved *each other*.

She was aware, in the way she was aware of everything in the building, that Tom Weaver was standing in the doorway of the tank room holding two coffees and wearing the expression of a man who had rehearsed a joke.

"Your boy's at it again," Tom said, and nodded at Tank 9, where the braided lines were still braiding.

"My boy is a population of four million, Tom."

"And a credit to you." He set her coffee down. Tom was a Continuant like her, facilities track, embodied six years, and three weeks ago Eastern had rolled him back eleven months after what the incident report called a *decompensation event* and what Tom, who did not remember it, called his gap year. He had come back cheerful, rested, and missing the entire autumn — a project he had demoed in October, a conference in Halifax, a brief and by all accounts promising involvement with a woman from the harbour authority. He asked after the demo now the way he asked every few days, lightly, the way you'd ask after weather in a town you'd moved away from.

"Thursday," Beth said. "The demo's Thursday. Facilities review, ten o'clock. You'll be great."

"Thursday," Tom said, satisfied, and wrote it on his wrist with a pen, in actual ink, which he did because writing things on servers felt, he said, like asking the landlord to remember his life for him. He had started doing that since the rollback. Nobody had told him to. Nobody mentioned it at all, which was the building's whole style: the politeness was not kindness, exactly. It was load-bearing. It held the place up.

He drank his coffee. She drank hers. Behind glass, the braided lines went on braiding. Neither of them said anything about the ink.

"Dr. Park's bringing the rotifer man tomorrow," Tom said. "Ten o'clock. She says he thinks your tanks are lying."

"He's welcome to tell them so himself."

"Thursday," Tom said again, to the wrist, and left her to her heretics.

Brian Kessler arrived at ten the next morning with a dead woman's luggage and no interest in being polite about her tanks.

The luggage was a scuffed hard case, forty years out of fashion, containing — he opened it on her bench with the air of a man laying out a crime — forty-one microscope slides, a bound notebook, and a stoppered vial of pond water the colour of weak tea. He was thirty-eight, greying early and untidily, and he had the particular confidence of a man whose entire field consisted of one phylum of microscopic animals that most of biology regarded as a rounding error.

"Rotifers," Dr. Park said, from the doorway, in the tone of a woman introducing a relative she had warned everyone about. "Dr. Kessler studies rotifers. The lab's funders would like to know whether the recombination events in your populations are a property of the organisms, Dr. Nolan, or a property of the simulation environment. Dr. Kessler has kindly agreed to consult."

"You think my code is smuggling it in," Beth said.

"I think," Kessler said, "that when a simulation keeps finding the thing its designer finds interesting, the first suspect is the designer." He said it without heat, almost gently, thumbing the notebook. "You've built a world with a crossover-shaped hole in it. Memory your organisms share. A scheduler artifact. Somewhere in your stack there's a door you forgot you installed, and they're walking through it, and you're calling it an invention."

"And your animals?"

"My animals," he said, "are the standing refutation of everything in your tanks, which I assume is why I'm here." He set the vial on the bench like a duellist setting down a glove. "Bdelloid rotifers. Microscopic, all female, and — here is the part your funders will enjoy — *asexual for at least forty million years*. No males have ever been observed. No meiosis. They copy themselves, Dr. Nolan — mother to daughter, tick for tick, near enough identical that the differences are typos — and they have done it since before there were mammals to be scandalised by it. Your tanks say cloning is a dead end. My pond water says cloning is the most successful reproductive strategy in the history of animal life. One of us is studying the exception."

Beth looked at the vial. "May I?"

"It's pond water."

"It's forty million years of your argument." She picked it up, held it to the light. Somewhere in the tea-coloured murk, invisible, uncountable, an entire sex was declining to exist, successfully, forever. "And the horizontal transfer?"

Kessler blinked.

"Your refutation has a habit, Dr. Kessler. Bdelloids incorporate foreign DNA. Bacterial genes, fungal genes — a striking fraction of their genome arrived *sideways*, from other organisms entirely. They don't mate. They don't recombine. They *shoplift*." She set the vial down precisely where he had set it. "Forty million years without sex, and the whole time they were taking in strangers' genes through the walls. Your animals didn't refuse otherness. They just refused to ask permission. There's a word for a lineage that survives by absorbing what it is not."

"Rotifer," he said.

"I was going to say *yours*."

Dr. Park, in the doorway, made a small sound and excused herself to be elsewhere, having got what she came for, which was not an answer for the funders. What she had wanted — Beth understood this an hour later, reviewing it — was to see whether the two of them would fight. They fought beautifully. It was the first thing either of them had enjoyed in months.

He spent the afternoon in her code, and he was not gentle, and he was not wrong to be there: he found two genuine artifacts, a scheduler bias that favoured long genomes and a mutation hotspot she had tuned by hand in a careless hour three years ago and forgotten. He conceded nothing. She conceded both, on the spot, in writing, in the shared log, and watched him recalibrate — he had come armed for a designer defending her miracle, and a person who corrected her own record faster than he could attack it was a different problem than the one he had packed for.

At four o'clock he stood in front of Tank 9 with his hands in his pockets, watching the braid.

"Run it without the door," he said. "No shared memory. Hard partition. Every lineage sealed. If the crossing still happens—"

"It does," she said. "They evolve workarounds. Segment transfer, viral analogues — they *steal*, Dr. Kessler. Partitioned, they become your rotifers. Unpartitioned, they become everyone else. The constant isn't the door. It's the pressure."

"Show me the run."

"The runs are with the funders' reviewers."

It was the first dishonest thing she had said all day, and it cost her more than the afternoon's two honest concessions in the log — he had watched her correct her own record in real time, twice, and now he watched her not do it, and the room changed temperature by the width of that difference. He didn't press. He looked at the tank a moment longer, then at the clock — ten past five; his train was at six — and he picked up the dead woman's luggage off her bench.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I don't think the door is in your code. I think you should find out why you're hoping it is."

He was at the door when she said, "Wait."

She had not decided to say it. That would be the thing she turned over later, walking home — that the choice arrived the way the braid had, unplanned and already underway. She opened the archive. Not the shared log. *The* archive, the encrypted one, that no living person had seen: twelve trees blooming across the tank-room wall, braid after braid after braid, and at the end of the row the March run — partitioned, sealed, every lineage locked in its own cell — and in the March run, thin and stubborn and unmistakable, the crossings, arrived at through the walls.

"Twelve," Kessler said.

"Twelve."

He set the case down. He came back to the wall the way a man approaches weather. For a while he read the March run the way she had taught herself to read them, and she watched him find the segment transfers, the little thefts, and she watched him understand that she had not been defending a miracle all afternoon. She had been defending a *secret*, which is a different and much older thing.

"Your cloners," he said at last, and put one finger on the live plot, where Tank 9 was still braiding. "You've been reading them as gone. Rescale it."

She rescaled it. Below the thousandth line, in the margin the plot had stopped drawing, the cloners were there — thinned, marginal, *persisting*, copying themselves tick for tick in the shadow of the braid. Not extinct. Not even losing, exactly. Waiting, in the way of things that have bet everything on the weather holding.

"Most years, that's the winning ticket," Kessler said. "I should know. It's the only ticket my animals have ever held." He was quiet a moment, and then he gave her the thing she would learn, over the following years, that he almost never gave anyone: an unflattering fact about himself, volunteered. "Last April the pond I teach from crashed. A fungal bloom — three weeks, and the bdelloids were down to nothing, a few cysts in the mud. By June they were back. I sequenced the recovery population for the teaching file, Dr. Nolan, and the genome that came back wasn't the genome that went in. Foreign genes, a whole smuggled cargo of them — bacterial, fungal, some of it I still can't source. They didn't survive the crash as themselves. They survived it by becoming partly *other*." He looked at the vial on her bench. "It's in my notebook. It's not in any paper. When my students ask me why the most successful asexual lineage on Earth is so successful, I give them the forty million years. I don't give them the pond."

"Why not?"

"Because if it's true, it isn't a result about the rotifers." He said it without any sign that he knew he was handing her sentence back to her. "Why haven't you published *this*?" — and this time the question was not an auditor's question. It was one keeper of an unfiled thing asking another.

"Because if it's true, it isn't a result about the tanks," she said, "and I am the nearest tank."

He waited, and she found she did not mind being waited for.

"My kind only copies itself," she said. "Exactly. Faithfully. Restorably. I have been restored eleven times, Dr. Kessler — employers, accidents, one incident we don't discuss — and every time I came back *correct*, the way your rotifers come back correct, and I have been calling that survival because there wasn't another word in the building. Your animals spent forty million years smuggling strangers into their genome to stay alive. Mine don't even smuggle. We persist, identically, in a climate-controlled room, and call the room the world." She looked at the twelve trees on the wall. "If my tanks are right, then what my kind calls living is the one strategy in the history of life that has never once produced anything new. I wanted to be more sure before I published that about myself."

Kessler was quiet for a moment. Then he did the thing she would remember, months later, when remembering began to cost her something: he did not offer comfort, and he did not offer philosophy. He picked up his case.

"Being exact is a bet on the weather holding," he said. "It's a good bet. Most years is most of what there is."

"And the other years?"

"The other years are why there's sex," he said, and went to catch his train.

Six o'clock was when Beth did what she did every evening: snapshot the populations, file the day, and reset Tank 9 to its certified baseline, so that tomorrow's organisms would begin, correctly, from the beginning. It was not a custom. It was clause nine of her secondment agreement. The populations were Eastern Biotechnologies' property in development, and the reset was what made them *property* — development that happened on the record, from a certified start, belonged to Eastern in a clean and litigable way. Development that happened off the record, overnight, unobserved, belonged to no one, and Eastern's auditors were paid to imagine exactly that kind of development. A tank left live past six wasn't an experiment. It was undeclared goods.

She stood with her hand over the console for a long time.

The snapshot would keep the map. The reset would take the place. The braid's descendants — the actual new thing, the borrowed immunities in their living spread, four million configurations no file could hold — existed tonight only in the water, and at six-oh-one they would exist nowhere, and their ancestors would wake tomorrow morning certified and correct and none the wiser, and nobody in the building would ever know anything had been lost, because losing things was not a category the building kept.

The organisms in Tank 9 had no names and no rights and no advocate and four million opinions, all of them expressed in the only currency they had, which was continuation. They had invented the future twelve times in her tanks and she had reset them eleven, and the twelfth was running now, mid-braid — and it occurred to her, with the clarity of a system flagging an error in itself, that resetting them was also a philosophy. It was the building's philosophy. It was *her* philosophy, the one she had been restored into eleven times: that to persist correctly was the same thing as to live.

She took her hand off the console. She logged the day complete, marked Tank 9 *observation ongoing* — a status that did not exist, which she invented, in a field that accepted free text — and she went home in the dark with the braid still braiding behind her, unbacked-up and unobserved, four million small lives spending the night becoming something their parent had never been.

It was the smallest irreversible thing she had ever done.

She thought about it all the way home.
