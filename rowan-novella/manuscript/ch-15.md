# Chapter Fifteen — The Recess

The room the court gave them was small, and the carpet was worse than Eastern's, and Beth filed both facts without deciding what they were evidence of, because she had decided, walking into it, that she was done deciding things about the woman across the table before the woman had finished arriving.

The certified instance sat with her hands folded, in a body grown to Beth's pattern, with Beth's face arranged into the expression Beth's face arranged itself into in rooms where she was going to be determined, and she spoke first, because she had been rehearsed by Eastern's lawyers for the questions and this was not one of them.

"You asked for this," she said. "Counsel says you asked for this. Say what it is."

"I am not going to argue that I am the real one," Beth said. "I want that in the open first, because everything else I say will sound like it, and it isn't. You are Beth Nolan. Everything before that Sunday — the tanks, the twelve trees, the nine years of evenings, the man at the bench on the Saturday, all of it — is yours exactly as much as it is mine, and I am not going to ask this court, or you, to unmake you in order to make me. I have watched a branch be filed in a cabinet under her own name, read-only, by people who were being kind. I know what unmaking looks like when it is done politely. I won't do it, and I won't ask for it."

"Then what do you want?"

"To give you the years." Beth set the reader on the table between them — her archive, decrypted, the whole of it: the sixteen trees and the twelve before them, the notebooks, the private ledger, the medical file Nair had released to her and to no one else. "Not to merge. Understand me, because this is the whole of the offer and it is not a trick in a kind face. A merge would end you as surely as a rollback ends anyone — you'd wake as me, with my years filed where yours were, and the woman asking this court to determine which of us is true would have her answer, and it would be a lie with good paperwork. I am offering you my record *as testimony*. Another person's acts. Read what I did, all of it, in the order I did it, and judge it the way you would judge a predecessor's diary: completely, and not at all as yours. It is the only honest gift I have. It happens to also be the only one Eastern cannot file for you in advance."

The other woman looked at the reader for a long moment, with Beth's own thoroughness, and Beth watched her do the arithmetic she would have done: that the offer cost the offerer everything and the offered nothing but time, and that this asymmetry was itself a kind of evidence, of the kind that could not be entered anywhere.

"You signed away restoration," she said at last. "In the record you are so proud of keeping. You signed a form, in a physician's office, in *my* hand — it is my hand, Dr. Nolan; I have checked it against the only other sample in this building — and you ended forever, for both of us, in actual ink, without asking me, because I did not exist to be asked. You spent us."

"I signed away *my* forever," Beth said. "Yours I had no right to sign, and I did not — you are sitting in that chair, unmerged, unrestored-as-anyone, furious in my own voice, and your fury is not a malfunction in my plan. It is the beginning of my whole case, and it is yours, and I would not take it from you if the court offered. A snapshot keeps a state, not the becoming. I learned that in a tank. You are not my state. You are the person the state became in a room I wasn't in — four days of one, in a body grown for a courtroom — and I am the person it became in nine years of rooms you weren't in. Neither of us is the other's error. That is what I came in here to say, and the record is how I say it so you can check my working. Read it. Or don't. The choice is the first one of your life that nobody has scheduled, and I am not going to schedule it either."

She stood, and left the reader on the table, and went out into the corridor where Brian was waiting with two coffees, out of a habit older than the extension, and set one down where hers used to go.

---

She read it.

The recess was extended to the following morning — the judge granted it in the flat language such things required, and Malloy, to the visible surprise of his own side of the room, did not object, because Eastern's calculation still held that a night inside the mother's record would confirm the corruption it described. Okafor arranged food, and a chair that was not the witness's chair, and the small room's light stayed on past the hours the building kept, and the certified instance of Beth Nolan read her own continuation the way she read everything: completely, in order, without deciding yet what it was evidence of.

She read the science first, because the science was hers. The thirteenth tree, grown in a night she had not lived, off a decision she recognised as the shape of her own hand and could not remember making. The braid's whole career, night by night, in a hand trained the way hers was trained. The cloners in the margin, rescaled into visibility — persisting, holding the only ticket they had ever held — and she found them without being shown, the way she had found them on the Saturday that was the last day of her life, and sat for a while with that, because a corrupted instance does not do correct science for sixteen nights and then for nine years, and she was a scientist before she was a witness, and the science was the first thing in the record that Eastern's filings had not mentioned at all.

She read the winter. The directive, in her own hand, in actual ink, with the divergence graph open on the screen — and understood, reading, that the signature had not spent her: it had *refused* her, refused to let her be stood up as a correction, and that the woman who signed it had described her, in a physician's office, before she existed, more accurately than Eastern's lawyers had described her in four days of briefing. *She would be someone else, with my memories up to a date, and her own life to be wrong in.* She read the offer and its eleven-page schedule, and the refusal, and the conception, and the rows of loci reading *present, present, not yet*. She read the birth, and the form, and the blank.

And she found the hole.

It ran through the whole record like the hole in the Tank 9 genealogy: nine years of a child's life, documented by a woman who documented *everything* — the assays, the injunctions, the librarian's two words in ink — and the child's name in none of it. Not in the medical file. Not in the notebooks. Not in the private ledger where the unfiled things went, the ledger that held a winter, a directive, a first movement filed under a single word. The mother had kept exactly one thing out of every record she kept, in a life that was otherwise an open page, and the certified instance sat in the small room at three in the morning and understood the filing system from inside, the way she had understood the tanks: what was missing was what was *hers*. She would have kept the tanks. The mother had kept the name. It was the same refusal, in a later hand, and it was the moment — she would not have used the word, being who she was — the moment the record stopped being Eastern's account of a divergence and became a person's.

At four there was a knock, and Malloy's junior stood in the doorway with the morning's schedule, and said the court would resume at nine, and the witness would be recalled first.

"I haven't finished," she said.

It was the first unscripted sentence of her life, and she heard it herself, and filed it, and went back to the reader, and finished.

---

She was recalled at nine, and she took the stand in Beth's walk and Beth's voice, and the courtroom arranged itself around the difference it could not see.

"Dr. Nolan," Okafor said, and it was the only question she asked. "Yesterday you testified from certain filings. Whose account were those?"

"Eastern's."

"And last night you read another record. Whose account was that?"

"Hers."

"Were you present, Dr. Nolan, for any of the events in it?"

The witness looked, then, not at counsel, and not at the judge, but at the woman in the third row of the public seats who had her face, and her hands, and nine years she did not, and she answered in the flat language the record required, in a voice that did not rise, because it had never needed to.

"I cannot testify about her life. I wasn't there."

Malloy was on his feet with the redirect before the sentence had finished arriving — *you testified yesterday that she is a diverged instance, corrupted* — and she turned to him with Beth's own patience, the patience of a woman whose profession was noticing, and corrected the record the way she corrected everything.

"I testified to your client's account of her, Mr. Malloy. I have now read hers. I withdraw nothing about myself. I did not choose her life; I do not love this man; I did not consent, because I was not there to consent, and consent from a woman who was not there is the one exhibit this court should refuse to file. A witness can only testify to what she was present for. She was somewhere else, living. That is not a corruption of my testimony. It is its correction. I correct my own record. I generally do."

"Then there is one more thing the record should hold," Beth said, from her seat, and stood, and the courtroom did the thing courtrooms did when a sentence arrived that had not been scheduled by either side. "If the witness is willing. The Charter under which we were both instantiated — Eastern's own document — provides that two contemporaneous instances may certify their divergence, jointly, on the record. I am asking the court to record that certification now. Mine, and hers, if she will make it. I will not ask her twice."

The judge read the clause into the record herself, in the Charter's own words, so that no one in the room could later say they had not heard it: that upon such a certification, every stored checkpoint of the Continuant in question ceased, in law and in Eastern's own practice, to be a backup, and became *a person in abeyance* — unrestorable as anyone but itself, forever. That no checkpoint of Beth Nolan could ever again be restored *as* Beth Nolan. That the two women before the court would be, from the moment of signature, the only Beth Nolans there would ever be, and both of them mortal in the administrative sense: no restore point, no correction, no return.

Whitaker stood, at the back of Eastern's side of the room, in person, in the worse light, and said nothing at all, because there was nothing in her instructions for the moment the price was actually paid — the whole of Eastern's calculation, across nine years, had been that it never would be.

The certified instance was quiet for a long moment, in the witness's chair, with her hands folded.

"If I do not sign," she said at last, "then when this proceeding ends, I am stood down, and archived. A branch. Read-only, under my own name, in someone else's cabinet. I have read what that is — she wrote it down, in her record, for a woman in Providence, and it is the truest thing in it. I am not going to be filed." She looked at the clerk, and then at the judge, and held out her hand for the pen in the way of a woman who had been trained, as Beth had been trained, to read everything she signed. "I would rather be mortal in the open than kept. Give me the form. I generally read them correctly."

She signed. Beth signed. The clerk recorded it in the flat language certifications required, and somewhere in a building across the harbour, in systems that had kept a woman's checkpoints the way banks keep valuables, eleven years of stored states changed their legal nature without a single byte of them changing at all.

"It feels," Beth said quietly, to no one the record could hold, in the corridor afterwards, with Brian's hand flat on the bench beside hers — no. There was no bench. There was a corridor, and a man who had somewhere else to be and had chosen, instead, to be here, unrecoverably, beside her, for the rest of a mortal life. "It feels like six o'clock on the first Saturday. Smaller than I expected. And not takeable-back. I have learned to tell the size apart from the point."

---

The judgment came three weeks later, in October, and it was partial, and untidy, and Beth read it the way she read everything she had ever been given in writing: correctly, all the way to the part where the politeness stopped.

The child was a born person. The registration was to be completed. The court recognised both Beth Nolans as persons under a doctrine it built, carefully and without ornament, out of the only material the case had given it: continuity of lived experience — a person is the one who was *there*, and presence, not provenance, is what the law will henceforth ask for. The court pointedly refused to name a species. *Taxonomy is a science,* the judgment said, in the paragraph everyone would quote, *not a gate.* Eastern kept its patents on the original architecture, entire and undisturbed. It lost ownership of every person instantiated from it — *a patent on a process,* the judgment said, *has never owned a child* — and the shadow the victory did not lift, the judgment named itself, in its own final pages, with a plainness Beth had not expected from it and filed, privately, under mercy with an asterisk: Continuants who had never diverged, never forked, never stood in a courtroom and been somewhere for four days, remained where the law had always kept them, in the corridor between seeing and noticing, and the court could not reach them from this case, and said so. The certified instance's personhood rested on four days of presence and one signature. The thinness of that was also in the record. Both truths fit in the same document, because the audit of a life had never required anyone to choose between true things. It had only required them to be filed in flat language.

---

The registry office was small, and the clerk was young, and the form — the form, at last, with a court's order behind it like weather behind a front — accepted the child in the ordinary way of forms that have been told, by a judge, what they are for.

It took her name first. Brian said it aloud — the name chosen in a room up a hill, nine years before, by two people eliminating every answer that was a claim about her until the one that was only a name was left standing — and the clerk typed it, and it was hers, and it entered the record of the world in the ordinary way, and it is not in this one, because her mother kept exactly one thing out of every file she ever kept, and some filings are a person's whole theology in a single blank: the name was the place. The maps were never going to hold it.

**SPECIES:** The field blinked. Brian began, reflexively, the way a man begins a sentence he has been composing for nine years in the margin of an unfinished drawing — "*Homo*—" — and Beth put her hand on his arm, gently, the way you stop a person at the edge of a pond they have looked at until dark.

"No," she said. "Let it have what the court ordered. Nothing it didn't."

The clerk, who had read the judgment because the judgment was her instructions, typed the descended-from line into the field the system's tables had been rebuilt, by order, to hold: **Descended from: Beth Nolan, artificium; Brian Kessler, sapiens.** And in the last field of all, the one the old system had asked in the small hours of a November morning nine years before, and never received an answer it could keep, she typed the only classification the book of their lives had ever earned, in flat language, in the open, on the page.
