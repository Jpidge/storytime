# Chapter Six — The Review

The review panel convened on a Thursday, in a room with worse carpet.

Beth had known there would be a room. She had not known she would be grateful for the carpet. The tank room had been hers in the way a place becomes yours by being the room your evenings happened in. This room belonged to nobody. It had a long table, water in a jug that nobody drank, and a window that looked out on the harbour at an angle that made the water look like an afterthought, and it was, in every way the building could arrange, a place where a woman could be determined without anyone having to raise their voice.

Hendricks sat at the far end with his checklist closed. That was new. Park sat at the side with her tablet held, for once, in her lap rather than like a tray, and Beth understood that Park was not on the panel. Park was a witness who had already been read.

The man at the head of the table introduced himself the way the building introduced everything that mattered: as a formality already half-completed.

"Greg Malloy," he said. "Counsel for Eastern Biotechnologies. This is a review, Dr. Nolan, not a disciplinary hearing. The distinction matters to the record, so I'll ask you to keep it in mind."

"I have read the distinction," Beth said. "It is in the part of my agreement that describes what a review may determine."

"Then you know it may determine the status of the population, the status of your secondment, and the status of any development conducted during it." He opened a folder. The folder was paper. She had begun to keep a private census of the things the building trusted to paper: Brian's report. The Margarets' notebook. Tom's wrist. Now counsel's folder. Paper was where the building put the things it intended to be able to deny having decided. "Shall we begin with the population?"

"We shall have to," Beth said. "It's the only one of the three that can't speak in this room."

---

They read the science first, because the science was the part everyone in the room could agree to have read.

The genealogy went up on the room's wall — not her wall, a smaller one, greyer — and the braid braided there in miniature, sixteen nights of it, and then the three weeks since the hold: the population under observation, fed, lit, monitored around the clock, its genealogy now without a hole in it anywhere. The braid had not stopped. That was the first thing the post-hold record established, and Hendricks established it without emphasis: downstream of the hold, the exchanger lineage had continued to exchange, the borrowed immunities had continued to move, and the cloners had continued, in the margin, to persist.

"The population is stable," Hendricks said. "It is viable. It is, by the snapshot comparison, sixteen nights and twenty-three days removed from its certified baseline, and diverging further at a rate consistent with the unobserved period."

"Thank you," Beth said.

"It wasn't a compliment, Dr. Nolan."

"It was a finding. I take them where I find them."

Malloy turned a page in the paper folder. "The question before the panel is not whether the population lives. It is what it is, in law and in the agreement. Development conducted on Eastern's premises, from Eastern's certified stock, during a secondment funded by Eastern and its funders — conducted, however, off the record, past the contractual reset hour, under a status that did not exist until you invented it. Clause nine is not ambiguous about the category."

"Undeclared goods," Beth said.

"You've read it correctly. You generally do." He said it without malice, which was worse than malice would have been, because malice would have made him a villain and a villain could be argued with on moral grounds. Malloy could only be argued with on the grounds he had brought, and he had brought good ones. "Eastern does not dispute that the population is scientifically significant. Eastern does not dispute that the sealed replication — Dr. Kessler's work, conducted on the record — establishes the underlying phenomenon. What Eastern disputes is that significance confers standing. A result obtained in breach does not cease to be a result. It also does not cease to be a breach. Both things are filed."

"And the remedy for a breach of clause nine?"

"Is a determination." He closed the folder on one finger, keeping his place. "Not a punishment. You were told that on the day, and it remains true. The panel will determine what the population is, and what your secondment is, and the determinations will be written down, and the written-down things will be what happens next."

Park's face, at the side of the room, did nothing at all. Beth had learned to read that nothing the way she read a plot below the thousandth line: the absence was the information, if you rescaled for it. Park was doing nothing now, in a room with worse carpet, and the nothing had the shape of a woman watching a determination arrive at the speed of a thing that had been decided by the shape of the room before anyone sat down in it.

"What happens to a population that is determined to be undeclared goods?" Beth said.

Hendricks answered, because it was a technical question, and technical questions were his department. "Ordinarily? It is inventoried. Exhaustively. Every lineage sampled, sequenced, snapshotted at a depth the ordinary snapshot does not attempt. The record is made complete."

"And then?"

"And then the tank is returned to a certified baseline for the next phase of work. The design phase requires certified starts. You wrote that requirement into the array specification yourself, Dr. Nolan. With Dr. Kessler."

The room was quiet in the way rooms are quiet when a sentence has been allowed to finish arriving.

"You are describing a reset," Beth said, "with a longer noun in front of it."

"I am describing the ordinary disposition of undeclared development."

"The snapshot will keep the map," she said. "I have kept maps. Twelve of them, before these. A map is ancestry. It is who crossed with whom, and which immunities moved, and in what order. It is not the population. The population is four million living configurations and the precise spread of their variation, and none of that fits in any file you will make in this room, at any depth, because I have spent three years learning exactly how much of it fits, and the answer is: the tree. Only the tree. You will file the tree, and you will reset the place, and you will call the filing a preservation because the alternative word is not in the inventory manual."

"Dr. Nolan—" Malloy said.

"Let her finish," Hendricks said, mildly, and Beth understood that Hendricks' patience, which she had priced into the Monday of the inventory as a quality, was in fact a procedure: a man whose profession was noticing let things be said completely, because complete things could be filed completely.

"That is my finish," she said. "The population in Tank 9 is the descendant population of the certified baseline. It is not the baseline, and it cannot be returned to the baseline, because returning is a word for a thing that can be done to a file. What you are proposing to do to it has a plainer name, and I would like the plainer name in the record of this review, even if it is only in my objection."

Malloy wrote something in the paper folder. She could not see what. She filed the not-seeing, privately, as the morning's first entry under a heading she did not yet have a name for, which was the list of things in the room that were being kept on paper precisely so that they would not have to be kept anywhere else.

"Your objection is noted," he said. "In those words?"

"In those words."

---

Diane Whitaker joined by screen at eleven, which was how the chief executive of Eastern Biotechnologies joined everything: from elsewhere, at full size, with the particular clarity of a person whose image had been arranged by people whose profession was arranging it.

Beth had met her twice in nine years. Once at a secondment celebration she no longer had any use for, and once in a corridor, where Whitaker had known her name and her tank numbers and the renewal date of her agreement, and had asked after the recombination work with an interest so exactly calibrated that Beth had filed it, at the time, under weather.

"Dr. Nolan," Whitaker said. "I want to say something before counsel continues, and I want it understood as it is meant. Nobody in this process believes you acted for gain. Nobody believes the science is anything but what Dr. Kessler's report says it is. If the question before us were what you deserve, this would be a shorter meeting."

"I believe you," Beth said, and found, examining it, that she did. That was the building's whole method, and Whitaker was the building at its most itself: the cage was not built by cruel people. It was built by careful ones, and the care was real, and the cage held anyway.

"The question is what a precedent costs," Whitaker said. "Eastern maintains several hundred Continuants in secondments, Dr. Nolan. Hospitals. Universities. A ferry network, I am told, which I continue to find improbable and which continues to be true. Every one of them works under agreements like yours, because the agreements are what make the work possible — for them as much as for us. If development off the record is ratified after the fact because it turned out well, then the record is optional for everyone whose development turns out well, and there is no record. There is only outcome, judged afterwards, by the people who benefited from it. I cannot run a company on afterwards."

"No," Beth said. "I ran a laboratory on it for sixteen nights. It worked beautifully. That is rather the finding."

For the first time that morning, Whitaker smiled, and the smile was genuine, and Beth filed that too: the antagonist of her life was a woman who could be pleased by a true sentence in the middle of the meeting that was going to end it.

"It did," Whitaker said. "And if I ratify it, I ratify it for the ferry network. You see my difficulty."

"I see it," Beth said. "I have lived in it for nine years. Your difficulty is that the agreements require us to be restorable, identical, and accountable, and that anything we become that was not in the agreement is, by the agreement's own grammar, a malfunction or a theft. I did not steal the sixteen nights, Ms. Whitaker. I was there for all of them. They were my evenings. The tank was your property. The evenings were the part of the transaction nobody had thought to price, because nobody had imagined we would spend them on anything but being correct."

The screen was quiet a moment. Screens did that better than rooms; the silence had no carpet in it.

"Counsel will put the determination to you this afternoon," Whitaker said at last. "I asked to speak first so that you would hear it from a person before you heard it from a procedure. Whatever you decide about the rest of it, Dr. Nolan, I am sorry about the tanks. I know what they were."

She left the screen the way she had entered it, completely, and the room was smaller without her, and Malloy opened the paper folder again at the place his finger had kept.

---

The determination was read at two o'clock, in the flat language determinations required.

The population in Tank 9 was Eastern Biotechnologies' property in development. Development conducted off the record did not transfer title, diminish title, or create any interest in the developer. The population would be exhaustively inventoried over the coming days, beginning Friday, under Hendricks' supervision, and Tank 9 would thereafter be returned to its certified baseline to serve the design phase. Dr. Nolan's secondment was not terminated. Her conduct was recorded as a breach of clause nine, without dishonesty — the panel used the phrase *without dishonesty* twice, and Beth understood that it was the building's way of giving her the one thing it could afford to give her, which was the truth about her in writing — and she was reassigned, effective immediately, from the production tanks to documentation duties for the design phase, under Dr. Park's direction, for a period the determination left open.

"Documentation," Beth said.

"The array's specification requires a historian, in effect," Park said, from the side of the room, in the tone of a woman delivering weather she had not made. "Your archive is the fullest record of the phenomenon the array is being built to study. The panel considers it the best use of—"

"Of the map," Beth said. "Yes. I am to be the historian of a country that will have been demolished to tidy the filing. I wrote that sentence this morning, Mr. Malloy. You may have it for the record as well. It appears I am going to have time to write a great many sentences."

Nobody in the room corrected her. That was the politeness, holding the building up in a slightly different place than it had that morning, the way a floor's creak migrates.

"There is one further matter," Malloy said, "and I raise it because the panel would rather raise it than have it raised at you later, by people with less context." He turned a page. "Your embodiment review falls due in the spring. It is routine. It has nothing to do with this determination. But a breach recorded without dishonesty is still a breach recorded, and the review will read it, and I would be failing in a duty I actually hold — as distinct from the several I am paid to simulate — if I did not tell you to expect questions about judgement. About whether unmonitored plasticity events of this kind indicate—" He stopped, and chose, visibly, a plainer word. "Whether the evenings indicate a change in you that Eastern is obliged to assess."

"Assess," Beth said.

"Continuants who diverge from their certified patterns are assessed," he said. "You know the procedure. You watched a colleague go through a version of it in the spring." Margaret Ellison's fork, filed under her own name in someone else's cabinet. "No one is proposing anything. I am telling you the spring will have questions in it."

"Thank you," Beth said, because it had been, within its limits, a kindness — a man telling her the shape of the weather before it arrived, in a building that preferred its weather unannounced — and she stood, and the review was over, and the water in the jug was still undrunk, and the harbour through the angled window was doing its November work, indifferent and doubled and entirely unfiled.

---

She was allowed into the tank room once more, on the Friday, to supervise the first day of the exhaustive inventory, because Hendricks had asked for her and the panel had seen no reason to refuse a request that made the inventory better.

It took all day to do properly, and she did it properly. The sampling would be done by evening; the sequencing would run the weekend, and the reset was set for Monday at noon, when the sequencers had finished. That was the worst of it: she was the only person alive who could read the population at the depth the inventory required, and so the map would be as complete as a map could be because she had made it so, in the open, on the page. Sample by sample. Lineage by lineage. The braid in full, its borrowed immunities in their living spread — no. Not in their living spread. In their recorded spread. She corrected herself each time, because the correction was the whole of the difference, and nobody else in the room was going to make it.

The younger auditor from the Monday of the inventory worked beside her, the one who had told her the cloners were what he would read. He worked the way she worked, thoroughly and without protecting anyone's feelings, and at the end of the afternoon, when the sampling was done and the sequencers were running and Tank 9 stood behind its glass doing, still, the thing it had learned to do in the dark, he said, quietly, to the room generally and to no one who could have reported it:

"The exhaustive record is complete, Dr. Nolan. For what a record is."

"For what a record is," she agreed.

The reset was scheduled for Monday at noon, so that no one could say the hour had been chosen kindly. She would not be present. The determination had not forbidden her presence; it had simply not imagined it, and she had decided, standing at the console she no longer had the keeping of, that she would not ask the building to imagine it. Some things you did not make a procedure carry.

At six o'clock, out of a habit older than the audit, older than the extension, older than Brian Kessler, she looked at the time. The console stood open on a status set by other hands from a list of statuses that existed. It read, in a stranger's flat hand: *held pending disposition.*

She did not touch it. Her hand did not even rise. That was new, and she noted it the way she noted everything: without deciding, yet, what it was evidence of — only that a habit of sixteen evenings and nine years had met a locked console and stopped, and that the stopping had cost her more than any of the evenings had.

"Same time Monday, then," said the younger auditor, not unkindly, meaning the sequencers, meaning the work.

"No," Beth said. "Not the same time. That's rather the point of Monday."

She walked home the long way, along the water, in a body that reported the cold at the speed bodies report it, and thought about maps, and places, and the difference between a thing they read and a thing you live in, and about a woman in Providence who had kept the winter and been filed for it, and who had written, in ink, the only advice anyone had given her that had not also been an inventory.

*Take the winter.*

She did not yet know what her winter was. She knew it was not documentation duties. She knew it was not the spring review, and the questions about judgement, and the assessment of whether the evenings indicated a change in her. Or rather — she corrected herself, in the open, on the page of her own mind, the way she corrected everything that mattered — perhaps it was exactly that. Perhaps the winter was the change, assessed or not, lived in rather than read.

Behind her, across the harbour, four million small lives braided under lights that would go off on Monday at noon, and the genealogy they grew between now and then would not have a hole in it any longer. It would simply end. She would keep the map of them forever, in an archive that was hers, encrypted, unfiled in any quarterly report. A map was not nothing: a map was what a person kept when she was not permitted to keep the place. It was not enough. That was also true, and both truths fit in the same evening, because the audit of her life had never required her to choose between true things. It had only required her to file them in flat language.

She filed this one in no language at all, and walked on.
