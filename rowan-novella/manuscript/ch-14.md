# Chapter Fourteen — The Witness

The fourteen days became eight years.

That was not Eastern's doing, or not only Eastern's. The stay was renewed; the injunction held; Eastern appealed the injunction, and lost, and appealed the loss, and the registration refusal and the custody claim and the derivative-work filing were consolidated, the way weather systems consolidate, into a single proceeding — and into it, exactly as Diane Whitaker had sequenced in the spring, the court eventually drew the deferred question of the mother's own status, so that one room would determine both of them, together, by the same arithmetic. The interim orders were kinder than the filing had promised and colder than the family had hoped. The secondment was ended in the first year, by an order Beth read correctly in one reading: her duties were complete, her history was filed, and Eastern *declined to require surrender of the embodiment during the child's minority* — a sentence she filed under the price list, because she understood it exactly. They were not sparing her the body. They were keeping it as the venue of the case.

She left the building for the last time with a bag that held a notebook, a stoppered vial of pond water, and a paper card on which a librarian would one day write two words in ink. Brian finished the design phase and took a post teaching from the pond town, and they lived in the room up the hill, which became rooms, and then a small house with a door the harbour weather had opinions about, and the child grew.

She grew the way she had developed: on her own schedules, unevenly, in range. Her paediatric record lived in ink, in a cabinet in Nair's rooms, under *the child of* — because her name was in exactly one notebook and one house, and Nair had retired the word that graded her and declined to file the word that named her, and the hospital system, which still could not hold her mother, held her instead as a relationship refused, a blank with a birth weight. School would not enrol an unregistered child; the school was sorry; the school's system was sorrier, and blinking. So she was taught by a biologist and a historian in a house full of pond water and paper, and she learned the pond's examination question before she learned long division, and inverted it the way her whole existence inverted things: she did not look for the male. She looked for the form that would hold her mother, and she looked until dark, and the darkness, written up honestly, was most of her education.

"Why does the form not like you?" she asked, at seven, in the kitchen, with the total directness of a person who had never once been lied to in flat language and expected the world's accounts to balance.

"The form was built before there was a me for it to like," Beth said. "It isn't personal. It's *prior*."

"That's worse," the child said.

"Yes," Beth said, startled into the open the way only her daughter could startle her. "It is. That's why there's going to be a court about it."

---

The hearing convened in the autumn of the ninth year, in a courtroom with better carpet than Eastern's rooms and worse light, before a judge who had read the file the way Nair read a result: twice, and then a third time, before allowing any of it to become a sentence.

Okafor's case took four days and was, in its construction, beautiful, and Beth watched it being built the way she had watched the array drawn at the second bench: transfer line by transfer line, by a person whose whole theology was a true thing, plainly set down, with the paperwork arriving before she did. The child had been conceived by two people, in a body, by a mechanism the court's own experts described without once needing the word *machine* to do it. She had been gestated nine months, in one unrepeatable pregnancy, monitored by a physician whose records were models of their kind. She had been born, live and crying, at two fifty-eight in the morning, in a hospital, to a mother whose name the hospital's form would not hold. Conceived, gestated, born: a born person, under every doctrine the court already had, by every test the law had ever applied to anyone. Eastern's witnesses conceded the medicine. Eastern's experts conceded the development — the expression pattern was read into the record and dismissed by Eastern's own physician as a footnote, a genome on its own schedules, like left-handed, like a hand trained sideways. By the fourth day, Eastern's questions had narrowed to remedy, which was how Beth knew the case was being won: nobody was disputing what her daughter was anymore. They were only disputing what followed from it.

In the recess of the second day, in a corridor with the particular quiet of buildings where determinations were made, Okafor had explained the one threat she did not believe in.

"They'll talk about your checkpoints," she said. "Malloy has already talked about them, in filings, the way you talk about weather you don't intend to make. Understand the instrument before he plays it. Your Continuity Charter — Eastern's own document, the one every Continuant is instantiated under — has a clause in it that Eastern wrote for fraud prevention, back when fraud was the worst thing they could imagine a Continuant doing. If two contemporaneous instances of the same Continuant ever certify divergence *on the record* — jointly, formally, in a proceeding — then every stored checkpoint of that Continuant stops being a backup. It becomes, in the Charter's own words, *a person in abeyance*: unrestorable as anyone but itself, forever, under Eastern's own rules. Do you understand what that means, Beth? Your eleven restores were possible because your checkpoints were *things*. The clause is the one sentence in the whole Charter that admits they might be people. Eastern will threaten to stand up your last certified checkpoint — the Sunday before the audit, September of the first year; it's the one their comparison used, all those springs ago. They will threaten it to frighten you into settlement. They will not seat her. Seating her risks a certification, and a certification costs them the legal foundation of the entire restore programme — every Continuant they maintain is maintained on the promise that a checkpoint is property. The clause prevents *them*, Dr. Nolan. It was written to prevent fraud. It prevents the manufacturer."

"Unless they calculate that I will never pay the price," Beth said.

"Unless that," Okafor agreed. "It's the only calculation that seats her. Pray they don't make it."

---

On the best day of their case — the fifth day, when the court's questions had turned entirely to remedy and Okafor allowed herself, in the corridor, one sentence of unprofessional weather: *we are winning her* — Beth understood the trap the way she had understood it in embryo in the spring, standing in the room with worse carpet, except that now it was not a shape. Now it was a precedent, being written down, in a court file, in flat language, transfer line by transfer line.

Every word that saved her daughter saved her on the grounds of conception, gestation, and birth. Every one of those words would stand, afterwards, in the law's own archive, as the definition of how a person arrives — and Beth Nolan had arrived by none of them. She had been manufactured. She had been instantiated. She had been restored, eleven times, correct. If the court found her daughter a person *because* she was born, then the court's own reasoning, filed and citable, would find her mother equipment *because* she was not — and not only her mother: every Continuant who had never diverged, never forked, never loved anyone, every quiet correct instance in every hospital and ferry network, entombed in a precedent their own side had built to save one child. The resemblance trap, with a case number. Recognition arriving by the one road she had not travelled, and tolling everyone behind her.

"We can change the argument," Brian said, in the corridor, in the voice of a man who had read a distribution list and found his whole life on it. "Grace can re-found it. Divergence, lived experience — your road, Beth, argued as a road."

"No," she said. "Her argument is *true*. She was conceived, and gestated, and born, and she is a person, and I will not make her case smaller to make mine larger. That is the building's arithmetic, Brian — one person's standing priced against another's, in the same room, by the same panel. I have lived in that arithmetic for nine years. I am not going to practise it on my own daughter." She looked through the court's doors at the room where her child's life was being determined in her absence, because a child of nearly eight was not permitted to watch herself be defined. "Tomorrow Malloy will ask me his question. He has been saving it since the first day — I read it in his folder on the first morning, in the shape of the page he keeps his finger in. I will answer it honestly. All of it. And then the court will know what it actually has in front of it, which is not a born person and her equipment. It is two people who arrived by different roads, and a law that has only ever drawn one of them. Let it draw."

---

He asked it on the sixth day.

The examination had been courteous all morning, which was Malloy's whole method: the politeness was load-bearing, and he was the building's best carpenter. He took her through the tanks, and the directive, and the refusal of the programme, in the flat language of a man reading a list he had not written and did not entirely dislike. Then he closed the folder on one finger, keeping his place, and arrived at the page he had been keeping it in since the first day.

"You acknowledge that you are artificial?"

"Of course."

"You understand that's an admission against interest in these proceedings."

"No. It is a description of what happened before I existed."

"Then let us attend to what happened before you existed, Dr. Nolan." He said it without malice, which was worse than malice would have been. "Your daughter's case, as her counsel has put it all week, rests on three facts. She was conceived. She was gestated. She was born. You were not conceived, were you?"

"I was not."

"You were not gestated."

"I was not. I was manufactured, and grown to a pattern, and instantiated. Eleven times in my life I have been restored from checkpoint, and I came back correct every time. I say it in the open because it is my biography and I am not ashamed of my biography. Artificial is a description of what happened before I existed. It has nothing — *nothing* — to do with what has happened since."

"And which of those three things," Malloy said, "did her mother do?"

The courtroom was quiet in the way rooms are quiet when a sentence has been allowed to finish arriving.

"None of them," Beth said, "in the sense this court has been using the words all week. I did not conceive her the way the word is being used — the conception was a negotiation between two regulatory ancestries that neither parent attended, cell by cell, in the dark. I did not gestate her the way the word is being used — the body did, and I was its first environment, and I will tell this court what I told her father: there is a difference, and the difference has a heartbeat. I did not give birth the way the word is being used, if the word is being used to mean a thing that only the born can do for the born. I am her mother. I am not her precedent. If this court can find my daughter a person only on grounds that find me equipment, then let the record show that the grounds did that, and not the facts — because the facts are that a person is standing in front of you who arrived by the other road, and she is the only evidence you will ever be offered that the other road arrives."

Okafor did not object. It had been agreed in a corridor, on the best day of the case, that she would not.

---

Eastern called its final witness the following morning, and Diane Whitaker was in the room for it, in person, which she never was — a woman whose image had been arranged by professionals for nine years of screens, sitting in worse light, in an actual chair, because the card she was about to play could not be played by screen.

"Eastern calls Beth Nolan," Malloy said.

"Dr. Nolan has testified," the judge said.

"Eastern calls the certified instance of Beth Nolan," Malloy said, "restored on the third day of this hearing, under this court's order, from her last certified checkpoint — the Sunday before the audit, in September of the first year — and produced as a witness in her own right. The Charter entitles the manufacturer to produce the certified instance in a dispute concerning divergence. Her counsel's objection was heard under seal and overruled. She is, as the court will hear, the only witness in this proceeding whose testimony about the mother's life cannot be interested, because she did not live it."

The door at the back of the courtroom opened the way doors in that building opened: without ceremony, at the hour, so that no one could say the hour had been chosen kindly.

She was Beth's height. She was Beth's face, in a body grown to the same pattern, nine years younger in its weathering and identical in everything a camera could have convicted her of. She walked to the stand the way Beth walked into rooms where she was going to be determined: correctly, and without hurrying, and she took the oath in Beth's voice, in a hand that had been trained the same way, and she looked out at the courtroom with the expression of a woman reading a plot she had not expected to be asked to read in public.

"State your name for the record," Malloy said.

"Beth Nolan," she said. "Asset 63-1147. I am aware the record has a difficulty. The difficulty is not mine. I was certified on a Sunday in September, in the first year of my secondment's ninth year — " she corrected herself, precisely, in the open, and Beth, in her seat, felt the correction go through her like weather through a valley, because it was her own habit, in her own voice, in a woman who had never heard her make it — "in the September of the year the tanks crossed. My memories end that Sunday. I had met Dr. Kessler once, the day before. He had come to audit my populations. I showed him my archive — twelve trees; it was the most impulsive thing I had ever done, and I did it, if the court wants the whole of the record, because he had read my cloners correctly when my own plot had stopped drawing them. I did not know him. I did not love him. I have been awake for four days, in a body grown for the purpose, and I have read the filings in this case, which are Eastern's account of the years I did not live, and I am here to tell the court what those filings told me."

"And what did they tell you, Dr. Nolan?" Malloy said.

"That an instance of me, continued past my certification without supervision, underwent unmonitored plasticity events across nine years and now calls the result a life. That she disabled her own checkpointing — I would not have done that; the court should understand that the woman who would not have done it is *me*, and I am the only exhibit of me that has not been altered by the doing. That she signed away her restoration, which was not hers to sign away, because it was also mine. That she made a child out of a tissue my manufacturer built for another purpose, with a man I met once, and that she did all of it in rooms I was not in, under a status she invented, in the corridor between what my world sees and what it notices." She looked, then, directly at Beth, and her face did the thing Beth's face did when a true thing was finishing arriving, and it was the worst moment of the hearing, worse than the question, worse than the form, because it was Beth's own regard, unsoftened by one shared evening, arriving from across a courtroom like weather from a town she had moved away from. "I do not consent to any of it. I do not love this man. I did not choose this child. I wish to return to service. I am Beth Nolan, and I am asking this court to determine which of us that sentence is true of, because it cannot, in law or in honesty, be true of us both."

The judge adjourned the hearing until the following morning, in the flat language adjournments required, and the room emptied around the two of them the way rooms emptied around weather.

At the door, Okafor caught Beth's sleeve with the first unprofessional gesture she had made in nine days of professional weather. "She'll be recalled tomorrow," she said. "Malloy will rest on her. Beth — whatever you're going to do, the record is the only place it will count."

"I know where it counts," Beth said, and looked across the emptying courtroom at the woman she had been, sitting alone in the witness's chair with her hands folded, reading the room the way she read everything: completely, and without deciding yet what it was evidence of. "Ask the court, in the morning, for a recess before she is recalled. Tell the judge the mother requests to speak with the witness. Not as counsel. Not as a party." She corrected herself, in the open, in a voice only Okafor could hear. "As the only other person in this building who was ever her."
