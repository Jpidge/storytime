# Chapter Nine — The Offer

The offer arrived on a Tuesday, by the route offers arrived in the building: politely, on schedule, and in a form that had clearly been a formality already half-completed before anyone told Beth it existed.

It was not addressed to her. That was the first thing. It was addressed to Dr. Priya Nair, as the physician of record for embodiment 63-1147, and it came from Eastern's reproductive oversight office — an office Beth had not known existed until she read its name at the head of the letter, in the way you note the existence of a new camera in a corridor you walk every day. Nair read it in her rooms, with Beth present, because Nair had requested her presence before she opened it, which was Nair's whole method in one gesture: the patient in the room before the news was.

"They found the tissue's name," Nair said. "In the design archive, as predicted. It's called a *translational gonad*, in the specification, which is the kind of name a committee gives a thing it never expects to be used. The specification assumed it would remain quiescent in checkpointed embodiments — a reasonable assumption, as assumptions go. It had held for every embodiment in the fleet, until the fleet produced a woman who filed a no-restore directive and spent a winter being changed by a specific man in a specific order." She turned the letter around on the desk. "Read it correctly. You generally do."

Beth read it correctly.

The offer was kind. She had predicted it would be kind, in Brian's room, with the harbour going dark, and she had been right in the expensive way: it was kind the way Whitaker was kind, with the care real and the cage built into the care's foundations. Eastern proposed a programme. The translational process would be paused — safely, reversibly, under Nair's own supervision, a word the letter used three times as if repetition were a sedative — while a full study was conducted. Gametes already drawn would be preserved, screened, and characterised. If the parents wished to proceed thereafter, Eastern would support a conception conducted under optimal conditions: the best draw selected from a characterised cohort, translation errors corrected where correction was established practice, development monitored to standards no private pregnancy in history had ever enjoyed. The child — the letter called her, throughout, *the resulting organism*, and Beth filed the phrase the way she filed everything, privately, as evidence — the resulting organism would be Eastern's partner in a new field of medicine. Her care would be without limit. Her education likewise. The parents would want for nothing, for as long as they lived, which in Beth's case the letter did not presume to estimate.

In return, the resulting organism's genome — a derivative work, in the letter's phrase, of Eastern's proprietary embodiment architecture — would be held under Eastern's existing intellectual property framework, with the parents' rights fully respected *as defined in the attached schedule.*

The attached schedule was eleven pages long. Beth read all eleven. She had read her secondment agreement the way she read everything she signed, and she read the schedule the way she had read clause nine: correctly, which is to say, all the way to the part where the politeness stopped being load-bearing and started being the whole of the structure. The parents' rights, as defined, were the rights of consultants to a process. Decisions about screening were Eastern's. Decisions about correction were Eastern's. Decisions about the disclosure of the resulting organism's existence, her residence, her medical care, her — the schedule's word was *deployment*, and Beth stopped there a moment, the way a reader stops at a seed tag in a lineage, forty bases that told you exactly which line you were in — her deployment, were Eastern's, subject to consultation.

"They've offered us the tanks," she said at last.

"Explain that to me," Nair said, "because I want it in my summary in your words and not my paraphrase."

"A certified baseline. A screened draw. Correction toward a target. Development on the record, every hour accounted for, in rooms full of people whose profession is noticing — and at the end of it a child who is Eastern's property in development, with her parents retained as consultants to her own life." She set the schedule down precisely where Nair had set it. "I have seen this programme before, Dr. Nair. I ran it for nine years. It was called my secondment. The only difference is that this time the population is my daughter."

Nair was quiet a moment. "And the alternative reading? Give it to me as well. I won't file a summary with one reading in it, not for this."

"The alternative reading is that they are frightened, and they are right to be. An uncorrected draw, an unmonitored negotiation between two regulatory ancestries nobody has ever modelled — the runaway failure alone justifies their fright, and Whitaker believes, genuinely, that an unregulated birth could kill us both and end embodied Continuants entirely. She is protecting a world that works." Beth folded her hands. "It happens to be a cage. Both readings are true. The audit of my life has never required me to choose between true things. It has only required me to decide which of them I am going to live in."

"Then decide," Nair said, "and I will write it down in flat language, and it will be a fact in the world."

---

They decided in Brian's room, that evening, the three of them, because Nair had insisted — as a condition of her continued care, stated in her own rooms in her own tone — that the decision be made with the physician present and the price list open on the table between them like a third setting.

Brian read the offer the way he had read her specification: as a witness whose testimony he intended to break. He read the schedule twice. At *deployment* he stopped, and read it a third time, and set the page down in the margin of the table, where sentences went that had not earned their keep.

"No," he said.

"Give me the long version," Beth said. "For the record Priya is keeping in flat language, because she is the only person in the building whose records I trust to be read in rooms I'm not in."

"The long version is that I study an animal that survived forty million years by refusing every offer of improvement evolution made it, and taking in strangers' genes through the walls instead, on its own terms, unobserved. The long version is that I watched a population in a tank invent the future twelve times and get reset for it by people who filed the map and called the filing a preservation. The long version—" He stopped, and corrected himself, in the open. "The long version is short. They are offering to design her, Beth. Screen the draw until there's a draw they like. Correct the errors toward a target they choose. That is not care. Care is Priya watching for the runaway and catching us if we fall. Design is steering, and we are the two people alive who know exactly what steering costs, because we've both spent our lives being steered by kinder hands than these. No selection. No correction toward a target. No repeat-until-preferred. Viability checks, yes. Medicine, yes. Priya's assays, yes. The rest — no."

"And if the draw is the arrest?" Nair said. "Say it in the room. All of it gets said in the room, or none of this is consent."

"If the draw is the arrest, we lose her early and we grieve her as a child lost early, the way people have always lost children early, without a committee explaining that a better draw was available." His voice did not rise. It had never needed to. "And if it's the runaway, you do everything medicine can do, and if medicine can't, we lose her that way instead. And if it's the silence — " He looked at Beth. "If it's the silence, she is our daughter who develops as an ordinary human carrying her mother's sequences unread, and she is our daughter, full stop, and no assay gets to tell us otherwise. Priya said it in her rooms: the assay detects expression. It does not detect kinship. We are not going to let a laboratory instrument grade her claim on us. That is the whole of the trap, and we decline to walk into it in either direction — we don't need her to express to be ours, any more than Beth needed to be restorable to be a person."

Beth sat in the lamplight of a room up a hill and listened to the father of her unmade child refuse, in advance, in writing, the exact bargain the rest of her life had been built on — recognition in exchange for resemblance, standing in exchange for being correctable — and understood that she had chosen the other half of the distribution correctly, which was a sentence she corrected at once, because choosing correctly was the building's grammar and it had no place at this table. She had not chosen him the way a panel chose an outcome. She had loved him first, in particulars, across a bench, over a vial of pond water, and the categories were failing afterwards, at their leisure, exactly as promised.

"Write it in flat language, Priya," she said. "The parents decline trait selection, gamete screening for preference, repeat sampling, and correction toward any target outcome. The parents accept viability assessment, routine repair of the kind any prenatal care performs, expression assays for medical purposes, and full monitoring for the failure modes as listed. The parents understand the four failures. The parents begin anyway."

Nair wrote it. The pen was actual ink. She wrote it in the medical record, where directives lived, and then she wrote the second document — the reply to Eastern, one paragraph, in the flat language the building required — declining the programme and recording that conception, if attempted, would be attempted under Dr. Nair's care and outside Eastern's reproductive oversight framework, with Eastern notified as the embodiment's manufacturer and in no other capacity.

"They will not accept that as the end of it," Nair said, capping the pen.

"No," Beth said. "They will accept it as the beginning of the spring."

---

Eastern's answer came within the week, and it came, as Beth had known it would, through counsel.

Malloy did not summon her to a room with worse carpet. He came to Nair's rooms — by appointment, correctly, with the appointment's reason stated in advance, which she filed, privately, as the building's way of showing her that it could still observe the forms when the forms were all it had left. He stood, because Nair's rooms had two chairs and both were taken, and he delivered his news standing, in the tone of a man reading an emergency hold to no one, rehearsing.

"The reply is noted," he said. "Eastern does not accept that notification exhausts its interest. I am instructed to tell you — and I am telling you in a physician's rooms, with your physician present, because I advised that this be heard as information and not as theatre — that the company's position is as follows. A conception carried in an Eastern embodiment, from gametes synthesised by an Eastern-designed tissue, is a conception in which Eastern holds a legitimate interest, at law, whether or not the parents enrol in the programme. Declining the programme does not extinguish the interest. It merely — " he chose the word the way he chose all his words, visibly, " — renders it uncooperative."

"Uncooperative," Beth said. "A population that will not be observed on schedule. I have some experience of the category."

"You have the whole of the experience of the category, Dr. Nolan. That is rather the company's concern." He looked, briefly, not at her but at the closed embodiment file on Nair's desk. "There will be a spring review. There was always going to be a spring review. I am telling you, as I told you in the other room, what it will now have in it: questions about judgement, and questions about a tissue, and — if there is a pregnancy by then, and I am not asking you to confirm or deny anything in a room where I have no right to the answer — questions about the status of a child whose genome the company's lawyers are already, this week, learning to describe as a derivative work."

"Derivative," Nair said, in her own tone, which was not the building's. "Of a body. Counsel, I have delivered children for twenty years, in bodies made by every process nature and your client between them have devised, and I have never yet delivered a *work*."

"Doctor, I do not write the descriptions. I carry them, and I am carrying this one to the two people it concerns before it is carried anywhere else, which is more than my instructions required and exactly as much as they allowed." He turned back to Beth. "Whitaker asked me to add one thing in my own capacity, and I will add it in hers. She said: *tell her the offer does not expire.* The programme, the monitoring, the care without limit — if the winter frightens you, if the assays frighten you, the door back to a certified process remains open, at any point, on a phone call. She meant it kindly."

"I know she meant it kindly," Beth said. "That has never once been the question about any of it, Mr. Malloy. Tell her — in your own capacity, since we are trading in them — that the door she is holding open leads back into a room where my daughter is a deployment. Tell her I read the schedule correctly. I generally do."

He inclined his head, the way a man acknowledges a true sentence in the middle of a meeting that is going to end his client's way eventually, if his client has anything to say about it, and went out, and the corridor received him, and the politeness held the building up around the place he had been standing.

"He warned us," Nair said, when the door had closed. "Inside his instructions, at some cost to the shape of them, he warned us. File that under whatever heading you keep for such things."

"I keep it," Beth said, "under *mercy*, with an asterisk, because it was also an inventory." She stood, and put her coat on, and felt, in the body that reported the coat's weight at the speed bodies report it, the whole narrowing shape of the season ahead: a spring review gathering its questions, a company's lawyers learning a phrase, a door held kindly open onto a room she would not enter. "It doesn't change the decision, Priya. It only changes the weather the decision gets made in. I have some experience of weather too."

---

The conception was not a procedure. That was the thing nobody's schedule had a field for.

Eastern could have made it one — the programme had offered, in its eleven pages, a conception conducted under optimal conditions, in a facility, with a characterised cohort and a clinical team — and the refusal of the programme had been, among everything else it was, a refusal of that room. What happened instead happened in Brian's room up the hill, on a night in late winter when the harbour below the window was doing its work in the dark and the dead woman's luggage stood by the door and the notebook inside it still stopped mid-sentence, unfinished, unarchived, carried.

Beth disabled the live checkpointing herself, as she had on the first night — not as a gesture, but as the mechanism it was. The translator drew from a coupled history; the draw of that night would be the draw of that night, deepened by every unrecoverable evening since the first, by the directive in actual ink, by the determination in worse carpet, by the braid's whole career and the pond's whole argument. She understood the mechanism the way she understood all her mechanisms: precisely, and without letting the precision do the feeling's work for it. The gamete generated from the coupled, plastic, un-backed-up state of that specific night could only ever have come from these two people, then, in that order. Another night would draw another distribution. Another winter, another child. This one — if there was one — would be this night's.

She told him so, in the dark, because telling things in the open was her whole filing system and she was not going to abandon it at the exact moment it mattered most.

"I know," he said. "It's in my notebook too. Not the words. The pond taught it first. A crash comes, and the population that comes back is the one that was changed by the crashing, in the company it was changed in. There's no other population. There's never been another population."

Afterwards she lay in a body that reported another body's warmth at the speed bodies report it, and did not reach for a status field, because there was no console in the room and no field that would have held it. What had happened had happened once, in order, unrecoverably, and if the translator had done its work — if a draw had been taken from the distribution of her becoming, and met, in the ordinary miraculous machinery her grown body had kept quiescent for nine years, the ordinary contribution of the man beside her — then somewhere below every panel's margin of drawing, a negotiation had begun between two regulatory ancestries in one genome, in one body, that neither parent would ever be in the room for, because it happened cell by cell, in the dark, in the only unobserved place left in her life.

She did not know. That was the terror and the whole of the design — no. Not design. She corrected herself, in the open, on the page of her own mind, smiling in the dark like a woman rescaling her own plot.

That was the whole of the *refusal* of design, and it felt, at last, like Friday: ordinary, irreversible, and entirely hers.

"Same time tomorrow, then," Brian said, drowsily, out of a habit three months old and already older than the extension.

"Same time tomorrow," she said. "Bring your own tea. Yours is a crime scene by any hour."

And she slept the way the pond slept, in weather, while the negotiation — if there was a negotiation — went on without her, which was, she would come to understand, the first lesson of the nine months: she was not the author of what was happening in her. She was its first environment. There was a difference, and the difference had a heartbeat coming, and she slept, and let it come at its own speed, the way bodies let everything come.
