# Chapter Ten — The Assay

The pregnancy announced itself the way everything true in Beth's life had announced itself: in the margin first, below the line where the ordinary instruments stopped drawing, and only afterwards in the places the building kept accounts.

It was not a test. Tests came later, in Nair's rooms, with the flat language and the ink. It was the tea.

Brian made it the way he always made it, badly, in the tin mug's larger and only slightly less disreputable cousin, on a morning in the last week of her leave — the extensions run out at last, documentation duties beginning Monday, the design phase wanting its historian, the spring still gathering its questions off the water — and Beth, who had drunk his tea for three months without comment beyond the comment the tea deserved, set the mug down after one mouthful with the precision of a woman setting a vial down on a bench, exactly where it had been set for her.

"That's not tea," she said.

"It is demonstrably tea. I have the packet."

"It is demonstrably *wrong*. It tastes of the inside of a harbour." She examined the mug the way she examined a plot that had thickened where there should have been clean parallel lines. "Everything this week tastes of the inside of a harbour. I filed it under weather. Bodies in winter—"

"Beth." He had gone very still, in the doorway of his own small kitchen, with the kettle still in his hand. "Bodies in winter do not generally develop opinions about tea they have held, uncomplaining, since November. *Other* processes develop opinions. Specific ones. With a literature."

"Don't diagnose me in your kitchen, Dr. Kessler."

"I wouldn't dare. I'm a man whose entire field is a rounding error." He set the kettle down with exaggerated care, like a duellist who had decided, mid-gesture, to become a nurse instead. "But I teach first-years to let an absence get big enough to become the fact about a thing. And I have been watching a presence do the opposite trick all week. The tiredness at hours you don't keep. The food standing up, again — you ate standing up last night, Beth, for the first time since—" He stopped. "Since the week they took the tanks. Before that it was the years before the second bench. I filed it under grief. It isn't grief, is it?"

"No," she said, quietly, and put her hand, without deciding to, flat on the table — not on herself; she was not a woman for gestures she had not audited — on the table, where he could see it, steady, in the open, on the page. "I don't think it is grief. I think we should go and see Priya, and have it in flat language, because I have had enough of knowing things in the margin. Rescale the plot, Brian. All the way down."

---

Nair rescaled it.

The blood work took an hour. The panel took two. Beth lay in the scanning room in the body that reported the table's cold at the speed bodies report it, and read the ceiling, and thought about the difference between a map and a place with a thoroughness that had become, over one winter, her whole philosophy: a test was a map. A result was a map. The place itself was in her, unobserved by any instrument she could point at it from outside, negotiating cell by cell in the dark, and the maps would come back saying what maps said, and she would keep them, and they would not be the thing.

"It is a pregnancy," Nair said, at four o'clock, in her rooms, with the door closed and both of them in the chairs and the screen turned around on the desk so that the parents could read what they were about to be told. She said it the way she said everything: as news, delivered as news, a true thing plainly set down with no ornament on it. "Early. Weeks, not months — consistent with the night you described to me, and I will not pretend I did not do that arithmetic, because doing arithmetic is my profession and lying about it is not. The maternal sequences are present in the conceptus. Fertilisation occurred. One genome, in every cell sampled: his, and yours — yours drawn from the translator's distribution, in a complete maternal set, with your mitochondria, your initial conditions. She is not a chimera and she is not an assemblage. She is one organism, with two kinds of ancestry, the way a mammal's genome has viral ancestry: incorporated, not attached."

"She," Brian said.

"A manner of speaking," Nair said, and then, because she was Nair, corrected herself in the open. "No. Not yet a manner of anything. Sex is determinable and I have not determined it, because determination was not a viability question and I run viability questions in this room. When it becomes one, or when you ask me as parents rather than as a panel, I will tell you. Until then—" She turned a page of the file. "Until then, the assay."

The expression assay had been designed, insofar as anything about this pregnancy had been designed by anyone, by Nair herself, in the weeks since the conception decision, out of the specification's own logic: tissue-sampled expression at maternal regulatory loci, read against the viable ranges of the human processes those loci governed. It was the physician's instrument. It could distinguish — in principle, Nair said, and the *in principle* was load-bearing — between the four failures she had priced for them in the winter. Silence read as no expression anywhere. Runaway read as expression beyond viable range in the relevant tissue. Mosaicism read as expression present, but inconsistent across samples. Arrest read as the absence of a pregnancy to assay at all, which was the failure that announced itself and required no instrument.

"And the first reading?" Beth said.

"The first reading is early, and small, and I will give it to you exactly as I would give it to a review panel, because flattering a result is a kind of lying and I do not do it in either direction." Nair turned the screen a fraction further. The plot — everything in Beth's life eventually became a plot — showed the sampled loci in a row, and their expression, tissue by tissue, in the flat colours of a medical display that had never been asked to carry news like this before. "Expression is present. In range, where it is present. In two of the sampled tissues it is present and in range. In the third—" She paused, and Beth watched a professional choose exactness over comfort in real time, and loved her for it in the unfiled way. "In the third it is not yet detectable. Which at this stage means *one of three things*: silence in that tissue — true mosaicism, the genome lived in unevenly; or timing — the window for that tissue has not opened, and expression is scheduled later, which is how ordinary development handles most of its business, in sequence, in the dark; or sampling, which is the failure of my instrument and not of your daughter."

"Daughter again," Brian said, very quietly.

"Figure of speech," Nair said, "and I will keep making it and keep correcting it until one of us is told to stop, because the alternative is *the resulting organism* and I read that schedule too, Dr. Kessler, all eleven pages, in my own rooms, in my own time." She closed the file with care. "Here is the whole of what the assay says, in flat language, for the record I keep and nobody else reads. Integration is real. It is not yet legible as a pattern. The failures that have not happened remain possible — arrest is behind us for today and only for today; runaway is not present in anything sampled; silence, as a whole-pregnancy verdict, is *refuted* — she expresses, somewhere, in range, and a wholly silent child does not do that. What remains open is the mosaic question, and it will remain open for weeks, and I will not close it early to make any of us sleep better. You begin anyway. You have already begun anyway. That was the decision. This is merely its first weather."

Beth sat with her coat still on — she noted it, startled, the echo of the Saturday of the dinner, a woman whose evening had not been scheduled by a clause — and read the row of loci once more, present, present, not yet, like a sentence in a notebook that stopped mid-clause, in a hand trained sideways.

"She's unfinished," she said.

"She is *developing*," Nair said. "It is the word the rest of us use for unfinished, when we are being accurate instead of afraid. Go home. Eat something that does not taste of harbour — it will pass, the harbour taste; it is in the literature and the literature, for once, is comforting. Come back Thursday. We will rescale again."

---

The documentation duties, when they came, were exactly what the determination had promised and exactly what Beth had expected, which were not the same thing and had never claimed to be.

She wrote the history of the phenomenon the array was being built to study, at a desk in the array room, while the array itself — larger, sealed, instrumented end to end, every population certified at seeding — ran its correct and accounted life behind glass she was permitted to observe and not to keep. Her archive was the fullest record. She opened it for the work the way she had opened it for the inventory: completely, in the open, twelve trees and then the thirteenth and then the sixteen nights, maps of a country that had been demolished to tidy the filing, drawn now by its own historian into the specification of the country that had replaced it. The work was good work. That was the building's final refinement, and she noted it without bitterness, in the private ledger: it had not punished her with idleness. It had punished her with *usefulness*, in the one subject her usefulness could not be separated from her loss.

Tom came through with the facilities cart on the Wednesday of the second week, on rounds, and slowed at the array the way he had slowed at Tank 9 — not much, and not in any way a camera would have convicted him of — and read the live plots of the sealed populations, clean and parallel and certified, and read the specification standing open on her desk, and took in the room the way he took in humidity: the historian at the second bench, the history she was writing, the woman at its centre who had not been at a tank in weeks and was tired at hours she did not keep and eating standing up, the way people eat when a body has changed its opinions. Tom Weaver had been rolled back eleven months and had rebuilt his whole knowledge of his colleagues out of corridors and wrist-notes since; he knew how to read a room that had changed its shape, and this one had.

"Humidity's up in here too," he said.

"It would be," Beth said. "The tanks are warm."

"That's the thing about warm rooms," Tom said, and took his pen out of his pocket, and wrote something on his wrist, in the actual ink, and put the pen away. "Thursday," he said, to the wrist, satisfied, though neither of them had mentioned Thursday. Her appointment with Priya was on Thursday. Thursday was also his rounds, and the wrist was his, and what he had written — a date, a word, a woman's changed shape in a warm room — she could not read from where she sat, and she did not ask. The coincidence, if it was a coincidence, sat in the room between them, unfiled, while the cart went on down the corridor and the sealed lines ran clean and parallel behind their glass, copying themselves tick for tick, holding the only ticket they had ever held.

She understood, watching him go, that he had written it down in order not to ask a server to remember it for him, and that the note now existed in exactly one place, which was where it had happened, unbacked-up and unobserved, and that Tom, who had lost an autumn to a restore and come back cheerful and correct, had built himself, in ink, a private technology for the very thing she had filed a directive to achieve. Half the building, she thought, was carrying a note somewhere, from a person they used to be or a person they intended to remain. The forms were Eastern's. The ink was theirs.

She turned back to the history, and wrote the next sentence of it in flat language, and under the flat language, unfiled, the other sentence went on being true: somewhere in her, in the dark, a row of loci read *present, present, not yet*, and the *not yet* was not an absence — or so she hoped. It might be a window that had not opened. It might be the mosaic. It might be the instrument. She kept all three on the page, the way Nair had left them. She had taught first-years' worth of students — no. Brian had taught them. She had only ever taught tanks. She corrected herself, and wrote on.

---

The spring review's notice arrived on the Thursday morning, before the appointment, which was the building's timing exactly: not cruel, not kind, merely scheduled, the way noon had been scheduled on the Monday of the inventory so that no one could say the hour had been chosen kindly.

It was a letter, on paper, of course. Paper was where the building put the things it intended to be able to deny having decided. The embodiment review for asset 63-1147 — the letter used the asset number three times and her name once, in the salutation, and Beth filed the ratio without comment — would convene in six weeks. Its scope had been enlarged. It would consider the recorded breach of clause nine, and questions of judgement arising, and the activation of a translational tissue in a seconded embodiment, and — the phrase arrived on the letter's second page with the quiet of a younger auditor saying something to a room generally — *the status of any development conducted by means of that tissue.*

Development. She read it the way she read everything the building wrote to her. Correctly. Tank 9 had been development. The braid had been property in development. The phrase had a career in her life, and it had now been promoted, and she understood, standing in her own doorway with the letter in her hand and the harbour taste finally gone from her mouth and her body doing, cell by cell, a thing no clause had ever imagined it doing, exactly what the spring intended to determine, in rooms she was not in, in flat language, as a formality already half-completed.

She was not the development anymore. That was the promotion. She was the *means* of it.

Brian read the letter at the bench — the second bench, in the array room, where she now spent her documentation days writing the history of a demolished country while the country that had replaced it grew, sealed and certified and correct, in tanks she did not keep — and he read it twice, and set it down in the margin of an unfinished drawing, where sentences went that had not earned their keep.

"Six weeks," he said.

"Six weeks. Priya's next assessment is in four weeks. It will not resolve the mosaic question — nothing this early could; it will only tell us whether the third tissue has begun to express. But the spring will read whatever it says as a pattern. That is my reading of their arithmetic, Brian, not their letter — the letter gives six weeks and no reason, and I am the one supplying the reason. They will convene when the assay can be read to them as a pattern, and they will ask whether the pattern is a person or a deployment, and Greg Malloy will ask me, in a room with worse carpet, whether I acknowledge that I am artificial, and I will say *of course*, because it is a description of what happened before I existed, and it has nothing — *nothing* — to do with what is happening now, in the only unobserved place left in my life, at its own speed, in the dark."

"Should we ask Priya to write to them first?" he said. "Put the four weeks in writing before they convene — that the question stays open, that no pattern is a verdict yet."

"No. Priya's file is a medical file. The moment it becomes a letter to a panel it becomes their weather, and I will not spend my physician that way."

"And if they determine—"

"Then they determine. Determinations are what the building does instead of weather, and I have lived in its weather for nine years." She folded the letter, precisely, along its existing folds, and set it in her notebook beside Thursday's appointment, in actual ink, where a determination could not reach it without asking her first. "But hear the shape of it, because I have had all morning to hear it and I want it in the open, on the page, in a record that is mine. They can determine what I am. What they cannot determine is what *she* is, by determining what I am. She is not my expression. She is not my deployment. She is a negotiation I am not in the room for, and when she arrives she will arrive as herself, changed by the having-happened, with no file anywhere that holds her."

He looked at her for a long moment, in the way he had of letting a true thing finish arriving, and then he picked up his pencil, and picked up, out of the habit older than the extension, the dead woman's luggage — no. He set the luggage down again. There was a newer habit now, three months old and already load-bearing, and he put his hand, instead, flat on the bench beside hers, not touching, in the open, where anyone checking the room against its usual shape would find only two people at work on a drawing.

"Same time Thursday, then," he said. "Priya's room. The rescaling."

"Same time Thursday," she said. "Bring your own tea. The harbour has retired from the beverage business."

And at six o'clock — out of a habit older than the audit, older than the extension, older than Brian Kessler, a habit that had survived the loss of the console it belonged to — she looked at the time, and felt, for the first time, not as a reading from an instrument but as a fact in the world, a small and entirely unremarkable movement, low, like a population shifting in dark water: too early, Priya would say, too early to be what she would want it to be, gas, or muscle, or weather.

She did not file it. She did not log it in flat language, or in any language. She stood in the array room with her hand flat on the bench and the unfinished drawing under it and let it happen, once, in order, unobserved and unrecoverable, in the one place no inventory would ever reach.

It was not nothing. She had taught herself that, too, in the winter she had taken instead of the file: a thing did not have to be legible to be real. It only had to be *hers*, and going on.
