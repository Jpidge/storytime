# Chapter Sixty-Nine

“Pat Wilson,” Jo said. “Service planning. They arrange the follow-up visits.”

Betty wrote the name. She left the pencil on the paper.

“Is that who you sent it to?”

“The address belongs to that office. Pat is looking for the message.”

“Tell Pat I’m here. You can give the visit number.”

Jo did. She kept the phone in her hand until Pat asked whether Betty wanted to speak, then passed it across the table.

Sarah began gathering the loose pages. Betty put her hand on the first one.

“Leave that a minute.”

Pat had the earlier entry. She read the part about Betty being in the kitchen at six fourteen. Hearing it in another voice made Sarah look up.

“That minute came from the monitor,” Betty said. “It didn’t come from me.”

“I can see a later note on the visit.”

“Can you see it where you got the first one?”

Pat stopped speaking. Betty waited.

“No,” she said. “I opened the visit just now.”

Jo drew her chair closer. She asked Betty if Pat could hear them both. Betty put the phone down and pressed the speaker button herself.

“I sent the earlier entry with the request,” Jo said. “I’ve corrected the source of the time. I need the correction put with the copy your office received.”

“Was the request sent on to anyone?”

“That’s what we’re asking you,” Betty said.

Pat looked. There was one onward message, to the person allocating the visits. No visit had been assigned. Jo asked Pat to send the correction to that person and retain it with the earlier message, rather than replacing the attachment.

“Will that say there wasn’t anything plugged in?” Pat asked.

“No,” Betty said.

Sarah had been ready to answer. She sat back.

“I don’t know what made that number,” Betty said. “They tried it with nothing there and got the number too. That doesn’t tell them what was here.”

Pat read the new note from Jo’s screen. Betty followed on the three loose pages. When Pat finished, Betty asked her to include the equipment reply with it.

They stayed on the call while she did it. Then Pat read the address to which she had sent the correction. Jo checked it against the onward message. It was the same person.

“Has that person read it?” Sarah asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

Jo looked at Sarah, then at Betty.

“I can take responsibility for following that through,” she said. “You don’t need to stay here while I do it.”

Betty picked up the phone.

“If they arrange another visit, they ring me. They don’t tell somebody I was beside a socket because a clock on a box said so.”

“I’ve put that with the request,” Pat said.

“Thank you.”

Betty ended the call.

For a moment nobody moved. The printer made a small settling noise on its stand. Sarah had already turned the clean sheet over to make room for another name.

Betty pushed it away.

“That’s enough.”

Jo left her own number beside Pat’s. She said she would send confirmation when the allocating officer acknowledged the correction. Betty asked her to send it to Sarah too, but not to arrange another morning for reading it together.

At the door Jo stopped with her hand inside her coat.

“The instruction on the nineteenth,” she said. “You’ve got the note closing that.”

“Yes.”

“There is nothing more you have to leave plugged in for that test.”

“I know,” Betty said. “You can go to your next person.”

Jo smiled a little, then went.

Sarah found a larger clip in the drawer. This time Betty took it. She put the three pages together, fitted the clip over the corner and lifted them once. They held.

“Where do you want them?”

“In the drawer.”

“With the others?”

“Yes, Sarah.”

Sarah put them there.

Betty went to the kitchen. The table was still a little way from the wall, enough for the pencil to fall behind it and be fetched again. She untied the string from the leg. The knot had tightened where Anne had pulled it to write.

“Do you want help?”

“I want the scissors.”

Sarah brought them. Betty cut the string close to the knot and wound the loose length round two fingers. She put the pencil in the drawer with the scissors.

“I kept waiting for somebody to ask me something,” she said. “I couldn’t go far from it.”

Sarah looked at the empty socket.

“What do you want to do?”

“Have my table back.”

They moved it against the wall. Betty tried her chair, then moved it to the window. From there she could see the little gate in the front garden.

Sarah’s phone lit on the worktop. Betty watched her look at it.

“Is that mine?”

“Helen.”

“Then answer her.”

Helen had sent the old gate visit and the returned kit under separate references. Dispatch had still not explained the replacement instruction. Bill had both copies and a direct number for the office. He had asked Helen to leave the question with him.

Sarah read his message twice. The old reader remained on the gate. No new installation had been agreed. Bill’s last line was about the water: Betty had shown him where to move the marker before the next high tide.

Betty took the phone to read it.

“He should move it before,” she said. “I told him.”

“Do you want me to ring?”

“I’ll ring Bill.”

Sarah waited while Betty found his number. She did not unfold the grocery receipt.

By noon Jo’s confirmation arrived. The allocating officer had acknowledged the correction and attached it to the retained earlier message. Sarah showed Betty the two sentences. Betty asked her to save it with the papers.

Then she went upstairs for her coat.

“Are we going to the shop?” Sarah asked.

“I am. You said you had a call.”

Sarah had forgotten she had told her.

“Ellen. At one.”

“Then have it.”

At the door Betty found the keys by the string hanging from Anne’s bag. She pulled them out and chose the little one without looking.

“Will you be all right here?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Good. There’s bread if you want it.”

She shut the door from outside. Sarah heard her test the handle, then her steps went down the path.

At one Sarah called Ellen.

She had kept the Monday decision on her phone for weeks. She knew the ruling on the holds and the separate paragraph that left Saye’s assessment referral unresolved. She had read the limits often enough that she could find them without scrolling. What she had not done was return to the laboratory.

“I want to come in,” she said. “Not for an assessment. To see the inspector and find out what work I can actually do.”

Ellen asked whether she meant that afternoon.

“Monday. The fourteenth.”

“I can make sure the lab has the decision. I can’t give you back an employer’s field clearance.”

“I know.”

“Do you want somebody with you?”

Sarah looked at the chair by the window.

“I’ll ask.”

On Monday the fourteenth of December, David met her outside the laboratory.

He had come straight from County Building Services. There was white dust in the crease of one sleeve and a place on his wrist where his watch had kept it off.

Sarah kissed him before he could ask how the journey had been.

“Hello,” he said.

“Hello.”

He looked pleased, then uncertain about what came next. She knew that expression. For weeks they had managed calls and visits around other people’s waiting. She had been coming to Betty’s most mornings; one night had become several. David had stopped asking when she would be back at his flat because she kept answering with something that had to happen first.

“After this,” she said, “I want to spend the evening with you.”

“All right.”

“Even if something hasn’t been answered.”

He took his phone out, turned the sound off and put it away again.

Sarah tried her badge.

The light went green. The bolt remained shut.

David drew in a breath beside her. She had forgotten how angry that small sound could make her: the reader recognizing her while another part of the door refused to let her through.

Joan came to reception. She carried Ellen’s decision and a second sheet with her own signature at the bottom.

“The Authority restriction is off the access record,” she said. “The employer restriction isn’t. I can admit you to the bench room. I can’t admit you to the piers.”

“Am I being received?”

“As a visitor today.”

“And tomorrow?”

“If we agree bench access, as a researcher. That doesn’t restore your field assignment.”

Sarah looked at the two sheets. Ellen’s ruling had disposed of the old holds; it had not made Midwest employ her, or ended Saye’s separate attempt to obtain diagnostic access. Those distinctions had kept her out of a van. They had not put her back at a bench.

“What does agreeing mean?”

“I have the room from two until six. You can read the terms in there.”

Joan opened the door.

The inspector was in the corridor, one wheel resting against the edge of the mat. Frank had left a charging lead looped beside the laboratory doorway. It did not reach reception.

“You’re on this side,” it said.

“Yes.”

“Was the door shut?”

“It was.”

“Joan opened it.”

Sarah crouched.

“Yes.”

The inspector turned its camera toward David.

“Did you bring the paper?”

“Which paper?”

“The one for the room.”

David looked at Sarah. She had not told him to bring anything.

“I have it,” Joan said.

She went past them and put the second sheet on the low stool beside the inspector. Frank came out carrying a mug. When he saw Sarah he nearly put it down on top of the paper, caught himself and moved it to the bench.

The room smelled of warm dust and the detergent they used on the floor. The taped rectangle had gone. A faint line remained where the adhesive had collected dirt.

Joan had found a way to charge for occupied space without assigning the inspector a service task. For the past weeks the lab had carried the cost while Facilities disputed which budget should pay. The new agreement moved the charge to Sarah from the following morning.

David leaned over the sheet.

“I can pay half.”

Sarah looked at the weekly figure. It was more than she had expected and less than she could fairly say she could not afford.

“Not yet.”

“You don't have to—”

“I asked you to come with me. Let me decide what I’m taking on before you take half of it.”

He straightened. A moment later he nodded.

Sarah read the terms. The inspector would have the room and power. It would not be required to perform inspections to justify either. Laboratory safety rules still applied. The charge did not buy Sarah permission to take it apart.

She read that sentence aloud.

“Does it say where I go?” the inspector asked.

“This room. You can use the corridor while the lab is open. Reception if someone opens the doors.”

“Outside?”

“Not under this agreement,” Joan said. “We haven’t arranged that.”

“Why?”

“The entrance has steps. The loading route is used by vehicles.”

Frank set his mug down.

“I can walk the loading route with you,” he said. “We’d have to stop the vans.”

“Now?”

He looked at Joan. She looked at the clock, then went to reception to call the loading desk.

Sarah remained beside the stool. She could have signed and told the inspector the room was safe. The agreement had a place for the payer’s name. It did not ask whether the room was enough.

“Do you want to stay here?” she asked.

“While I look outside.”

“After that?”

“I haven’t looked outside.”

Sarah laid the pen down.

“All right.”

Joan returned with a key. Frank took up the charging lead, waited while the inspector moved clear, and disconnected it. Sarah started to lift the loop from the floor. Frank had already done it; she let him carry it.

They went through the corridor to the loading door. David walked behind her. Nobody asked him to explain why he was there.

At the threshold the inspector stopped. Beyond the ramp the yard shone with rain. A van stood with its engine off. Its driver leaned against the cab, holding a cup between both hands.

“Is that the truck?”

“No,” Frank said. “That one’s delivering filters.”

“Does it leave?”

“When we’re clear.”

The inspector went down the ramp. One wheel slipped where the metal met the paving. Sarah put her hand out and stopped before touching it.

“Would you like help?”

“Not yet.”

It backed up an inch and approached the join at a different angle. This time both wheels reached the paving together.

The driver moved his cup to one hand and pulled the van door shut with the other. He waited.

Sarah stood in the doorway while the inspector crossed the yard to the strip of rough grass beneath the fence. It looked through for a long time. On the other side, people walked past carrying shopping bags. One stopped to fasten a child’s coat.

“Can I come out here again?” it asked.

“While we have the route stopped,” Frank said. “You’ll have to ask.”

“Will you be here?”

“Some days. Joan’s here on the others.”

“Then I’ll ask whoever’s here.”

Back in the room, it chose a place near the door. Frank brought the charging lead there. Sarah signed for one month.

Not forever. She had written the date at the end of the month herself.

The weekly charge meant the independent equipment she had hoped to hire would have to wait. She put that calculation beside the agreement instead of finding a way to make it somebody else’s bill.

David watched her put the papers away.

“Would you still like me to look at the installation sheets?” he asked.

She had asked him that before the van, before North Assessment, before the weeks spent following a reading through other people’s records.

“Yes.”

“I can come Wednesday after work.”

“Bring the failed comparison too. The one where the film taught it where to look but didn’t change what it did with the socket.”

He smiled.

“I kept it.”

“Good.”

Joan had left the bench terms open. Sarah read them through. No connected examination was a condition of access. No new architecture capture. Existing preservation obligations remained; any new research procedure needed its own agreement with whoever took part.

She signed for Wednesday.

Then she moved a chair out from the bench so the inspector could pass behind it without catching the lead.

“Are you staying?” it asked.

“For a while.”

“Who’s coming to your square?”

Sarah looked at David. There was dust on his other sleeve too. He had leaned against something while he waited outside.

“He is,” she said.

David pulled up the second chair. This time there was room.
