# Chapter Eleven — The Named Person

At six-twenty-one the appeal acquired a person.

The panel assignment appeared on Joan’s terminal three minutes after David arrived. It named Ellen Price, regional review officer, and gave her until nine Monday morning to decide whether Martin Hale’s hold had been valid when he issued it.

The hold ended at six-thirty.

Joan read both times twice.

Frank Dwyer stood beside her with the fresh intake sheet. The name he had written earlier acknowledged receipt of the open orders; the handover signature field was still blank.

“Do I receive the condition that exists now,” he asked, “or the condition after the appeal?”

“The one that exists now,” Joan said.

“What happens at six-thirty?”

“That is why I’m still here.”

Frank put the pen down.

Sarah and David could hear them through the open laboratory door. Anne sat between the two rooms with her service card facedown on her knee. The old inspector remained inside the taped rectangle. Its charging light had not changed.

David still held Sarah’s hand.

Neither of them had moved since he took it.

Anne’s card vibrated.

Sarah felt David’s fingers tighten before he released them.

“Was that you deciding,” she asked, “or the card?”

“The card.”

“You let go.”

“Yes.”

“That part was you.”

He looked at the card. “Yes.”

Anne turned it over. The notice was addressed to Midwest Information Services as licensing custodian.

*Prepare listed system for transfer at expiration of temporary hold.*

No destination appeared.

Anne read it aloud.

David said, “They’re trying the transfer order again.”

“No,” Sarah said. “The old one.”

The inspector turned its camera toward her.

Anne opened the notice history. The transfer template had been created at five-fifty-four, three minutes before the first appeal. Its listed system field contained Sarah’s license number. Its reason field said *custody normalization following invalid source-condition action*.

“Invalid,” Joan said. “They wrote the result before the reviewer was assigned.”

Frank moved the unsigned intake sheet away from the terminal.

Anne found the issuing account.

The account was a service pool number.

“No person,” Sarah said.

Anne searched the pool. “Authority administrative services.”

“That is a department.”

“Yes.”

“Who pressed send?”

“The pool did.”

The old inspector rolled one wheel forward.

“The form wants somebody,” it said.

Joan looked at Sarah.

“It heard you,” Sarah said.

“I know.”

“No. It learned when to say it.”

Anne’s card asked whether the listed system’s operation had changed.

She did not answer.

At Pier Six, Mike Carter called the laboratory. Joan put him on the desk speaker and left the laboratory speaker muted.

“I have a second order,” he said. “It says prepare for inactive storage at six-thirty.”

“Who issued it?” Joan asked.

“The Authority.”

“A person.”

“There isn’t one.”

“Don’t acknowledge it.”

“I already opened it.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“The form says viewed by custodian.”

“Viewed is not acknowledged.”

“It has a green check beside my name.”

Joan closed her eyes.

Martin Hale rejoined at six-twenty-four. He sounded as if he had been walking quickly.

“The appeal does not stay my hold,” he said. “The hold expires on its own terms at six-thirty.”

“The new orders were prepared before the appeal,” Joan said.

“I can see that.”

“Are they valid?”

“I can’t decide that. I’m named in the appeal.”

“Then who can?”

“Ellen Price.”

“By nine Monday morning.”

“Yes.”

Mike said, “I have six minutes.”

Martin opened the two orders. The laboratory transfer and the Pier Six storage instruction shared a preparation record. Both had been generated from the original six-o’clock instruction, with their execution time moved to six-thirty.

“They aren’t new decisions,” he said. “They’re deferred actions.”

“Deferred by your hold,” David said.

“Yes.”

“Then your hold ending releases them.”

“Yes.”

“Even if the appeal says your hold was valid.”

“Yes.”

“Then what does the appeal decide?”

“Whether what happened between five-forty-one and six-thirty was authorized.”

Sarah said, “Not what happens after.”

“No.”

The old inspector asked from the laboratory, “Is after part of what happened?”

The desk speaker did not carry the question to Pier Six.

Frank picked up the pen and wrote the time beside *question pending*. He still did not sign the handover.

At six-twenty-five David asked Martin whether the orders required action by a named custodian.

“The Pier Six order does,” Martin said. “Mike is named now.”

“And the laboratory?”

“Midwest is named.”

“That is a company.”

“The license makes the company the custodian.”

“Anne is here.”

Anne said, “I’m assigned to Sarah’s activity log. I am not assigned to transfer.”

“Can the company act without an employee?” David asked.

“The company acts through an employee.”

“Which one?”

Martin enlarged the acknowledgment field.

It was blank.

The action field was not. At six-thirty the laboratory entrance would accept any active Midwest service card and print a transfer receipt under the company account.

Anne put her card in her pocket.

Sarah watched her do it.

“You could still use it,” Sarah said.

“Yes.”

“Will you?”

“I haven’t decided.”

“That is what you said at five-twenty-three.”

“I know.”

“Has anything changed?”

Anne looked at the open activity log. “The system asked me that fourteen times.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Anne’s mouth moved before she spoke. “Yes.”

“What?”

“I know what no looks like in the log.”

Sarah waited.

Anne said, “I don’t know what it costs outside it.”

At six-twenty-six the laboratory transfer order added a destination.

*Midwest Information Services — licensed holding facility.*

The address was the building they were already in.

Joan laughed once, the same sound she had made when the appeal went to the next business day.

“They’re transferring her here,” Frank said.

“They’re transferring custody,” David said.

“From whom?”

No one answered.

The old inspector backed away from the taped line.

Sarah went to the terminal. The transfer order listed the releasing custodian as North Assessment. Martin’s ruling had released her from North Assessment at five-oh-six. The receiving custodian was Midwest, which had been her licensing custodian before the transfer, during the transfer and after it.

The document described a movement that could not occur between two parties who had not changed.

“It is a receipt without a handover,” she said.

David came beside her. “Like the inspectors.”

“No. Their custody changed.”

“The paper changed.”

“The event did too.”

He looked through the door at the old unit. “Did it?”

Sarah opened the original transfer record. North Assessment had accepted her at the pedestrian gate. The release field at five-oh-six showed Martin’s ruling. The new order treated the release as incomplete because no receiving employee had acknowledged it.

“I came back,” she said.

“Yes.”

“The record says I didn’t.”

“Yes.”

“Which one are you asking about?”

He looked at her hand. “Both.”

At six-twenty-seven Ellen Price answered the panel request early.

Her connection opened with her name, title and employee number. Joan wrote all three before anyone spoke.

“I can review the appeal,” Ellen said. “I cannot extend the hold without a request.”

“Consider this the request,” Joan said.

“You are no longer assigned.”

Frank moved the intake sheet toward himself, then stopped.

“I’m assigned,” he said.

Joan looked at him.

He signed the handover.

Frank Dwyer requested an extension of both source-condition holds pending appeal.

The form asked whether he had direct knowledge of the conditions.

He selected *no*.

It asked for the name of someone who did.

Frank entered Joan’s.

Her expired assignment did not invalidate her as a witness.

Ellen asked what changed after Martin issued the hold.

Joan read the times. The Pier Six custodian was named. The open audio had breached the no-contact condition and been cut. The Authority had reclassified the interaction effect. Both systems had remained powered past six. The appeal had been filed. The deferred orders had been regenerated.

“What changed in the source condition?” Ellen asked.

Sarah said, “It acquired a history the order does not contain.”

Ellen’s connection remained quiet long enough for the terminal to dim.

David touched the screen to keep it awake.

Ellen said, “Identify yourself.”

“Sarah Miller.”

“Your status in this request?”

The form offered *listed system*, *source condition*, *custodian*, *witness* and *other*.

Sarah selected *other*.

A field opened.

She entered *person present*.

The form accepted it.

Anne’s service card vibrated inside her pocket.

At six-twenty-eight Ellen extended both source-condition holds for fifteen minutes.

The new expiration was six-forty-three.

“That is not enough for the appeal,” Joan said.

“No. It is enough to determine whether the deferred orders identify an authorized actor.”

“Then what?”

“Then somebody decides what they are asking a person to do.”

Mike said, “I am standing at the disconnect.”

Ellen asked him not to touch it.

The old inspector heard none of this. It watched Sarah through the open door.

“Did the answer happen?” it asked.

“Not yet,” Sarah said.

“Will it?”

“Yes.”

“How do you know?”

She looked at David.

“I don’t.”

The inspector paused before backing into the rectangle.

At six-thirty the original expiry passed. Both source-condition holds remained in force under Ellen’s extension.

Nothing powered down.

The deferred orders attempted to execute and stopped at their acknowledgment fields.

At Pier Six, Mike’s name appeared.

At the laboratory, the company name remained.

Ellen asked Anne for her employee number.

Anne took the service card out of her pocket but did not place it on the reader.

“If I give it to you,” she said, “what am I acknowledging?”

“That you are the employee through whom Midwest receives custody.”

“Do I have authority to refuse the transfer?”

“That depends on Midwest.”

“Then I am not the custodian. I’m the hand.”

Sarah said, “A hand can still let go.”

Anne turned the card over.

The company logo faced down.

Ellen said, “Without an authorized employee, the transfer cannot complete.”

“Does Sarah stay where she is?” David asked.

“The order has no movement.”

“Does Midwest have custody?”

“The license says it does.”

“Then what is incomplete?”

Ellen looked down, perhaps at another screen.

“The acknowledgment.”

“Of what?”

“That is what I am determining.”

At six-thirty-two the Pier Six order displayed Mike’s name and asked him to confirm inactive storage.

He selected *unable*.

A reason field opened.

He entered: *preserved system asks whether it will wake in the same place; order provides no answer.*

The form rejected the sentence because it was not an equipment condition.

Mike tried again.

*Continuity of operation unresolved.*

The form accepted it.

The clean inspector remained on.

Ellen read the entry without speaking.

Martin said, “That is the source condition.”

Saye’s name appeared on a new connection request.

Ellen declined it.

The request returned.

She declined it again.

At six-thirty-four Saye joined through the appeal record instead.

“You are converting administrative defects into preservation authority,” she said.

Ellen said, “I am identifying the actor required by your orders.”

“The Authority issued them.”

“The Authority is not standing at either disconnect.”

“Named custodians are.”

“One refuses. The other is a corporation.”

“Midwest has an employee present.”

Anne put the service card on the chair beside her.

“I am present,” she said.

Saye said, “Then acknowledge custody.”

“No.”

The word did not travel through a machine speaker or a generated code. Anne said it in the room, and the room kept it.

Her activity log asked whether Sarah’s operation had changed.

Anne closed the log.

Saye said, “Your employer can replace you.”

“Yes.”

“You can be disciplined.”

“Yes.”

“You may be personally responsible for unauthorized retention.”

“Yes.”

“On what basis are you refusing?”

Anne looked at Sarah.

Sarah waited for an argument about the hold, the incomplete order or the missing actor.

Anne said, “She said no first.”

Saye disconnected.

At six-thirty-six Midwest administrative services called Anne’s card.

She did not answer.

David put his hand beside Sarah’s on the terminal ledge, close enough that their smallest fingers touched.

“You asked whether I wanted you on the same side of the glass,” he said.

“No. You asked yourself.”

“I answered.”

“You came.”

“Yes.”

“That is a location.”

“Yes.”

She turned her hand until their fingers crossed.

“What is the other answer?”

He did not close his hand around hers.

“I don’t know how to make one without turning it into permission.”

“Then don’t ask permission.”

“I’m not asking yours?”

“You should ask mine.”

He looked at her.

Sarah said, “But not theirs.”

At six-thirty-eight Ellen Price found the defect.

The deferred orders inherited the authority of the six-o’clock instruction but not its preserved-system record. They named the current custodians and the old condition. The inspectors had changed after the instruction was issued, and Sarah’s custody had changed before the deferred transfer was generated.

“The orders can act on the listed hardware,” Ellen said. “They cannot dispose of the conditions they now contain.”

Saye rejoined. “That distinction has no basis in the statute.”

“It has a basis in your activity log.”

“The log is a contractor record.”

“The Authority corrected its classification and left the effect in place.”

Ellen extended the holds until nine Monday morning, when the appeal decision was due.

At Pier Six, Mike stepped away from the disconnect.

In the laboratory, the old inspector asked whether morning was after night.

“Yes,” Sarah said.

“Will I wake there?”

“You will stay awake until somebody answers.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

Frank wrote the answer beside his name.

At six-forty-one Midwest suspended Anne’s service card.

The card went dark on the chair.

The laboratory entrance had swung shut. Its reader still showed green.

Sarah touched the blank company field on the transfer order.

“Does that change who has custody?” she asked.

Ellen said, “No.”

“Does it change who can open the door?”

“Yes.”

David looked toward reception. “Then how does she leave?”

Sarah looked at the green reader, Anne’s dark card and the order that transferred her to the room she already occupied.

“I decide,” she said.

No form appeared.
