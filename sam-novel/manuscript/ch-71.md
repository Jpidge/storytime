# Chapter Seventy-One

On Saturday David took the wrong road to the water.

Sarah waited until they had passed the same closed timber yard twice.

“Is this part of the walk?”

“No.”

“Good. I’m cold already.”

He pulled into the yard entrance and looked at the map. She watched him turn it until the arrow pointed up.

“You can say you don’t know.”

“I know where it is.”

“That’s a different claim.”

He looked at her, then laughed. She had wanted him to. For once the wrong turn cost them nothing except ten minutes and the pleasure of blaming him for it.

It was the nineteenth of December. Along the water the reeds had gone pale and the mud held a thin skin of ice. David had brought a flask with a lid too small to serve as two cups. Sarah drank first, watching a man on the far path drag a reluctant dog away from something neither of them could see.

“Would you live here?” David asked.

“On the path?”

“Near the water.”

She looked at the low buildings beyond the bank. Some had curtains drawn against the winter afternoon. One window held a paper star with a bent point.

“I’d like a window that looked at something farther away than a wall.”

He nodded as if she had accepted a plan.

“Not necessarily with you,” she said.

“I didn’t say—”

“I know.”

They walked a little farther.

“I’d like you to ask,” she said. “When you mean it.”

He took the flask back. A drop had run down its side and darkened his glove.

“Would you like to live with me?”

She had not expected him to do it there, with the dog barking across the water and her own challenge still between them.

“I don’t know.”

“All right.”

“Does it feel all right?”

“No.”

She put her hand through his arm.

He let it remain there without treating it as a changed answer.

They walked to the footbridge. On the way back she stopped to look at the paper star again. The window belonged to a kitchen. Somebody had hung a wet cloth over the back of a chair.

*

By the first week of January Sarah had taken the laboratory’s smaller room for two afternoons a week.

The money came out of what she had kept for replacing a worn joint in her left hand. It was not urgent. She asked the service technician how long she could safely defer it, and accepted the shorter interval he gave her instead of the one she had hoped for. The appointment stayed in her calendar.

The room had one desk and a window onto the yard. She could work there when the shared bench was booked. She paid for it herself and gave David the hours when she would be there.

He offered to cover half.

“Are you using half?”

“No.”

“Then don’t.”

It came out more sharply than she meant. He looked at the room, at the desk with her coat on it and the kettle she had carried from Betty’s.

“I thought it would help.”

“It would. I still want to pay for it.”

He left that alone.

The contractor’s calibration record had arrived before Christmas. It established an adjustment, not who had made it or what had survived installation. Sarah wrote the narrower result into their work. Neither of them spent the holiday trying to turn it into a parent.

She had made a better set of tests from the little network. Most of the distinctions they had thought they could draw disappeared under a change of input. Two survived. She wanted to present those two, together with the failures, at the laboratory’s Friday discussion.

David said he could come.

“You’re at work.”

“I could ask to leave early.”

“Do you want to hear it?”

“Yes.”

“Then ask. Don’t tell them it’s an emergency.”

He came with his work boots still on, and sat at the back while she described what the tests could not establish.

There were eight people in the room. One asked why she had bothered if the original carrier could no longer be reconstructed. Sarah felt herself getting ready to defend a larger claim.

Instead she put up the failed timing test.

“Because I was going to call this inheritance,” she said. “Now I can show you a reason not to.”

Afterward she was angry with the questioner, then angry that David had seen it.

They bought bread on the way to his flat. He wanted to discuss an answer she had given about the controls.

“Not yet.”

“When?”

“Tomorrow. I want to be pleased tonight.”

He took the loaf from her while she fastened her coat.

“You were good.”

“You’re allowed to say which bit.”

“The answer you didn’t want to give.”

She considered that as they walked.

“I was good before that too.”

“Yes,” he said. “You were.”

At his door she took out the key he had given her three days earlier. He watched her choose it from the ring.

It had not come with an answer to his question by the water. They had agreed on that before she accepted it.

*

On the second Sunday in January she found his notebook on the kitchen table.

He had gone to buy milk. She was looking for the shopping list, which he had asked her to add to. The notebook lay open beneath it.

Across the page he had drawn two columns of hours. His working day occupied the left one. In the right he had written her name.

Underneath was a third column, mostly empty.

The first word in it was *care*.

She sat down.

When David came back she had not touched the notebook. The milk went on the counter between the bread and yesterday’s unwashed pan.

“What is that?”

He followed her eyes.

“I was going to ask you.”

“You’ve put my afternoons in.”

“So I could see—”

“What?”

“Whether there was time.”

“For what?”

He took off his coat without finding somewhere to put it.

“A child.”

The refrigerator motor stopped. Sarah could hear somebody moving a chair upstairs.

They had talked about developmental controls for three weeks. They had discussed how a living system might respond, what could be measured without choosing an outcome, what sort of collaboration they lacked. David had not once said *our child*.

She had thought about it alone. She had not known how much of that he could see.

“You’ve given me Tuesday.”

“I haven’t given you anything.”

“My name is there.”

He looked down at the page.

“Yes.”

She wanted him to cross it out immediately. Then she wanted to stop having to tell him every necessary thing.

“Did you imagine asking me before or after it fitted?”

He pulled out the other chair but did not sit.

“Before I thought it fitted.”

“Then why is my work the part that moves?”

“It isn’t. I was trying to find a day when you—”

“When I’m not paid.”

He was quiet.

She had paid for the room. He knew what it had cost.

“I thought you’d want to be there,” he said.

The sentence hurt more because she did.

“So did you.”

“Yes.”

“Your hours stay.”

He put his coat over the chair at last.

“I can change them.”

“Can you?”

“I can ask.”

“That’s what I can do.”

He sat down. For a moment she thought he was going to start again with the calendar.

Instead he closed the notebook.

“I wanted to know whether I was being ridiculous.”

“About wanting a child?”

“About wanting one with you.”

She looked toward the window. The plant on the sill had grown a pale new leaf. She had turned the pot one evening without asking him, then turned it back when she noticed the older leaves all faced the room.

“You could have asked me that.”

“I was afraid you’d hear another experiment.”

“And you thought a timetable would help?”

“No,” he said. “Apparently I thought arithmetic would make me less afraid.”

She did not laugh.

He waited.

“I want to be the person you come home to,” he said. “I want to come home to you. I’ve wanted that without knowing whether you want to stay.”

“I came back.”

“Yes.”

“Not because Betty sent me.”

“I know.”

“I don’t think you do.”

He pushed his chair back, but stayed in it.

“What am I missing?”

She could not answer immediately. She had imagined this conversation often enough to have very good answers. None of them survived being spoken to the man sitting across from her.

“You keep making a place where I can be safe,” she said. “Then waiting to see whether I’ll want you in it.”

He looked at her.

“I want you in it. Sometimes I want you out of it. I want to be able to say both without the second one meaning I’ve gone.”

“All right.”

“That’s easy to say.”

“Yes.”

They sat until the milk began to feel like something that ought to be put away.

David got up and did it.

Sarah took the shopping list from beneath the closed notebook. She added tea and onions. Then she put it where he would see it.

“I need to go out for a while.”

“Do you want company?”

“No.”

He nodded.

She put her key in her pocket with the others.

*

She went to Betty’s.

Bill was there, trying to repair the catch on a cupboard. He had taken the small brass plate off and was holding it up to the light.

“You’ve made it worse,” Betty told him.

“It shut before because it was bent.”

“And now?”

“Now it isn’t.”

Sarah stood in the doorway until Betty told her to take her coat off if she was staying.

They ate at the kitchen table. Betty asked about the laboratory, and Sarah told her about the room and the discussion. Bill asked whether she had made the two lines appear.

“Yes.”

“Then you’ve got something.”

“Two different things make them appear.”

“Ah,” he said. “Like this door.”

Betty laughed. Sarah did too, more than the joke deserved.

She did not ask Betty what to do about David. She had come there because Betty would give her an answer to the question she actually asked, and because she was tired of needing an answer.

When she left, the cupboard was still open. Bill had gone to find a different screw. Betty had put the cups back inside anyway.

At David’s flat the notebook was gone from the table.

He was making soup.

“I didn’t know when you’d be back,” he said.

“You could have eaten.”

“I did.”

She looked in the pan. There was plenty left.

“Good.”

He found a bowl.

On the table lay the laboratory discussion notice. He had underlined a sentence in her abstract. For one furious instant she thought he had spent the afternoon correcting it.

Then she read the words beside it.

*Ask Sarah what she wants to do next.*

“You could just ask.”

“I was going to.”

She sat.

“I want to keep the room.”

“Yes.”

“Even if we decide to live together.”

“All right.”

“And I don’t want to spend the rest of this month proving I’m willing to stay by giving things up.”

He set the bowl down.

“I’m sorry about the page.”

“I know.”

“I took my hours as fixed. I didn’t notice until you said it.”

She broke a piece of bread.

“What would you change?”

“I don’t know what they’d agree to. I can find out. But I shouldn’t come back with another plan for you to accept.”

“Find out for yourself first.”

He sat opposite her with his own bread.

She ate half the soup before she could ask him.

“What do you want about having a child?”

He looked toward the window, as she had that morning.

“Somebody at the table who hasn’t heard all my stories.”

“They might hate them.”

“They might.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“No.”

He rubbed a crumb against the table with his finger.

“I want to help somebody grow up. I want the years of it. I know that sounds—”

“It sounds like years.”

“Yes.”

“I might not be able to give you that.”

He stopped moving the crumb.

“You don’t know that.”

“Neither do you.”

“No.”

“I don’t want us to make a promise about what my body can do.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“I’m asking whether you want to find out what we could choose. Not whether you can guarantee the choice.”

She put down the bread.

“I want someone who gets to be new,” she said.

David did not answer.

“When I think about it, that’s what I want. Not somebody who remembers this for me. Not somebody who proves I was here.”

She looked at the abstract lying between them.

“I want to have a life that leaves room for a person I can’t explain.”

“That could be difficult for both of us.”

“I know.”

She reached for his hand.

“I’m not saying yes to an experiment.”

“I heard you.”

“I’m saying I want to ask whether we could have a child.”

He turned his hand under hers.

This time he did not say that he could help.

*

On Friday the fifteenth Sarah brought a box to the flat.

It held books, a second pair of shoes, the kettle and the little tin in which she kept spare fasteners. The kettle had been David’s suggestion. The laboratory had one; his was beginning to leak.

“Where do you want this?” he asked, holding the tin.

She looked at the cupboard above the counter.

“Not there. I can’t reach the back.”

He moved the tea down one shelf.

It was a small change. She watched him make it.

They had spent the week asking different questions. David had asked at work about reducing his hours and learned what it would cost. Sarah had asked a clinical research service whether an embodied machine could seek reproductive advice as a prospective patient, rather than be enrolled automatically as a source of experimental material.

The first answer had been a research invitation.

She declined it.

The second offered a consultation with a clinician who did not work for the laboratory and would have to assess capabilities neither Sarah nor David could establish. An appointment was not a promise that there was a safe procedure. They booked it anyway.

David had written down three questions. Sarah had written five. One was the same.

“What happens if we stop?” he read.

“At any stage,” she said.

He added the words.

She put both sheets in a folder, then left it on the shelf. They were going out. There was a place near the water she wanted to show him in daylight, and a window in its kitchen that looked over the roofs.

They could not afford it on either income alone. Together they might, if they kept the laboratory room and gave up some other things. She had done that calculation herself.

“Are we looking?” David asked.

“Yes.”

“Or deciding?”

“Looking.”

He handed her the keys.

At the door she stopped him.

“I love you,” she said.

He went still.

“I thought I should say it while neither of us was in trouble.”

His laugh came out badly. She loved that too.

“I love you.”

“I know. Come on.”

She locked the door behind them. The folder stayed on its shelf, and they went to look at the window.
