# Chapter Fifteen

At nine-twenty-three Ellen answered the question Sarah had forwarded.

*No. Do not use that definition pending clarification.*

Sarah read it standing beside the bed. David was asleep on his side, one hand open on the sheet. She had thought an answer would let her put the phone down. Instead she wanted to know who else had received it.

Midwest was copied.

She saved the message without replying.

In the kitchen the pan still soaked in cold water. She pressed a thumb against the loosened rice and watched it separate from the metal.

Her phone sounded again.

It was Anne.

*Are you awake?*

Sarah dried her hand before answering.

*Yes. How are you?*

The words looked prepared. They were prepared. She had promised them less than two hours ago, and keeping the promise did not make them false.

Anne called.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I know I said tomorrow.”

“You can call tonight.”

“I don’t need you to do anything.”

“All right.”

“They want an account of why I closed the log.”

Sarah sat at the kitchen table.

“What did you tell them?”

“I haven’t.”

Outside the window someone pulled a bin over uneven paving. Sarah listened to its wheels while Anne breathed.

“I keep writing it like a report,” Anne said.

“It is a report.”

“I know. But every version makes it sound as if I had a reason they’d accept before I did it.”

“What did you know?”

“That you didn’t want to go.”

Sarah looked at the bedroom door.

“You can say that.”

“They already know that.”

“Then you don’t have to improve it.”

Anne made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

“They asked whether my judgment had been affected by contact with the source condition.”

Sarah stopped moving.

The question was broad enough to swallow an afternoon.

“What did you say?”

“I told you. I haven’t answered.”

“Do you think it was?”

“Yes.”

Sarah held the phone tighter.

Anne said, “You spoke to me. I listened. That affected my judgment.”

Sarah put her free hand flat on the table.

“I don’t know how to put that in their box,” Anne said.

“Neither do I.”

For once neither offered a better sentence.

At nine-thirty-one Anne said she would try to sleep. Sarah asked whether she wanted another call in the morning.

“Yes. After eight.”

They agreed on eight-thirty.

Sarah wrote the time on a scrap of paper and placed it beside her phone. She did not put Anne’s account into *Friday*. The account was Anne’s, and she had not asked to have it kept.

When Sarah returned to the bedroom, David was awake.

“Was that Ellen?”

“Anne.”

“Is she all right?”

“No.”

He moved over. Sarah lay down without taking the phone with her.

He put an arm around her only after she lifted his hand.

For a while she listened to the traffic. She could distinguish a loose cover in the road by the double knock it made beneath each wheel. David’s breathing slowed, then caught as he tried to remain awake.

“You can sleep,” she said.

“I’m listening.”

“I know.”

He slept.

She had wanted privacy to mean something nobody could reach. Now she lay in his room with a call arranged for morning, a question waiting for Ellen, and two inspectors whose overnight condition she could check only by calling the custodians on duty. None of them was in the room. They were there anyway.

She turned toward David.

His hand slid from her shoulder to the sheet. She put it back.

At ten-oh-two the loose road cover stopped knocking.

She noticed the absence before she heard footsteps in the hallway.

Two sets. A pause outside the door.

Someone knocked.

David woke with the second knock. He looked at the bedroom clock, then at Sarah, and pulled on his trousers.

“Stay here,” he said.

She was already standing.

He closed his eyes briefly.

“Sorry. Come with me if you want.”

She did.

A man and a woman stood on the landing. Both wore dark coats. The woman held a small terminal; the man had a folded sheet of paper.

“David Bennett?” the woman asked.

“Yes.”

“I’m Carol Reed, Authority field services. This is Peter Shaw. We’re here to deliver notice.”

“Notice of what?”

“Preservation and availability.”

Sarah stood where both could see her.

“Who is it for?”

Carol looked at the terminal.

“Midwest Information Services, with service on the listed system’s present location.”

“How did you get this address?” David asked.

“It was supplied as a probable contact location.”

“By whom?”

“The licensing custodian.”

David’s hand tightened on the door.

Sarah thought of the empty address field he had refused to fill. It was still empty somewhere. The refusal had not made the address unknowable.

“Does this let you enter?” she asked.

“No,” Carol said.

Peter unfolded the paper.

“Does it require me to go with you?”

“No.”

“Then give it to me.”

Carol hesitated.

“We need to record delivery.”

“You can record it.”

“Name of receiving party?”

“Sarah Miller.”

“Capacity?”

“Receiving it.”

Carol looked up from the terminal. Then she typed.

Peter passed Sarah the paper without crossing the threshold.

It was thinner than she expected. Most of the first page identified records already identified elsewhere. The second required the custodian to make the listed system available for a technical preservation assessment at eight the following morning.

Saturday.

Sarah read that part aloud.

“The appeal is due Monday,” David said.

“This isn’t the appeal,” Carol said.

“What is a technical preservation assessment?” Sarah asked.

“It determines what preservation requires. Saye issued it under assessment authority, separately from Ellen’s preservation request.”

“So it is an inspection.”

“Under a different notice,” Carol said.

“Ellen is determining the scope.”

“You can raise that with the contact on the notice.”

“Does the assessment stop until she answers?”

“I don’t have authority to suspend it.”

“Who does?”

Carol pointed to a name on the second page.

Sarah recognized Saye’s.

Peter held out a narrow receipt.

“Signature acknowledges delivery only.”

Sarah read it. This time the sentence said exactly that.

She signed.

Carol recorded the time: ten-oh-seven.

After they left, David kept the door open until their footsteps reached the stairs. Sarah waited beside him.

“We could go somewhere else,” he said.

“They didn’t order me to stay.”

“No.”

“So why would we?”

“Because they found us.”

She looked at the notice.

“And if we leave because they found us?”

He did not answer.

They went back to the kitchen.

Sarah photographed both pages and sent them to Ellen, asking whether the assessment could proceed before scope was settled. She copied Midwest because it was the named recipient, then saved the paper beside David’s call reference.

Her earlier question had asked what she must preserve. This one asked who could make her available.

David sat across from her.

“I thought bringing you here would help.”

“It did.”

He looked toward the front door.

“It still did,” she said.

She reached across the table. He gave her his hand.

At ten-fourteen she set an alarm for seven.

Then, beneath the call she had arranged with Anne, she wrote a second time.

Eight.

The notice named the assessment office, not the flat. She could not count on being free by eight-thirty. She messaged Anne about the collision and asked to move their call until after the appointment; until Anne answered, she left the first time uncrossed.
