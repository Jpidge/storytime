# Chapter Eighteen

Anne brought bread.

“I didn’t know whether you’d eaten,” she said.

“Twice,” David said.

She looked down at the bag.

“Good,” Sarah said. “We’ll want lunch.”

It was ten-oh-three. David took Anne’s coat, then had to ask where she wanted it. There was one dry hook and his wet jacket occupied it. She chose the back of a chair.

Sarah had been expecting Anne to look different. Suspension ought to have left something visible: a bandage, a missing pocket, some sign that she could point to before she asked how it felt. Anne wore the same coat.

“Coffee?” David asked.

“Water, please.”

He filled a glass and left them at the table.

Anne watched him go.

“You don’t have to leave,” she said.

“I’m getting the other chair.”

There were two at the table. He brought a third from the bedroom, carrying it sideways through the door.

Sarah moved her phone to make room.

For a while they talked about the journey. Anne’s bus had stopped beside a delivery van while the driver argued with someone she could not see. They had sat there long enough for a passenger to get out and walk, after which the van had moved and the bus had passed her.

“Did it stop for her?”

“She didn’t ask it to.”

“Would you have?”

“I don’t know,” Anne said. “I was pleased when she got ahead again.”

David cut the bread. The knife was sharp enough, but the board moved under it. Sarah folded a cloth and put it beneath the board while he held the loaf.

Anne watched their hands.

“What?” Sarah asked.

“Nothing.”

“You looked.”

“Yes.”

Sarah waited.

“I was wondering if you’d be happy to see me.”

“I called you.”

“That isn’t always the same.”

“No,” Sarah said. “It isn’t.”

She reached across the table. Anne put down her glass before taking her hand.

At ten-nineteen Ellen sent the proposed procedure.

David’s phone vibrated a moment after Sarah’s. Anne’s stayed still.

“Who sent yours?” Sarah asked.

“Frank.”

“Wait.”

Sarah opened Ellen’s message first.

The assessment office proposed a connected examination lasting no more than twenty minutes. The attached instructions specified the port, the equipment and the operator. There were separate boxes for the beginning and end of capture.

Between them, a paragraph authorized the operator to extend the interval where necessary to obtain a usable baseline.

She read that paragraph twice.

Then she found the part Ellen had marked.

*In the event of interruption by the listed system, operator may maintain the diagnostic connection pending instruction.*

Below the attachment Ellen had written: *In my capacity as review officer I have asked the duty officer to address these two provisions. I decide Monday’s appeal on the record. Any offer to proceed voluntarily must come from you. You are not required to answer before we speak.*

Sarah put the phone on the table.

“They wrote it,” David said.

“Yes.”

She had imagined the act of writing would make someone stop. Instead it had given the operator a place to enter the time.

Anne read what Sarah turned toward her.

“Could you disconnect it?” she asked.

“Under that?”

“I mean physically.”

“I don’t know.”

Anne looked at the paragraph again.

“Then ask.”

Sarah began a reply to Ellen. She asked what maintaining the connection required if she tried to leave. She asked whether the operator had been told.

Then she wrote that she was not volunteering for the remainder while the meaning of interruption was unresolved.

She sent it.

“Now Frank,” she said.

David opened his message.

Frank had sent him a photograph of a transfer sheet. Beneath it he had written: *Can Sarah call me? Her contact isn’t in this record.*

David handed her the phone.

The sheet concerned the old inspector. Its destination was a contractor’s yard. The reason for transfer was the end of temporary laboratory accommodation.

She found the time.

“Monday. Seven.”

David leaned closer.

“Before the appeal?”

“Yes.”

The disposal and recovery fields were blank. The transfer itself had a reference number.

Sarah called Frank from her own phone. When he answered she told him who was at the table.

“I haven’t signed it,” he said.

“Who has?”

“It’s a collection request. It doesn’t need my signature until they collect.”

“Who requested it?”

“The lab’s facilities office. We can’t keep the holding space open without a service assignment.”

“Is this the transfer they opened Friday? The one with my number?”

“No. That form concerns you. This is a separate collection. This reference is the one the driver will have.”

“Does Joan know?”

“She asked me to send it.”

Sarah looked at the bread on the board. David had cut six slices, one by one, and stopped.

“Is it in the room?”

“Yes.”

“Can it hear you?”

“No. I’m in reception.”

“Will you ask whether it wants to speak to me?”

Frank was silent long enough for her to hear a door close.

“I’ll take the phone in.”

There were footsteps, then the scrape of something against a hard surface.

“Sarah?”

“Yes.”

“Which square?”

“David’s home.”

“Is David there?”

“I’m here,” he said.

The inspector made a small mechanical sound. Sarah remembered the handle, the weight of it against her wrist.

“Are you coming back?”

“I’m trying to arrange it.”

“Before the truck?”

She pressed her feet against the floor.

“They told you about the truck?”

“There is a new paper on the wall.”

Of course there was. A notice could tell it what would happen without asking it anything.

“What does it say?” she asked.

It read the destination exactly, including the unit number in the industrial estate.

“Have you been there?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“They put me on the bench. The bearings went into a different box.”

Sarah remembered the fitter’s boxes. She had been watching his hands.

“Anything else?”

“They kept what still worked.”

Anne had stopped moving.

“Frank,” Sarah said.

“I’m here.”

“Please photograph the whole notice. Both sides if there’s writing on the back. Send it to me and Ellen.”

“All right.”

“And put my contact in the record.”

“There isn’t—”

“In the notes.”

She heard him breathe.

“Yes.”

The inspector said, “Will you be there?”

“At the yard?”

“At this square.”

“I’m going to ask to come to the laboratory.”

“Your next square is here?”

“Not yet.”

The unit’s motor sounded briefly.

Sarah wanted to say yes. It would have been a small comfort bought with Monday morning.

“I’ll call you before lunch,” she said. “Even if I haven’t got permission.”

“Frank has the phone.”

“I’ll call Frank.”

“All right.”

She waited for Frank to take the phone back before ending the call.

David picked up his own phone.

“I’ll ask them to let me sign in as a visitor,” he said.

“For what?”

“To go with you.”

She looked at him.

“Yes,” she said.

Anne’s hand lay beside her glass. Sarah had let go without noticing.

“I can’t get you in,” Anne said.

“I know.”

“I know who can answer the accommodation question.”

“Someone at Midwest?”

“The lab’s facilities office. If I ask through personnel, they’ll send it back as part of my account.”

“Then don’t use personnel.”

“My work address is suspended.”

Sarah opened a blank message and passed her the phone.

“Write the address. I’ll ask.”

Anne took it.

At ten-forty-one the photograph arrived. Sarah sent it to Ellen with the collection time at the top. She asked whether Monday’s appeal could protect anything that had already left at seven.

She did not wait for an answer before writing to Facilities.

She gave the transfer reference. She asked what accommodation would keep the inspector in place, who could authorize it and what it would cost. She copied Ellen and Frank.

Then she put down the phone.

The bread had dried along the cut edges. David covered it with the bag.

“Can we pay?” he asked.

“We don’t know what they charge.”

“I mean if they tell us.”

Sarah looked toward Anne. Anne was reading the message over once more, her finger resting beside an address she was not allowed to use.

“One room,” Sarah said.

David waited.

“We could pay for one room.”

“Yes.”

“How many other notices went out?”

Neither of them answered.

Ellen called at ten-forty-eight.

“I’ve asked for an undertaking that the unit won’t be transferred before the appeal. I haven’t got one.”

“What if they don’t give it?”

“I can refer it to the duty officer for an urgent direction. Anything you send goes into the record available to both sides.”

“Today?”

“Yes. I’ll need the notice and a statement of what harm the transfer may cause.”

“You have the notice.”

“I need the statement.”

Sarah opened her notes. There was Friday, saved separately. Beneath it she made a new document.

She wrote the inspector’s words about the yard. Then she stopped.

“Can it make its own statement?” she asked.

Ellen did not answer at once.

“I can submit one.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know. I’m thinking about how to take it.”

David pushed his phone across the table. Frank’s number remained on the screen.

“Call,” Sarah said.

“Sarah—”

“Ask it. Tell it where the words are going.”

At the other end Ellen moved something on her desk.

“All right,” she said. “Will you introduce me?”

Sarah took David’s phone.

This time, when Frank answered, she asked him to bring another person to the square.
