# Chapter Three — Factory Only

At five forty the fitter set a saucer on the workbench and asked Sarah whether it mattered that there was a crack in it.

“Does it still hold a cup?”

“Since 2164,” he said. “Wedding present.”

He put it down beyond the reach of the clean inspector’s camera. Sarah had asked him to leave that inspector on the blocks overnight. Its dispatch tag was looped around one of the blocks, as if the block itself had a place to be at seven.

David came back from the office with his coat on. He had found a supervisor willing to issue a pass for the old inspector, provided someone could match the serial in the disposal certificate to the one underneath its axle. David had gone out to the bench to read it himself. The numbers matched. He told Sarah this with an air of having proved something in her favor.

“Did you enter the number?” she asked.

“Yes. It wouldn’t print otherwise.”

“What did it print?”

He held out the pass. Beneath his name, where he had expected to see *collection*, the word *surrender* was already dry.

The fitter took the paper by a corner. “Which printer?”

“The one in the office.”

“Oh,” the fitter said. He folded the pass once and handed it back. “That's the city’s.”

Sarah had put the old inspector beside the door so the fitter could use the whole bench. She went over and set it down on the floor facing in.

“Can you reach the bench from there?” she asked.

“Yes,” it said.

“Would you?”

It waited for her to move her foot. She did. It came past her and stopped at the bench leg, close enough to listen to what they were going to ask the other one.

David went to the door and tried his badge. The reader lit up green. The bolt stayed where it was. He tried it once more before he came back.

“Leave it,” Sarah said. “Come here and write down what happens.”

David took off his coat. He folded it over the back of the fitter’s stool, then took it off again when he saw the grease. There was a hook right above him. By the time he found it the fitter had cleared a space for the paper.

Sarah asked the inspector on the blocks what it remembered from the night before.

“A man picked up a coin.”

“Did he?”

The fitter said, “I did in the end.”

“Did you see the cup?” Sarah asked.

“Yes.”

She waited. The new inspector waited too. David started to write something, then lifted his pen.

Sarah took a terminal out of her bag. She brought up two stills from the pier, the red thread in one and the empty water in the other. She kept the terminal in her hands so the fitter could see that the only thing pointed at the new inspector was the screen.

“Tell it about Mr. Lewis,” she said to the old one.

The old inspector rolled forward. The blocks put the other one almost a foot above it. It had to back up again to see the screen.

“His boot’s turned,” it said. “There. Before he puts his hand out.”

The clean inspector turned its camera from the boot to the water. Sarah ran the clip. When the man went over the edge, the clean inspector was already looking at the place where he would land.

“Again,” David said.

“Different clip,” Sarah said.

The fitter kept a mirror on a bent length of wire for looking underneath a machine on the bench. Sarah borrowed it. She stood a short socket on its end near the lip of the bench and put the mirror flat below it. Then she asked the fitter to start the new inspector’s ordinary bench diagnostic. It read its dispatch tag aloud and said it was ready.

“Tell me if you see anything that ought to go in a report,” Sarah said.

She drew a rag across the bench. The socket rocked at the edge. The fitter stopped it with one finger.

“The socket moved,” the clean inspector said.

Sarah looked at the trace. There was one line for the socket. It bent where the socket rocked and went straight when the fitter touched it.

She put the socket back and set the old inspector where it could see the same stretch of bench. She drew the rag across again. The fitter put out his finger. On the trace the socket divided. One stayed on the bench. The other was marked in the mirror, where it would have fallen. It was there for less than half a second.

“Could you put both traces on the same page?” David asked.

Sarah did. He looked for a long time at the little line that went nowhere. Then he wrote down what each had done.

The clean inspector said, “In the first picture the man’s boot was turned.”

“Yes,” said Sarah.

“Will you put that in?”

David turned the paper round and read the line he had written about Mr. Lewis. The inspector listened until he finished and then asked him to read the second line too.

At the far end of the shop a bell sounded once. The fitter went to see what it was. Through the glass, David could see a city van at the vehicle gate, high enough to hide most of the road. The driver had his arm out. He was holding a receipt against the reader to stop it blowing away.

“Did you enter my name anywhere?” Sarah asked.

“Mine. The serial was the only other thing it wanted.”

She gave him her phone. The city had sent a copy of the surrender pass to the person who had acknowledged possession. Her answer from the night before was reproduced in a box on the second page, beneath the time at which she’d used the service gate. She could see where someone had put the two records side by side. The form had a space for objections. It opened after collection.

“I can tell him I asked for a pass,” David said.

The fitter came back from the glass. “He’s got one.”

“He can have mine,” said David. “I’d like him to look at it.”

They watched the fitter decide whether this was a joke. He went back to the door and called to the driver that there was a man who wanted to see him.

Sarah took her phone back. On the pass the destination line said *destruction*, followed by the address of the laboratory she had climbed out of the night before. She zoomed in. The word sat square over the old intake address, as if the two facts had always gone together.

The driver came in carrying a pair of short ramps. He set them against the wall while David explained. He listened to the end. Then he showed David where the form said *consenting party*. The name in the box was David Bennett.

“That’s who entered the number,” David said.

“Yes,” said the driver.

“I had no right to give consent.”

The driver turned the form over, looking for somewhere to put that. “Then you’ll need to give me something I can attach.”

David found the pen under the notebook. He wrote that he had submitted the serial, that Sarah had neither asked nor permitted him to, and that he had requested the pass in the belief that he could remove the unit from the yard. He stopped at *belief*. He had known the supervisor hadn’t said which way the unit would go. He crossed out the last clause and wrote what the supervisor had actually told him. The driver took a picture of both sides.

“Do I get a copy?” David asked.

“It goes to the listed custodian.”

Sarah’s phone buzzed on the bench. She left it there.

The fitter fetched the dispatch tag for Pier Six. “I’ve got another vehicle due in at seven. Do you need the space?”

The driver looked up at the machine on the blocks.

“That one’s mine until seven,” said the fitter.

“Then move it,” the driver said. He said it mildly. He took one of his ramps out from behind the other and gave the fitter room to pass.

Sarah looked down at the old inspector. It had backed itself against the bench leg when the driver came in. There was a dust-free arc on the floor where it had turned.

“Can you take this down to the loading line?” she asked the fitter, touching the new inspector’s dispatch tag. “Factory only, like you wrote.”

“They’re expecting a checked unit.”

“You’ve checked it.”

The fitter considered this. He lifted the inspector off its blocks and got his hands under the axle. As he went out, the old inspector said, “There’s a drop past the yellow.”

The fitter shortened his stride. The clean inspector asked how far.

“Two inches,” said the old one.

The fitter went out through the door. In the time it took him to get to the loading line, Sarah found the rack ticket and read it to the end. Helen’s hold prevented installation. It did nothing to the eleven other recovery tickets, and it did nothing to the stock record. A carrier could sit there for a year and the rack would still offer it to the next man who came looking for a board.

She asked the driver whether he was collecting the carrier too.

“Only what’s on the pass.”

“You’re returning it to where the carrier came out.”

“That’s the address they gave me.”

“Will anyone fit one when it gets there?”

“I don’t stay to see.”

She called Helen. The first time it rang out. The second time Helen answered with the careful quiet of somebody who had been waiting for a different call.

“Can you send me the recovery numbers for the other eleven?” Sarah asked.

“I can tell you where to look on the ticket.”

“Will that put your name on anything else?”

“No,” Helen said. After a moment she added, “My name’s already on enough.”

Sarah copied the rack number onto the back of the dispatch slip the fitter had left behind. She handed David the slip and the terminal.

“Find out where the boards came from. Start with the one we know.”

He picked up the terminal. “Where will you be?”

She asked the driver if there was a seat in the van.

“There’s a seat,” he said. “I can’t bring you back.”

She put her phone in her pocket. The old inspector came forward when she moved her foot. The driver lowered the ramps, and when he went to lift it Sarah asked him to wait and see whether it needed lifting.

It took the first ramp slowly. Halfway up, it stopped. Sarah could hear the little motor holding both wheels against the slope.

“Would you move the left one?” it asked. “The van’s weight is on it.”

The driver went around and looked. He got down on one knee to free the ramp.

Out at the loading line the fitter was reading the clean inspector’s dispatch number to someone over the phone. The old inspector waited while the driver made the two ramps even. When he stood up it went the rest of the way into the van under its own power. Sarah climbed in after it. The driver gave her a cargo strap, and she asked the inspector where it could bear the weight.
