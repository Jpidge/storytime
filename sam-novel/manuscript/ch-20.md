# Chapter Twenty

Frank held the phone low enough that Sarah could see the marks left by the floor polisher. They ended halfway along the corridor. Beyond them the light changed from white to the color of the sky outside.

“What’s that door?” the inspector asked.

“The stairs,” Frank said.

“What’s through the other one?”

“Reception.”

The inspector turned toward reception. Frank started after it, and the picture rose.

“Could you hold it where it was?”

“Sorry,” he said, and bent down again.

At the table David began to get up. Sarah touched his elbow. She thought he wanted to leave her alone with the call, but he reached past her for the bread bag and sat down again.

Through the reception door’s window Sarah could see across the room to the glass wall of pre-service holding. Behind that second pane was the chair where she had been asked to wait. Someone had moved it a foot to the left. A rubber mark still showed where one of the legs had been.

“Does that go outside?” the inspector asked.

“Reception does,” Frank said.

“Would you open it?”

Frank put his hand on the bar. A woman on the other side saw him and got up from behind the desk. She picked up a set of keys before she looked down. When she saw what was waiting with him she put them back on the desk.

Frank opened the door.

“Is it going out?” the woman asked.

“I’m asking where it goes,” the inspector said.

She looked at Frank. He lowered the phone another inch.

“Past my desk,” she said. “Then there’s a vestibule.”

The inspector came through and stopped at the glass wall, level with the chair inside. Sarah remembered looking out through that glass, trying to work out whether she was allowed to take the next step. From this height there was nothing to see of the desk but the wood across its front and the woman’s shoes underneath.

At eleven-twenty-seven the woman came around the desk. She had left her keys behind.

“I can show you,” she said.

She walked to the inner door and pushed it open. The inspector followed her as far as the mat. In the vestibule there was a second door with a strip of daylight underneath it. Rain had dried on the glass in arcs where somebody had wiped it with a sleeve.

“Does the truck come here?” it asked.

“Round the back. There’s a loading door.”

“Who comes here?”

“People who have an appointment. Sometimes people who want one.”

The inspector looked toward the street. Frank moved the phone to follow its camera, and for a moment Sarah could see only the reflection of his sleeve.

“I can show you the loading door too,” the woman said.

“Later,” it said. “Can I go back now?”

The woman stood aside. The inspector backed off the mat, turned, and passed the chair on the other side. Frank kept the phone low. They went the whole length of the corridor like that, past a man who stepped out of a doorway with a mug in his hand. He stopped to let them by and carried on.

At the laboratory the inspector waited for Frank to open the door again.

“Will you leave it open?” it asked.

Frank looked down the corridor. “I can while I’m here.”

“When do you go?”

“At noon. Joan comes in after me.”

“Will you ask her?”

“Yes.”

Sarah had been watching the dark strip across the threshold. She had missed most of what the man with the mug had done. She could remember only that he had waited for the inspector to go by.

“I said I’d call before lunch,” she told it.

“You’re still on the call.”

“Yes.”

“Then you did.”

Frank asked where it wanted the phone. The inspector chose the bench again. This time Sarah waited until Frank had set it down to say goodbye.

David picked up his phone when the screen went dark. He plugged it in at the wall and left it there.

“Come outside with me,” Sarah said.

“Now?”

“For a minute.”

Anne was at the sink. Sarah asked if she minded being left alone. Anne looked up, a knife in one hand and a bread crust in the other.

“I was going to eat this,” she said.

Sarah took David down to the front step. There was room for the two of them if one stood sideways. David put a hand against the brick above her shoulder and then took it away because the brick was wet.

“Did you see the man let it pass?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I keep wanting to know what he thought.”

“He was carrying a mug.”

She laughed. She had come downstairs without her phone and had only now noticed. The house was still there behind her. Nothing was asking her to come back inside.

“Stay a minute,” she said.

He leaned his shoulder against the wet brick. This time he left it there.

When they came back upstairs, Anne had put water on for tea. Sarah’s phone showed a message from Ellen received at eleven forty-two. The duty officer had asked the lab to pause collection while she considered the statement. The contractor needed to confirm that the instruction had reached dispatch. Ellen would let Sarah know when it did.

Sarah began to dial Frank and stopped. He was still at the lab. The inspector had asked him to leave the phone on the bench. She sent the news to the lab’s reception address and asked him to read it aloud, including the part about dispatch.

David’s phone buzzed against the skirting board. He crouched to read it and stayed there.

“It’s Helen,” he said. “She sent a picture.”

He unplugged the phone and brought it to the table. The picture showed the bottom of a sheet of paper, with columns of small numbers and a black binder clip at the edge.

“What am I looking at?” Sarah asked.

“An installation sheet.”

“Which one?”

He opened Helen’s message. She had gone back to the unit serial he’d read her over the phone from the bus stop. The current system showed where the boards were picked up. A storekeeper still had the old receipts in a binder, and one receipt had a second page.

David enlarged the picture until Sarah could see a pencil mark beside three consecutive entries. At the top of that column somebody had written *previous unit*. The first two lines contained serial numbers. The third held a date and the words *see attached*.

“Is there an attachment?”

“Helen says there’s a fold.”

He opened the second picture. A short strip of paper had been stapled to the back of the receipt. The paper was wide enough for a serial number and somebody had written two, one above the other. The lower one had been struck through. Sarah recognized it before she could say where from.

“Open the picture from the bus stop,” she said.

He found it. In the corner of the recovery manifest was the number of the inspector Frank had just walked down the corridor with. David set the two images side by side. The number on the manifest was the one somebody had crossed out.

“What’s the other number?” Anne asked.

Sarah looked at David. He had already begun copying it onto the back of the grocery receipt beside the breadboard.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But this time I have somewhere to start.”
