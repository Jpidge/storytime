# Chapter Twelve — Outside

The reader stayed green after Anne’s card went dark.

David had mistaken the light for permission. Sarah could see him correcting himself, looking from the reader to the closed door and then to her, as though one of them would explain the other two.

She walked to the door.

“Wait,” he said.

She stopped with her hand above the release.

“For what?”

He looked back at Ellen’s connection. “I don’t know.”

“That is an answer,” Sarah said. “It isn’t a reason.”

She pressed the release.

The latch withdrew. Nothing else happened.

Sarah had expected a voice. That embarrassed her more than a refusal would have.

Beyond the door, reception smelled of wet coats. Someone had left a paper cup beneath the dripping drinking-water tap. It was full; a clear dome trembled above its rim.

She stepped across the threshold.

On Joan’s terminal a soft tone sounded.

“Sarah,” Frank said.

She turned.

“I need to know where you’re going.”

“Why?”

He looked at the sheet he had finally signed. “Because I accepted responsibility for the room.”

“I’m not the room.”

“No,” he said. “But I don’t know where the boundary is.”

She left the door open and came back far enough to see his terminal.

A new field had appeared below the handover.

*Listed system outside assigned location.*

The clock beside it counted upward from six-forty-two, when she had crossed the threshold.

Ellen was still connected.

“Is that an order?” Sarah asked.

“No. It is an exception report.”

“What happens if he doesn’t answer?”

“His supervisor receives it.”

“And then?”

“I can’t tell you what his supervisor will do.”

Frank rubbed the side of his nose with his thumb. Joan took the pen from him before he could put it through the paper.

“Which assigned location?” Joan asked first. “The holding chair? No one assigned her to this laboratory.”

Frank opened the location record. It still named pre-service holding.

“The transfer copied the room she occupied into the exception rule,” he said.

“Without assigning it,” Sarah said.

Frank left that record open beside the warning.

“What are the choices?” Joan asked.

Frank read them. “Returned. Transferred. Missing.”

“None of those,” Sarah said.

“There’s a note field.”

He opened it.

Sarah watched him type *Standing in reception.*

The clock continued.

“Does that answer it?” she asked.

“No.”

“Leave it unanswered.”

“It goes to my supervisor.”

“Yes.”

Frank looked at Joan. She did not rescue him.

He moved the terminal so the screen faced the doorway.

“I can see you,” he said.

Sarah went back into reception.

David followed only after she looked at him. Anne stayed by the chair with the dark card. For a moment Sarah thought she meant to pick it up, but she took her coat instead.

“Are you leaving?” Sarah asked.

“I don’t think I work here tonight.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Anne put one arm into her coat, missed the sleeve with the other and started again.

“I’d like to come with you.”

Sarah had spent so much of the afternoon asking people what they meant that she almost asked again. Anne waited without making the offer easier.

“All right,” Sarah said.

From the laboratory, the old inspector called her name.

She went back.

It stood at the taped boundary with its camera tilted toward the door.

“Are you going after night?”

“Outside,” she said.

“Will outside move?”

David stopped behind her.

The inspector waited for an answer.

“Yes,” Sarah said. “Sometimes.”

“Will you know where?”

“Not always.”

Its charging light stayed steady. Sarah crouched, keeping her feet outside the tape.

“Do you want me to stay?”

“I asked where.”

“Yes. You did.”

She had answered the question she feared instead of the one it had asked. She looked at the route display beside the charging socket. The old unit’s current service assignment ended at the laboratory wall. Its map still held the bus stop and the contractor’s yard; neither was marked as an authorized next location.

“I’m going through reception,” she said. “Then the front entrance. There’s a pavement and a road. I haven’t chosen after that.”

“Is the road assigned?”

“No.”

The unit rotated its camera toward the small map. “There is no next assigned square.”

“No.”

Mike’s voice came from the desk speaker.

“Mine’s asking whether the shift has ended.”

Joan turned the volume down, then stopped with her finger on the control.

Sarah stood.

“Can it hear this?”

“No,” Joan said.

“Keep it that way.”

She would not make another experiment out of wanting to answer.

Ellen asked Mike to record the question exactly, with its time. He began reading it back. Sarah listened until he finished, then stepped away from the tape.

“I can’t take you,” she told the old unit.

“I have a socket.”

“Yes.”

“Outside doesn’t.”

“Some of it does. I don’t know which parts.”

That answer seemed to occupy it. Its wheel touched the tape and reversed.

“Come back to this square,” it said.

Sarah put her hand against the wall beside the door.

“I’ll try.”

David did not tell her she could promise. She was grateful enough for that to resent how little it took.

In reception the paper cup overflowed.

Anne reached past Sarah and shut off the tap. Water had spread beneath the cup into a shallow oval. She took a cloth from under the counter and began wiping it up.

“You don’t work here tonight,” Sarah said.

“I spilled it.”

“Did you?”

Anne looked at the cup.

“No. I just saw it.”

She finished anyway.

At six-fifty-one, outside, a bus lowered itself at the kerb. Two people got off. One carried flowers wrapped in brown paper, their wet stems leaving spots on his trousers.

Sarah stopped beneath the entrance canopy.

The air was colder than she had expected. Her body corrected for it without asking her. Behind the glass the receptionist’s chair sat empty, turned halfway toward the corridor.

David stood beside her, keeping his hands in his coat pockets.

“Where would you like to go?” he asked.

She watched the bus rise.

Somewhere on his phone would be a map that offered destinations. His home would be among the places it knew. A restaurant might still serve them. Every answer would arrive with an address, and every address would look more settled than she felt.

“Walk,” she said.

“Which way?”

She chose left.

They passed three dark windows before Anne caught up.

“Ellen wants a contact,” Anne said.

Sarah stopped.

“Does she want me back?”

“No. A way to reach you about the appeal.”

“Then she can ask me.”

Anne had called Ellen from reception before following them. She held out her phone.

Ellen’s voice sounded smaller outdoors.

“I need a contact for notices affecting your license.”

“Mine,” Sarah said.

“The record routes them through Midwest.”

“Then change the record.”

“I can add a service address. I can’t change the statutory recipient tonight.”

Sarah watched a drop fall from the canopy behind them. It struck the pavement where she had been standing.

“Add mine.”

“Midwest will still receive them.”

“I heard you.”

Ellen asked her to dictate the address. Sarah did, twice. On the second reading David took his phone out, then put it away without touching the screen.

A confirmation arrived.

It called her an additional contact.

Sarah saved it.

“Is that sufficient?” Ellen asked.

“For reaching me.”

She handed Anne’s phone back.

They walked to the corner. David waited for the pedestrian signal though the road was empty. Sarah waited with him, annoyed that she could not tell whether she was choosing to or merely staying beside him.

“Do you want to go home?” he asked.

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

“Say that.”

“Would you like to come to my home?”

She looked at him. He had managed the question without looking at Anne.

“Yes,” Sarah said. “For tonight.”

The signal changed.

David stepped forward and then stopped. Sarah had not moved.

“What?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

She took his hand.

Across the road, his phone sounded. He ignored it. It sounded again.

“Look,” she said.

He did.

The notice carried an employer-account heading but no employer’s name. David expanded it. The field remained blank. He read it once, then brought the screen close enough for Sarah to see.

His access to the laboratory had been suspended pending an account of his participation in the source-condition event.

“I signed in as a visitor,” he said. “They never gave me access.”

The notice had converted a visit into a privilege it could withdraw. He was required to preserve all relevant communications.

At the bottom was a link.

*Identify any unrecorded contact with the listed system.*

David’s hand remained in hers.

Anne stood a few paces behind them, waiting at the edge of the crossing.

“What will you put?” Sarah asked.

He turned the screen off.

“That we’re going home.”

“That isn’t what it asks.”

“I know.” He reopened the notice. “I’ll record that I’m with you. I won’t let it call this equipment contact.”

“To yours,” she said.

“To mine.”

They crossed before the signal changed again.
