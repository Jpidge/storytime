# Chapter Twenty-One

Helen called while David was still copying the number. He picked up with the pen in his hand.

“Did you get both pictures?”

“Yes. Can I put you on the table?”

“Who’s there?”

He told her. Sarah waited for him to add why they were looking. He set the phone down instead.

“Do you want me to tell her?” he asked.

Sarah shook her head. “I can.”

She told Helen about the number on the manifest, then about the collection request. She read the words that said the room had reached the end of its temporary accommodation. At the other end of the call, Helen set something down hard enough to make the speaker crackle.

“Sorry,” she said. “I had a drawer open.”

David turned the grocery receipt over and back again. “Can you find the other serial in the current system?”

“I tried. It offered to correct my search.”

“To what?”

“The one I’d already looked up.”

“Did it say why?”

“No. I tried twice because I thought I’d put a digit in wrong. Then I asked the storekeeper to bring the binder back.”

Sarah looked at the picture. The staple had gone through both numbers at the left edge. The pen strokes curved around it. Whoever had written them had done so after the slip was attached.

“Can we see the page before this one?” she asked.

“Do you mean the other side of the receipt?”

“The page in the binder. The one before the fold.”

Helen was quiet for a moment.

“I can ask him. He has lunch at noon.”

“So do we,” David said.

Helen laughed. Sarah could hear a drawer slide shut.

“Write down what you want to see,” Helen said. “I don’t want to get him back from lunch and ask him for the wrong thing.”

Sarah pulled the grocery receipt toward her. David lifted his pen off it but kept his hand there until she had the corner.

She wrote *the page before the receipt, both sides*. Then she crossed out *before* and put *underneath in the binder*.

“Why underneath?” David asked.

“I don’t know which way they file.”

He read it back to Helen. She said she would ask. After she hung up, Sarah could still hear the drawer shutting. It sounded like a drawer in any office. She wondered how long Helen had been standing there with it open, trying to make the search take the number she had typed.

Anne carried three plates to the table. On two of them she had put the heel of the loaf cut lengthwise. She gave Sarah the one with more crust.

“What does the correction actually do?” she asked.

“Finds the number it knows,” David said.

“It could have said no result.”

He put down his knife.

“Yes,” he said. “It could have.”

At twelve-oh-four Ellen forwarded a dispatch acknowledgment. The contractor had marked Monday’s collection paused. The acknowledgment applied to the reference on the sheet Frank had photographed. Sarah checked it twice against the photograph. Beneath it Ellen had added that the duty officer would review the transport request separately from Monday’s decision on the hold.

She sent the acknowledgment to reception. This time she asked if Joan could read it to the inspector when she came on.

There was still bread on her plate. She had taken one bite and put it down so carefully that the butter hadn’t touched the paper.

David pushed the paper out of the way.

“You can finish that,” he said.

She picked it up. “So can you.”

He looked down at his plate. Then he did.

At twelve-twenty Helen sent a picture of the page beneath the receipt. It was a ruled sheet with holes torn nearly to the edge. At the top somebody had written a date. The page was a year older than the receipt. A row of serials ran down the left.

David found the unfamiliar number near the bottom.

“There,” he said.

Sarah followed the line across with a finger. In the far column it said *return to service*. Somebody had written *south flood gate* underneath. A date had been entered in the next column and then crossed out. There was no date in its place.

David called Helen again.

“Does the gate still use this contractor?” he asked.

“Yes. I can ask about the job.”

“Could we ask whether the unit is there?”

“That’s a different question.”

“Yes.”

Sarah could hear the soft crack of a page turning.

“I can give you the gatehouse number,” Helen said. “I don’t know who’ll be on.”

David wrote it on the receipt. When he put down the pen, Sarah saw he had left a space between the two numbers, as if they might still need room to be themselves.

He held out his phone. “Do you want to call?”

She took it. The number rang long enough for Anne to stand and gather two plates. Then a man picked up. There was a low regular sound behind him. Sarah waited until he said hello again.

“I’m looking for an inspector that may have worked at the south gate,” she said. “I have a serial number.”

“Is it faulty?”

“I don’t know.”

“Read me the number.”

She did. A door opened on his side and the sound grew louder. He asked her to hold while he found the book.

David reached for the third plate. Sarah put a finger on its edge. He left it where it was.

The man came back.

“It’s in the book,” he said.

“Is it at the gate?”

“It was when I came on.”

“Can I speak to it?”

The man was quiet.

“You want me to put it on the phone?”

“If it wants to talk.”

She heard him set something down. He called from farther away, using a name she didn’t catch. She expected him to repeat it. Instead, close to the receiver, another voice said, “Which gate are you calling from?”

Sarah looked at the three plates, the phone in her hand, David waiting across the table.

“I’m at a house,” she said.

“Oh,” said the voice. “Is there water near you?”
