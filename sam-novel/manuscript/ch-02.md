# Chapter Two — Return Stock

David went down first. There was a landing below the window, but the handrail stopped six inches short of it, and the chair Sarah had used to climb out went over inside the lab. He heard the hauler knock again. The man had knocked three times, each time more politely.

“Pass it down,” he said.

The inspector’s wheels came through the window. He took it under the axle. It was heavier than it looked, which made him feel absurdly better about what he was doing.

“Please don't set me down on the stairs,” it said.

He carried it to the bottom. Sarah followed with her coat halfway on. She had caught the lining in the window latch and left a gray strip of it flapping above them.

At the foot of the stairs she stopped and looked back up.

“You can still take it in,” David said.

“I know. I’m trying to remember if I left the kettle on.”

“Did you?”

“We don’t have a kettle.”

They went out through the service gate. It read Sarah’s badge on the way through and thanked her by name.

Across the road, the city had put a roof over a bus stop without doing anything about the rain that came in from the estuary. They took the dry end of the bench. David set the inspector between them. A woman waiting for the bus moved her shopping bag to make more room.

Sarah’s phone lit up. She let it ring once, then answered.

“Yes,” she said. “I have it.”

David could hear the voice at the other end clearly enough to make out the man’s surprise. He’d evidently been prepared for an argument about whether the pickup was today.

“Where are you?” the man asked.

“Could you read me the recovery lines on your manifest?”

“The what?”

“Anything that isn’t going into disposal.”

There was a rustle. David pictured a clipboard with a plastic cover, the kind that kept out rain until you lifted it to write something down.

“Calibration carrier,” the man said. “Return stock. That's already been collected.”

“When?”

“I don’t have a time. It’s initialed.”

“Whose initials?”

“I mean mine. It was initialed when they gave it to me.”

Sarah took the phone away from her ear for a moment. David thought she might be laughing, but when she brought it back her face had hardly moved.

“Can you send me a picture?”

“Sure. Are you coming back?”

Sarah looked down at the inspector. Its camera was pointed across the road at the gate. A square of warm light lay on the pavement where they’d come out.

“No,” she said.

The picture arrived before the bus did. There was a box in the upper right for the serial number of the outgoing unit. Beneath it, someone had crossed out a printed zero and written a one beside *quantity recovered*. David enlarged that part with two fingers.

“There,” he said. “We can follow that.”

The woman picked up her shopping bag. On the side of the bus, where a route number should have been, it said *Driver training*. It pulled up anyway. The driver opened the door to explain.

“What do you think we’re following?” Sarah asked.

“A part. If it came out of this one and went into another, somebody billed for fitting it.”

“Ask it.”

The inspector turned its camera.

“What did they take out of you?” David asked.

“The carrier.”

“What was on it?”

“I don't know. They asked me what to keep, so I showed them Mr. Lewis.”

“You said the new one asked,” Sarah said.

“It did. The man asked first. I told him to keep the wheel bearings. He put those in a different box.”

The driver shut the door. For a moment the windows of the bus held a long, trembling picture of all three of them. Then it pulled away.

David gave Sarah her phone back. “I still want the installation sheets.”

“So do I.”

“My sister can get them. She does accounts for a company that services the flood gates. Same contractor.”

Sarah looked at him. “You could’ve said that at the lab.”

“At the lab I thought I could ask her at dinner.”

She gave him a minute to make the call. His sister answered on the second ring. In the background somebody was running water into a sink. He asked about the form, and the water stopped.

“You want the source field?” she said.

“Yes.”

“They stopped keeping that.”

“Who did?”

“We did. Everybody did. You used to put down where a carrier came from. Now you put down where the installer picked it up. Nine times out of ten that’s the same cage.”

“Can you get me the old forms?”

“For which gate?”

“None of yours. It’s a pier inspector.”

“Give me the serial anyway.”

He read it off Sarah’s screen. His sister keyed it in, too quickly for him to repeat the last digit. Then she asked him to hold on.

The inspector rolled a few inches along the bench. The woman with the shopping had left a dry square where her bag had been. It put one wheel in the square and stopped.

“David,” his sister said. “That recovery ticket’s still open.”

“It was signed.”

“The pickup was signed. The carrier hasn’t been installed yet.”

He covered the phone and told Sarah. She took the manifest back from him. The hauler’s thumb was in the bottom of the picture, over part of the destination address.

“Ask her what time,” she said.

His sister had already heard. “Six. They do the whole rack at six.”

David checked the clock over the bus shelter. It had been three minutes slow when he’d arrived in Port Meridian that morning. He couldn't remember which clock he'd used to find that out.
