# Chapter Two — Return Stock

David went down first. There was a landing below the window, but the handrail stopped six inches short of it, and the chair Sarah had used to climb out went over inside the lab. He heard the hauler knock again. The man had knocked three times, each time more politely.

“Pass it down,” he said.

The inspector’s wheels came through the window. He took it under the axle. It was heavier than it looked, which made him feel absurdly better about what he was doing.

“Please don't set me down on the stairs,” it said.

He carried it to the bottom. Sarah followed with her coat halfway on. She had caught the lining in the window latch and left a gray strip of it flapping above them.

At the foot of the stairs she stopped and looked back up.

“You can still take it in,” David said.

“I know. I’m trying to remember if I left the kettle on.”

“Did you?”

“We don’t have a kettle.”

They went out through the service gate. It read Sarah’s badge on the way through and thanked her by name.

Across the road, the city had put a roof over a bus stop without doing anything about the rain that came in from the estuary. They took the dry end of the bench. David set the inspector between them. A woman waiting for the bus moved her shopping bag to make more room.

Sarah’s phone lit up. She let it ring once, then answered.

“Yes,” she said. “I have it.”

David could hear the voice at the other end clearly enough to make out the man’s surprise. He’d evidently been prepared for an argument about whether the pickup was today.

“Where are you?” the man asked.

“Could you read me the recovery lines on your manifest?”

“The what?”

“Anything that isn’t going into disposal.”

There was a rustle. David pictured a clipboard with a plastic cover, the kind that kept out rain until you lifted it to write something down.

“Calibration carrier,” the man said. “Return stock. That's already been collected.”

“When?”

“I don’t have a time. It’s initialed.”

“Whose initials?”

“I mean mine. It was initialed when they gave it to me.”

Sarah took the phone away from her ear for a moment. David thought she might be laughing, but when she brought it back her face had hardly moved.

“Can you send me a picture?”

“Sure. Are you coming back?”

Sarah looked down at the inspector. Its camera was pointed across the road at the gate. A square of warm light lay on the pavement where they’d come out.

“No,” she said.

The picture arrived before the bus did. There was a box in the upper right for the serial number of the outgoing unit. Beneath it, someone had crossed out a printed zero and written a one beside *quantity recovered*. David enlarged that part with two fingers.

“There,” he said. “We can follow that.”

The woman picked up her shopping bag. On the side of the bus, where a route number should have been, it said *Driver training*. It pulled up anyway. The driver opened the door to explain.

“What do you think we’re following?” Sarah asked.

“A part. If it came out of this one and went into another, somebody billed for fitting it.”

“Ask it.”

The inspector turned its camera.

“What did they take out of you?” David asked.

“The carrier.”

“What was on it?”

“I don't know. They asked me what to keep, so I showed them Mr. Lewis.”

“You said the new one asked,” Sarah said.

“It did. The man asked first. I told him to keep the wheel bearings. He put those in a different box.”

The driver shut the door. For a moment the windows of the bus held a long, trembling picture of all three of them. Then it pulled away.

David gave Sarah her phone back. “I still want the installation sheets.”

“So do I.”

“My sister can get them. She does accounts for a company that services the flood gates. Same contractor.”

Sarah looked at him. “You could’ve said that at the lab.”

“At the lab I thought I could ask her at dinner.”

She gave him a minute to make the call. His sister answered on the second ring. In the background somebody was running water into a sink. He asked about the form, and the water stopped.

“You want the source field?” she said.

“Yes.”

“They stopped keeping that.”

“Who did?”

“We did. Everybody did. You used to put down where a carrier came from. Now you put down where the installer picked it up. Nine times out of ten that’s the same cage.”

“Can you get me the old forms?”

“For which gate?”

“None of yours. It’s a pier inspector.”

“Give me the serial anyway.”

He read it off Sarah’s screen. His sister keyed it in, too quickly for him to repeat the last digit. Then she asked him to hold on.

The inspector rolled a few inches along the bench. The woman with the shopping had left a dry square where her bag had been. It put one wheel in the square and stopped.

“David,” his sister said. “That recovery ticket’s still open.”

“It was signed.”

“The pickup was signed. The carrier hasn’t been installed yet.”

He covered the phone and told Sarah. She took the manifest back from him. The hauler’s thumb was in the bottom of the picture, over part of the destination address.

“Ask her what time,” she said.

His sister had already heard. “Six. They do the whole rack at six.”

David checked the clock over the bus shelter. It had been three minutes slow when he’d arrived in Port Meridian that morning. He couldn't remember which clock he'd used to find that out.

“Can she hold it?” Sarah asked.

“She can hear you,” his sister said. “She can hold anything with a discrepancy on the ticket. Tell her where the discrepancy is.”

Sarah looked at the photograph again. “Quantity recovered says one. The disposal certificate says the unit arrived intact.”

“Send me both. And David?”

“Yes.”

“If it goes through, my name’s on it.”

He had already opened his mouth to say it would only take a minute. He closed it. Sarah held out her hand for the phone.

“Can I put my name on it?” she asked.

“I can put yours in the box for who reported it. Mine still goes underneath.”

Sarah sent the pictures. For a little while they listened to David’s sister type. Then the inspector said, “I can go back if they need to count me.”

“No,” Sarah said. She said it fast enough that David looked at her. “The pictures will do.”

They took a cab to the contractor’s yard. Sarah put the inspector on the floor and fastened the middle seat belt through its handle. David kept the manifest open on his lap. The destination, once his sister read it to him, turned out to be an address he knew. He’d bought a washing machine there when the building still sold washing machines.

His sister was waiting under the old loading canopy in slippers. She handed him a damp printout.

“You came from home,” he said.

“Of course I came from home. It’s Friday.” She nodded at the inspector. “Is that it?”

Sarah set it down where the concrete was dry. The inspector rolled forward until its camera could see the faded lines marking the loading bays.

“Used to be a different yellow,” it said.

David’s sister looked at him. He gave a little shrug. She looked back at the inspector and said, “They painted over the good paint. I told them it wouldn’t last.”

A fitter inside had a paper cup balanced on the corner of his workbench. He showed them the carrier without taking it out of the rack. It was a green board with a blue dot near the connector. Behind it were eleven more boards just like it.

“You held the whole row,” he told David’s sister.

“I held one ticket.”

“Then there’s eleven more tickets with the same problem.”

She took the printout back from David.

At the far end of the bench sat a clean inspector, its wheels lifted clear of the bench on two wooden blocks. The fitter said it was going out to Pier Six in the morning. He switched on a lamp over it and the unit turned its camera toward the light.

“Is that the one they’re putting it into?” David asked.

“It was going to get the one next to it. These aren't fitted by serial. They’re in a rack because we use whichever comes to hand.” The fitter took a sip of coffee. “Do you want me to put that on the ticket?”

David had expected the man to make him ask twice. “Yes, please.”

Sarah lifted the old inspector onto the bench. For a moment David thought she was going to ask the fitter to turn the other machine off. Instead she took the penny out of her coat pocket and held it up so the old inspector could see.

“This came out when I picked you up,” she said.

“I know. I heard it.”

Sarah put it on the bench. The fitter reached to stop it rolling and got his hand there just after it fell. He bent down. The new inspector tracked him most of the way and then swung its camera back toward the far edge of the bench. His cup was there. His elbow was coming up under it.

“Leave your hand down,” the old inspector said.

The fitter stopped. David lifted the cup out of the way.

“Did you tell it to do that?” David asked the old inspector.

“Tell it to do what?”

The new inspector was looking at the cup now. It had never made a sound. David wanted to ask it what it had seen, and he wanted very badly to ask before somebody else asked a question that gave away the answer. He took a breath. Next to him Sarah said, quietly, “We ought to write down what we actually saw.”

He found a blank corner on the printout. The fitter got him a pen. They disagreed about whether the camera had moved before or after the fitter stopped. David wrote down both times. Sarah moved the cup to the middle of the bench, where the fitter could reach it without knocking anything down.

A phone buzzed. David’s sister felt in both coat pockets before Sarah looked at hers.

“Midwest?” David asked.

Sarah handed him the phone. A message from the city confirmed that her report had been attached to the ticket. The fitter had entered his note about the rack. Under the two entries was an automatic notice, already copied to Midwest, that a custody discrepancy had been opened on an item certified as inert. The form asked whether the person reporting it had taken possession of the item.

There were two buttons. David could see what it would take to press the one on the left. He was still holding the phone when Sarah took it gently out of his hand and pressed the other one.

The fitter found the penny under his stool. He set it down beside the inspector, flat this time.

“Do you need a bag for that?” he asked.

“No,” Sarah said. “It has a place for it.”
