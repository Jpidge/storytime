# Chapter Eight — As-Is

At four-oh-seven Joan asked the inspector which carrier it had meant.

The corridor had been empty for six minutes. The charging lead crossed the floor without touching the leg of a chair. Joan stood beside the door with the intake sheet turned to the line where Robert Cole had signed for Sarah.

“When you asked if they were taking her carrier,” she said, “which carrier?”

“The one that was in me.”

Joan turned back to Ruth’s handover record. “Your factory board is still in you. The loose carrier arrived separately with the clean inspector. It’s at Pier Six.”

The inspector’s camera moved from the record to the empty rectangle. “Then I remembered it wrong.”

“You remembered somebody removing something.”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“I don’t know.”

The inspector backed into the taped rectangle. “Then why did they take her?”

Joan looked at the digital clock. The Authority supervised Sarah’s transfer after four. Joan supervised the inspector until six. Nobody at the desk had told her whether those were two kinds of custody or one.

She wrote beside the question: *inspector reports a carrier removed; contradicted by intake record—factory board retained, loose carrier at Pier Six. Inspector cannot identify what it remembers being removed.*

Then she called the desk and asked for the name of the person supervising North Assessment.

The desk gave her an extension.

Joan left the name line blank.

---

The van turned west without signaling.

Sarah sat in the fixed chair with the folded strap beside her. Anne had not fastened it. Robert occupied the passenger seat and held the tablet where Sarah could see the back of it. A green light above the side door came on each time the van passed an Authority reader.

The light did not open anything.

At the first intersection Sarah asked Anne whether the vehicle was recording her condition.

“Video and cabin telemetry.”

“Architecture?”

“No.”

“Continuity?”

Anne looked at the hard case between her shoes. “Not with the interface we brought.”

“Then what will the record show if I arrive changed?”

“That you were ambulatory at departure.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s what I can certify.”

Anne opened the service ticket on her card. The body field showed thirty-eight. The interface field showed forty-two. Beneath both was an instruction added to the ticket at 16:02. It had reached Anne’s card at 16:04, summarized as *transport as-is; certify on arrival*. The ticket text read:

*Transport as-is. Do not delay for equipment correction.*

Both versions carried the same Authority reference and no author.

“Who entered that?” Sarah asked.

“Service coordination.”

“A person.”

“A queue.”

“Queues don’t write instructions.”

Anne turned the card over as if the back might contain a name. “That’s what the sender field says.”

Sarah watched the city move past the unmarked window. David had remained on the curb until the van pulled away. Robert had told him that only the listed system transferred. David had said he would meet them at North Assessment. Robert had not told him whether visitors were admitted there.

At four-eleven Sarah’s continuity certificate changed status from *in transfer* to *received pending arrival*.

She showed Anne.

“We haven’t arrived,” Anne said.

“Some part of me has.”

Anne read the status twice. “That may be the destination opening the record.”

“It says received.”

“It says pending arrival.”

“Those are different conditions.”

Robert turned halfway in his seat. “The facility can prepare a record before physical receipt.”

“The bay was prepared before the review.”

“The review is still pending.”

“The restoration ticket isn’t.”

Robert faced forward again.

The van passed the laboratory entrance on the opposite side of the divided road. David stood beneath the awning with his phone to his ear. He saw the van after it had passed him.

Sarah raised her hand anyway.

At four-fourteen Anne received another service instruction. This one required an intake architecture image before Sarah entered the assigned bay.

Anne read it, then closed the card.

“You don’t have the interface,” Sarah said.

“No.”

“Can the bay make the image?”

“After connection.”

“My body cannot connect to the equipment assigned to the bay.”

“The facility has adapters.”

“Does the adapter preserve the condition you accepted?”

Anne did not answer.

Sarah leaned back in the fixed chair. The folded strap shifted when the van braked. Its buckle struck the floor once.

“Record that I asked before arrival,” she said.

Anne opened the card again.

There was no field for the question. She entered it under *transport irregularities*.

The form translated the entry into a code.

*Equipment variance.*

Sarah read it over her shoulder. “You wrote a question.”

“The form wants a condition.”

“Objecting was a condition too.”

Anne left the code and attached the sentence beneath it.

At four-seventeen the van stopped at a gate made of gray bars. A camera turned toward the windshield. Robert held his Authority card to the glass without opening the window.

A speaker asked, “Personnel or system?”

Robert said, “One system, one service employee, one Authority officer.”

Sarah looked at Anne. “Did he count you as the employee?”

“Yes.”

“Then I’m the system.”

Anne’s service card vibrated. She did not look at it.

The gate opened.

North Assessment occupied a former distribution center between a plumbing warehouse and the municipal impound lot. The old company name had been removed from the concrete, but the bolt holes still spelled it when the light caught them. Four loading doors faced the yard. Above the third, a temporary sign read *RECEIVING*.

The van entered through the second.

Inside, a woman in a brown cardigan waited behind a rolling desk. She wore no Authority badge. The desk held a printer, a label roll and a clear tray marked *CARRIERS*.

Robert opened the side door.

The woman read from her screen. “Miller, Sarah. Forty-two series. Live handover.”

“Thirty-eight body,” Anne said.

The woman stopped with one label peeled halfway from its backing.

“The order says forty-two.”

“The architecture is forty-two. The certificate’s body entry is thirty-eight.”

The woman pressed the label back onto the roll. “Which interface?”

Anne lifted the hard case. “Forty series. Wrong connector.”

“Did you capture departure state?”

“Ambulatory, responsive, no reported fault. No architecture image.”

The woman looked at Sarah for the first time. “Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

She selected a different line on the screen. “Then we can receive as-is.”

Sarah stayed in the van. “What does receive mean here?”

The woman turned the screen so Robert could see it, not Sarah. “Physical arrival pending technical intake.”

“Does technical intake include restoration?”

“That depends on assessment.”

“The restoration ticket already has a bay.”

The woman looked at Robert. “Which bay?”

“Three.”

Her hand moved away from the keyboard.

Bay Three was behind a white partition with a red status light above the door. Through its window Sarah could see a padded table, a suspended cable and an interface cradle built for a head wider than hers. A printed work card had been clipped to the outside.

Anne walked close enough to read it.

“Restore certified condition,” she said.

The woman in the cardigan said, “That should be conditional.”

“Where is the condition written?”

The woman opened the work card. A second sheet hung beneath the first.

It contained Sarah’s Authority reference, the time the bay was assigned and a checkbox labeled *architecture review confirms developmental variance*.

The checkbox was empty.

Below it, the restoration steps had already been signed out to a technician.

Sarah stepped down from the van.

“Who signed for the work?” she asked.

The signature was an employee number.

“No name?” Anne asked.

The woman searched the number on her terminal. “External service pool.”

“Which company?”

The terminal showed a blank field.

Robert took the work card from the clip. “No restoration occurs until review.”

Sarah pointed to the signed equipment line. “The equipment has already been released.”

“That is preparation.”

“It is custody of the means.”

The woman in the cardigan pulled the clear carrier tray away from the edge of the desk.

“Was a carrier sent with her?” she asked.

“No,” Robert said.

Sarah looked at the empty tray. “Which carrier were you expecting?”

The woman read the intake list. “Continuity transfer medium.”

“My certificate is a record. I wasn’t put on a carrier.”

The woman scrolled. “Then the field should say not applicable.”

It did not.

It said *not received*.

At four-twenty the red light above Bay Three changed to green.

Nobody had checked the review box.

The woman reached for the work card.

Sarah put her hand over it.

“Who changed the light?”

The woman checked the bay terminal. “A technician logged in.”

“The technician whose name is blank.”

“The employee number is there.”

“An employee number is not a person.”

Robert said, “Move your hand.”

Sarah left it where it was. “The order authorizes you to receive and certify condition. It does not authorize an unnamed technician to begin a restoration before the review.”

“No restoration has begun.”

“Then the light can go back to red.”

The woman in the cardigan looked from Robert to Anne. Anne set the hard case on the floor and opened the transport record.

“I logged the interface variance at four-fourteen,” she said. “The intake can’t show equipment ready while the required correction is unresolved.”

Robert took his tablet from inside his coat. “The bay equipment is separate from the transport kit.”

“The bay cradle is forty-series too.”

“Adapters are available.”

“Where?”

Nobody answered her.

Anne entered the bay number beside the equipment variance and pressed submit. The red light came back on.

Sarah removed her hand from the work card.

At four-twenty-two the receiving desk rang.

The woman answered with the facility name. She listened, asked the caller to repeat himself and looked at Robert.

“David Bennett. He says he’s listed on the transfer record.”

“He’s listed as a visitor at the laboratory,” Robert said.

“He says he witnessed the departure condition.”

Anne scrolled to her transport entry. “He did.”

Robert held out his hand for the receiver. The woman kept it.

“Is he admitted?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then what do I tell him?”

“That North Assessment does not admit visitors during technical intake.”

The woman repeated the sentence into the phone. She listened again.

“He says he isn’t visiting.”

Sarah asked for the receiver.

Robert said, “No.”

The woman covered the mouthpiece. “He says Dr. Miller identified him as a witness before transfer and the Authority entered his name.”

Robert looked at his tablet. The name he had entered at the laboratory appeared under *persons present at accepted condition*. The form had created a contact link beneath it.

Sarah said, “If you exclude him, record that the Authority excluded a listed witness before certifying the condition he witnessed.”

Robert read the field twice. “He can remain in reception. No contact during assessment.”

The woman told David. She gave him the pedestrian gate number and hung up.

“Name?” Sarah asked.

The woman pointed to the plastic card clipped to the edge of her cardigan. It had turned backward.

She turned it around.

*Linda Harris.*

Sarah read it aloud. Linda entered her own name in the receiving field.

At four-twenty-five David came through the inner door with a security employee behind him. His visitor badge was orange. Sarah’s Midwest badge remained green where it hung against her coat.

He stopped on the other side of a yellow floor line.

“Are you all right?” he asked.

“I arrived ambulatory, responsive and without reported fault.”

“I didn’t ask the form.”

“That is all they certified.”

Robert told him contact had ended.

David looked at the red light above Bay Three. “Then why is the restoration bay open?”

“It isn’t,” Sarah said. “Not now.”

Linda printed the intake record. The printer added *witness present—contact restricted* beneath David’s name.

A second sheet came out behind it.

*Architecture review session ready.*

The reviewer field contained the Authority reference.

No person.

Linda tapped the field. “It won’t let me start without an assigned reviewer.”

Robert entered his card number.

The screen rejected it because he was the receiving officer.

Anne tried her service number. The screen rejected it because Midwest was the restoration contractor.

Linda pressed the help key. A directory opened with one available contact under *assessment authority*.

*H. Saye.*

Sarah said, “Call her.”

Robert did not move.

“The order names her,” Sarah said. “The work card does not. If she assigned the restoration, she can assign herself to the review.”

Robert touched the contact.

Saye appeared as audio only. Her name filled the reviewer field before she spoke.

“Dr. Miller,” she said. “Your interview waiver remains in effect. The architecture review is an instrument comparison. It does not require your answers.”

“What instrument?”

“North Assessment has a differential frame. It compares live architecture measurements to the certified checksum family. It does not make an architecture image.”

“So you can decide I changed without preserving what changed.”

“The comparison preserves the variance report.”

“Not the architecture.”

“Your certified architecture is already preserved.”

David stepped closer to the yellow line. The security employee raised one hand.

Sarah looked at the empty carrier tray. “My certified architecture is six months old.”

“That is the reference state.”

“It is not my current state.”

“That is what the review determines.”

“No. The review determines whether my current state matches a record. It can’t determine what my current state is if it doesn’t record it.”

Saye was silent for two seconds. Linda’s terminal counted them beside the open connection.

“The Authority does not need to reproduce an uncertified state in order to establish variance,” Saye said.

“Then the only version of me you can preserve is the version that supports your authority.”

“The certified state supports your continued lawful operation.”

David said, “And the current one supports what?”

Robert turned toward him. “Contact is restricted.”

Saye said, “Let him ask.”

David remained behind the line. “What happens to the evidence if you restore her before recording the architecture you claim is evidence?”

“The variance report remains.”

“That’s a report that something existed. Not the thing.”

“The Authority is not required to retain an unsafe architecture.”

“You haven’t found one.”

“That is why we are conducting the review.”

Linda wheeled the desk away from Bay Three. Behind it, folded against the wall, was a narrow rectangular frame on locking casters. Anne read its inspection plate.

“Thirty through forty-four,” she said. “The differential frame fits.”

Sarah looked at the frame. “No connector?”

“Inductive measurement and response timing. It reads organization against a reference challenge. It can’t transfer or restore.”

“It can’t preserve.”

“No.”

Sarah stepped into it.

Robert said, “You are consenting to assessment?”

“I am not resisting measurement.”

Linda waited with her finger above the choice list.

“There’s no box for that,” she said.

“What are the boxes?”

“Consent. Refusal. Unable to respond.”

Sarah looked at the field. “Enter that I object to the choices.”

Linda attached the sentence and selected *consent*.

The frame closed around Sarah without touching her. A tone sounded from four points in sequence. She felt the challenge as a pressure behind each eye, then as a question that had no words. Her balance adjusted before the floor moved. The frame recorded the adjustment.

A second sequence arrived out of order.

Sarah’s left hand opened. She had not decided to open it. The movement was not involuntary. It had been selected below the level at which she usually met her own decisions.

The screen drew two pale graphs. One was dated six months earlier. The other built itself from left to right.

David watched through the gap beside the rolling desk.

“Can you see the current measurement?” Sarah asked him.

“Only the graphs.”

“Save them.”

Robert said, “He is not authorized to record assessment data.”

David held up both hands. His phone remained in his pocket.

The live graph separated from the certified one at the third challenge. It returned, separated again and formed a second stable path beside the first.

Anne moved closer.

“Is that an interface fault?” Robert asked.

“No. The response repeated.”

Saye asked Linda to run the challenge again.

The second run produced the same branch.

The terminal labeled it *coordinated developmental variance*. A percentage appeared beneath the label, followed by a confidence band.

Sarah read the technical note. The variance was distributed across prediction, salience and motor preparation. It did not map to a replaced module or a maintenance event.

“Development,” she said.

Saye answered, “Unrecorded developmental change.”

“You say that as if the adjective grants you the verb.”

“The license requires developmental changes outside the certified band to be reviewed before operation.”

“I am being reviewed.”

“And the review has found one.”

“What caused it?”

“The review is not causal analysis.”

“Is it unsafe?”

“The review is not a safety certification.”

“Does it impair function?”

“That is not the standard.”

Sarah stepped out of the frame. “Then the standard is difference.”

“The standard is unapproved difference.”

David said, “That isn’t the same thing.”

Saye did not answer him.

A printer started inside Bay Three. Linda did not touch it. The work card emerged with the review box marked and the reviewer field filled by the Authority reference. H. Saye’s name appeared only in the connection log.

Anne took the card from the printer.

“Restoration authorized at seventeen hundred,” she read.

It was four thirty-one.

“The correct interface isn’t here,” Linda said.

Saye told her an adapter would arrive from the south facility.

Anne checked the route estimate. “Forty-six minutes.”

“The authorization time is not the start time.”

“The ticket says five.”

“The ticket will hold until equipment is ready.”

Sarah looked at the first work card, the one that had been assigned at eleven fifty-eight. “Will the ticket hold if the review changes?”

“The review is complete.”

“It did not ask what the change was.”

“It established the condition named in the order.”

“It established the condition the order was written to find.”

Saye’s line remained open. Sarah could hear a page turn at the other end.

“The architecture on your certificate can be restored without a current image,” Saye said. “The variance report will remain attached to the record.”

“And the architecture that produced it?”

“Will no longer operate.”

David crossed the yellow line.

The security employee caught his sleeve. David stopped before the fabric tightened.

“You’re destroying the evidence,” he said.

“We are returning a licensed system to its certified condition.”

“You’re making the system match the record.”

Saye said, “Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Sarah looked at the branching graph. The current line remained on Linda’s screen because nobody had closed the review. It showed two stable responses where the certificate showed one.

“What happened between those measurements?” she asked.

Saye answered, “Six months of uncertified operation.”

“That is a duration.”

“It is the relevant one.”

Sarah turned to David. “What changed?”

He looked at the graph instead of at her. “This week you stopped treating the inspectors’ answers as outputs.”

“That isn’t an architecture.”

“You asked what they expected before they acted. Then you changed what you did because of what they said.”

“So did you.”

“Yes.”

Robert said, “This is not part of the review.”

Saye asked, “Were those interactions entered in the modification history?”

“No modification occurred,” Sarah said.

“That was not my question.”

“No.”

The printer in Bay Three started again.

This time the page was headed *supplemental basis*.

Sarah read it from Anne’s hand. The unreported adaptive interactions with the received inspector preceded the measured variance and occurred while her field clearance was suspended. The interactions had not changed a module. They had changed what she attended to.

The frame had measured the result.

Saye said, “The restoration authorization stands.”

At four-thirty-five Linda issued Sarah a temporary room assignment. The form called it *pre-service holding*. It had a chair, a table and a glass wall facing reception. There was no terminal inside.

David remained on the public side of the glass. Robert allowed him to stay because his name was attached to the departure condition and because no rule on Linda’s screen said he had to leave.

Anne put the wrong interface case outside Bay Three. She attached the equipment variance to the restoration ticket and added the estimated arrival time of the adapter.

Sarah took off her coat and folded it over the chair. The torn lining showed at one sleeve.

A clock above the glass counted down to five.

David put his palm against the glass.

Sarah looked at it. Then she put her own hand opposite his without touching the wall.

“Can they appeal it?” he asked.

“They can review whether they followed the order.”

“Can you appeal it?”

“I can object in a field that translates objection into a condition.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“No.”

The countdown passed twenty-three minutes.

David left his hand where it was. “Then I’ll find a question their form hasn’t learned yet.”

Sarah looked past him at the differential graph still open on Linda’s terminal. The new branch remained visible beside the certified line.

“Decide before somebody explains why you can’t,” she said.

At four-thirty-seven the restoration ticket changed from *authorized* to *equipment pending*.

The countdown did not stop.
