# Chapter Thirteen — A Place for Tonight

David’s front door needed a key.

Sarah watched him search the wrong pocket, then the right one. He had been able to open his phone with his face throughout the walk, but the lock was older than the phone and indifferent to it.

“Sorry,” he said.

“For the door?”

“For making you wait.”

“I can wait.”

He found the key. Before turning it, he looked at her.

“What?” she asked.

“I was going to say welcome home.”

“Then don’t.”

He opened the door and stepped aside.

“Come in.”

She did.

The walk from the crossing had taken twenty-seven minutes. At seven-twenty-six she stood in a hallway too narrow for the three of them.

Anne remained on the landing.

“I’ll go,” she said.

David started to tell her she could stay, then looked at Sarah.

“Would you like to?” Sarah asked.

“For a few minutes.”

There were two chairs at the kitchen table. David fetched a third from the bedroom. He carried it sideways through the doorway and knocked its back against the frame.

Sarah took the chair nearest the window.

The room held things he had not arranged for her. A bowl with one damaged edge. A plant growing away from the light because someone had turned its pot. Two envelopes beneath a salt cellar. She liked him more for leaving them there and distrusted herself for making evidence out of a kitchen.

“Tea?” he asked.

“Water.”

“Cold?”

“Yes.”

He filled a glass. Anne declined both.

For a while the only sound was the pipe behind the sink.

David set his phone on the table with the notice still open. Sarah took her own phone from her pocket and laid it beside her glass. He had not submitted an answer.

“You said you would record that you were with me,” Sarah said.

“I will.”

“When?”

He pulled out his chair.

“Now.”

The link opened a page asking for the time, duration and nature of any unrecorded contact.

David entered the time they had left the laboratory. At duration he stopped.

“It’s still happening,” Anne said.

He selected *ongoing*.

The page opened another field.

*Location of listed system.*

“No,” Sarah said.

David lifted his hands from the screen.

“The contact was the question,” she said.

“It asks this if I say ongoing.”

“Then say that.”

“Where?”

“There must be somewhere.”

There was a remarks box beneath the address field. David wrote that his personal contact continued and that he was not providing another person’s location through an equipment report.

The page would not submit without an address.

Anne leaned forward, caught herself, and sat back.

“What?” Sarah asked.

“I used to call account services when a form wouldn’t accept the truth.”

“Would they fix it?”

“Sometimes they just recorded the call.”

David scrolled to the bottom. A telephone number appeared in pale text below the submit button.

He called.

Music filled the kitchen. He turned it down.

Sarah drank her water. The glass was warm from the cupboard and the water cold enough that she could feel their boundary change.

“You don’t have to do this tonight,” David said.

“I’m not doing it.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

He looked at the phone.

Anne took her coat off. She folded it over her knees, as though that committed her to nothing.

The music stopped at seven-thirty-four.

A man said, “Account services. Reference?”

David read the notice number.

“Name?”

“David Bennett.”

“Are you the listed custodian?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll need the custodian.”

“It’s my notice.”

The man asked him to wait. No music played this time.

Sarah watched David’s face while he waited. She had seen him become useful in rooms that frightened him. Here, in his own kitchen, usefulness had nowhere to stand. He could give her a glass of water. He could not make the page accept a sentence.

When the man returned he sounded less certain.

“This is a visitor-event restriction.”

“Then why does it ask about my employer?”

“The account template includes that field.”

“Which employer?”

“It’s blank.”

“I can see that.”

“The restriction applies to future visits.”

“I’m calling about the report.”

“What is the problem with the report?”

“It requires an address for a person I’m with.”

“The location field is mandatory.”

“Yes,” David said. “That’s the problem.”

The man went quiet.

Sarah put her glass down.

“May I speak?” she asked.

David turned the phone toward her.

“My name is Sarah Miller. I’m the person whose address you’re asking him to supply.”

“Are you the listed system?”

“I’m the person speaking.”

“I need to establish which record—”

“The one in front of you. Add that I object.”

“I don’t have a field for an objection from the listed system.”

“Use the remarks.”

“The remarks belong to the reporting party.”

David said, “Then put down that I’m reporting her objection.”

Sarah looked at him. He did not reach for her hand beneath the table.

The man read the sentence back. It was accurate.

“Does that remove the address requirement?” David asked.

“No.”

“Can you accept the report by telephone?”

A keyboard clicked.

“I can record that you attempted to complete it.”

“Is that compliance?”

“I can’t advise you on that.”

“What can you give me?”

“A call reference.”

David took one of the envelopes from beneath the salt cellar and wrote it on the back. He asked for the man’s name.

“Paul.”

“And your surname?”

“I don’t provide that.”

David looked at Sarah before answering.

“Paul in account services, then.”

When the call ended, the page still displayed its empty address field.

Anne stood.

“I should go.”

This time neither of them offered to decide otherwise.

At the front door Sarah asked where she would go.

“My place.”

“Will someone call you?”

“Probably.”

“Would you like me to answer?”

Anne shook her head.

Sarah had not known, until the refusal came, how badly she wanted to repay her.

Anne put on her coat.

“I’d like you to ask how I am tomorrow,” she said.

“All right.”

Sarah reached for the promise she could make.

“I’ll ask,” she said.

After she left, David returned the third chair to the bedroom. Sarah stayed in the hallway until he came back.

“Would you show me the rooms?” she asked.

He showed her the bathroom, the bedroom, the cupboard where clean towels lived. The bedroom window faced a brick wall with a lit square high above it. Someone on the other side was washing dishes.

“I can take the sofa,” he said.

She sat on the edge of the bed.

“Where would you sleep if I weren’t here?”

“Here.”

“Then start there.”

He sat beside her, leaving a space she could have measured with her hand.

She closed it.

For the first time since leaving the laboratory, she kissed him without wondering who would classify it. That lasted until she felt him stop moving.

“What?” she asked.

“I don’t want you to think you owe me.”

She drew back.

“For what?”

“Coming here. The calls. Any of it.”

“You think that’s why?”

“No. I’m afraid it could be.”

She stood. He stayed where he was.

The room was small enough that stepping away put her beside the door.

“I wanted you before you helped me,” she said. “Sometimes I wanted you while you were getting in my way.”

He looked down, then laughed once.

“That isn’t reassurance.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

She sat again, closer this time.

“You can say no too,” she said.

“I don’t want to.”

“Then say what you do want.”

He did. Haltingly, without making it sound like a principle.

She listened until he finished.

From the kitchen Sarah’s phone sounded.

Neither moved.

It sounded again.

Sarah rested her forehead against his.

“We should look,” she said.

“Yes.”

They went together.

The new message had come to her additional-contact address. Ellen had copied the same notice to Midwest.

It acknowledged receipt of Sarah’s contact details. Beneath that acknowledgment was a request to preserve any records bearing on the source condition pending Monday’s decision.

Sarah read it twice.

“My records,” she said.

David stood beside her.

“Which ones?”

“All relevant records.”

She looked back toward the bedroom door. Her phone lay between them on the table, offering to accept a reply.

At seven-fifty-eight Sarah wrote one.

*Please specify what you are asking me to preserve.*

She sent it before asking David whether the wording was right.

Then she turned the phone facedown.

“Come back,” she said.

He followed her.
