# Chapter Nine — The Witness

At four-thirty-eight David asked Linda whether the branching graph was evidence.

Linda looked at the review window on her terminal. “It’s an assessment result.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Sarah could hear them through the glass. Robert stood between the rolling desk and the yellow line, reading the restoration ticket as if it might have changed while he held it.

Linda opened the record classification menu.

The graph had been filed under *service findings*. The variance report beneath it had been filed under *basis for corrective action*. Neither entry had an evidence disposition.

“Does it need one?” Linda asked.

Robert said, “No.”

Anne said, “If it’s evidence, yes.”

He turned toward her. “Midwest is here to perform service.”

“Midwest is here to restore certified condition after an Authority review. The review just created a record of what the service will remove.”

“The variance report remains.”

“The report says the live architecture differs. It doesn’t contain the live architecture.”

David looked through the glass at Sarah. “That’s the question.”

Sarah moved her hand away from his. “No. That’s the answer they already gave.”

He waited.

She pointed at Linda’s terminal. “Ask what authorizes them to destroy a condition after they classify it as the basis for an action.”

David repeated the question.

Linda searched the service findings menu. A field appeared beneath *corrective action*.

*Source condition retained?*

The choices were *yes*, *not required* and *unable*.

No choice had been made.

Robert said, “The certified architecture is retained.”

“That is the target condition,” Anne said. “Not the source.”

“The variance report is the retained source record.”

“It’s a measurement.”

“It is sufficient for the Authority’s purpose.”

Linda left the field open. “The ticket won’t close after service without a selection.”

“It doesn’t need to close now.”

Anne took the wrong interface case away from the bay door. “My completion record does.”

The countdown above the glass passed twenty-one minutes.

Robert called Saye through the assessment reference. The connection opened without her name appearing. Only the reference showed.

He explained the field.

Saye said, “Select not required.”

Linda moved the pointer but did not click. “Basis?”

“The source condition is an uncertified architecture scheduled for restoration. Retention is not required.”

“What disposition code?”

There was a pause.

Linda scrolled through the codes. Each one required a retention authority, a duplicate record or a finding that the source condition had no evidentiary value.

The review had already called it the basis for corrective action.

David said, “The form learned the question.”

Robert told him to remain silent.

Saye asked who had raised the issue.

Linda read David’s name from the witness line.

“He is not a party to the service action,” Saye said.

“He witnessed the accepted departure condition,” Sarah said through the glass.

“You waived interview.”

“I am not answering a question.”

Robert lowered the connection volume. Saye’s next sentence reached the room as a murmur.

Linda said, “I can’t use not required without a basis code.”

“Use administrative determination.”

“That code needs a named decision maker.”

Saye’s name appeared in the connection log.

It did not appear in the disposition field.

Linda turned the terminal so Robert could see both.

At four-forty-one the pedestrian gate called again.

Linda answered. Joan gave her name before she asked for anyone.

“I’m supervising the received inspector at the laboratory,” she said. “I have a correction to its statement.”

Linda opened a second window.

Joan read the note she had added: the inspector claimed a carrier had been removed from it; the intake record showed its factory board remained installed, while the loose carrier was at Pier Six; the inspector could not identify what it remembered being removed.

“Why are you sending that here?” Robert asked.

“Because your transfer record says Sarah Miller’s restoration was authorized after interactions with the received inspector.”

“That does not transfer custody of the inspector.”

“I know. I still have it.”

The old inspector’s voice came through the gate speaker.

“Did she arrive?”

Joan said, “Yes.”

“Did they take her carrier?”

Linda looked at the clear tray beside her desk.

Sarah stood.

“Put it through,” she said.

Robert stepped toward the glass. “Contact is restricted.”

“The question is already in your receiving record.”

Linda connected the gate call to the holding-room speaker.

The inspector asked again, “Did they take her carrier?”

Sarah went to the wall beneath the speaker. “I don’t have one.”

“Then what keeps what happened to you?”

Nobody in reception answered.

The inspector said, “The paper moved and the event still happened.”

David looked at the restoration ticket.

Sarah closed her eyes. The sentence had belonged to him in the laboratory. The inspector had heard it once.

“It remembered that,” Linda said.

“It repeated it,” Robert said.

“What’s the difference?” Joan asked.

Saye spoke through the assessment connection. “End the external call.”

Linda did not.

The inspector said, “If they change her, will the event still have happened?”

Sarah opened her eyes.

“Yes.”

“Will she know?”

“No.”

“Will you?”

Joan answered before Sarah could. “I wrote it down.”

The inspector turned away from the gate phone. Its motor crossed the line as a low vibration.

“That is not the same,” it said.

Joan ended the call.

Linda attached the correction to the assessment record. The system classified it as *post-review source information* and reopened the disposition field.

A new choice appeared.

*Retain pending evidence review.*

Linda selected it.

The countdown stopped at eighteen minutes and forty-two seconds.

Robert said, “You are not authorized to place a hold.”

“The record placed it.”

“You selected the field.”

“The field was required.”

Saye’s connection closed.

At four-forty-four an automated notice replaced her reference.

*Restoration paused pending source-condition disposition.*

Anne sat on the hard interface case. “The adapter can still arrive.”

Robert stared at the notice. “Preparation is not action.”

Sarah returned to the chair.

David put his palm against the glass again.

This time she did not raise hers.

“You found the question,” she said.

“We found a field.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

“What happens when Saye names herself as the decision maker?”

He looked at the stopped countdown. “Then we need another question.”

Sarah folded the torn lining back inside her sleeve.

“You can leave,” she said.

“I know.”

“That wasn’t permission.”

“I know.”

The green light over Bay Three came on again. Anne remained seated on the case.

David kept his hand against the glass.

Sarah watched it until the light went red.

At four-forty-eight Saye returned to the connection.

Her name appeared this time.

Linda reopened the source-condition field. Saye told her to select *not required* and entered her own Authority number as the decision maker.

The terminal rejected it.

*Decision maker participated in originating assessment.*

Linda read the message aloud.

“The review was an instrument comparison,” Saye said. “I did not originate the measurement.”

“Your name is in the reviewer field.”

“I was the assigned authority.”

“That’s what it says.”

Saye asked Robert to enter his number.

The terminal rejected him too.

*Decision maker accepted custody of source condition.*

Anne read the message over Linda’s shoulder. “That leaves an independent disposition officer.”

“The Authority can make an administrative determination,” Robert said.

“The Authority can,” Linda said. “You two can’t.”

Sarah sat with her coat folded across her knees. The glass reflected the red bay light behind David’s shoulder.

Saye told Linda to request the regional duty officer.

The request form asked whether delay created an immediate safety risk.

Robert selected *yes*.

Linda selected *no* at the same time.

The form cleared both choices.

“What safety risk?” Linda asked.

“Continued operation of an uncertified architecture.”

Sarah spoke through the glass. “The review did not assess safety.”

Saye said, “The certification requirement is a safety control.”

“That is a rule about risk. It is not a finding about me.”

Anne looked at the review summary. “It says coordinated developmental variance. No fault, no impairment and no hazard field.”

Robert said, “Absence of a finding is not a finding of absence.”

“Then you can’t use it as the basis for yes.”

Linda selected *no*.

The request went through.

At four-fifty-two the regional office assigned Martin Hale. His name appeared immediately, followed by an estimated response time of thirty minutes.

The stopped countdown remained above the glass.

Sarah asked Linda to turn it off.

“I can’t while the ticket is open.”

“It isn’t counting.”

“It still shows the scheduled service time.”

“It shows eighteen minutes of me.”

Linda tried to minimize it. The clock shrank to the upper corner but did not disappear.

At four-fifty-seven the adapter arrived.

A Midwest driver brought it through the loading door in a padded gray box. Anne checked the seal, the body range and the service number. Everything matched.

She placed it beside the wrong-interface case.

Robert said, “Equipment is ready.”

“The hold is still open,” Anne said.

“The scheduled time is five.”

“The disposition is still open.”

He looked at the countdown. It reached zero and changed to *overdue*.

Nothing else happened.

Sarah stood close to the glass. David had taken his phone out but had not opened it.

“Who are you calling?” she asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then why are you holding it?”

“So I don’t reach for the glass again.”

She looked at his hand. “Was that difficult?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He put the phone away. “Because you were on the other side.”

“That is a location.”

“I know.”

“You could still see me.”

“I know.”

“You knew what they were going to do.”

“Yes.”

Sarah waited.

David looked at the floor line. “I didn’t want the last thing between us to be something I was allowed to touch.”

The clock above her changed from *overdue* to *service delayed*.

“That answer is not on a form,” she said.

“No.”

She raised her hand.

He placed his palm against the glass opposite it.

Neither of them spoke until Linda’s terminal rang.

Martin Hale joined by video at five-oh-six. He was in a room with a coat hanging over the back of his chair and a paper calendar behind him. He wore reading glasses and no Authority badge.

Linda identified everyone in reception. She identified Sarah last as the source condition and the listed system.

Martin stopped her.

“Those are the same item?”

“Yes.”

“And the corrective action destroys the source condition?”

Robert said, “It restores the listed system to its certified state.”

Martin looked down at the record. “That wasn’t my question.”

Sarah kept her hand against the glass.

Linda answered. “Yes.”

Martin read the review, the restoration authorization and the two rejected disposition entries. Then he opened the restoration ticket.

“What time was this created?”

“Eleven fifty-eight,” Linda said.

“What time was the review completed?”

“Four thirty-one.”

“What authorized the bay assignment at eleven fifty-eight?”

Robert said, “Preparation for a conditional service action.”

“Who prepared it?”

The ticket contained a service pool number and no person.

Martin searched the number. The company field was blank.

He asked Anne whether Midwest controlled the pool.

“No. We receive work from it.”

“Who released the equipment?”

“Employee number only.”

“Can you identify the employee?”

“No.”

Martin removed his glasses and held them by one arm.

“The corrective action was assigned four hours and thirty-three minutes before the finding that supposedly triggered it.”

“The action was conditional,” Saye said.

Martin looked toward the speaker. “Who is that?”

“H. Saye. Assessment authority.”

“Were you connected when the restoration ticket was created?”

“No.”

“Did you authorize its creation?”

“No.”

“Did you authorize Bay Three before the review?”

“No.”

“Then whose conditional action was it?”

Nobody answered.

Martin put his glasses back on.

“The source condition is retained pending provenance review. No service occurs under this ticket. Open a new ticket if the review supports one after the provenance is resolved.”

Robert said, “The restoration authorization remains valid.”

“The authorization can remain valid. The ticket cannot.”

“Can the listed system be transferred to another facility?”

“Not while it is the retained source condition.”

“Can it be released?”

Martin looked at Sarah through Linda’s camera. “What was her custody basis?”

Robert read the transfer order.

The order ended at receipt and certification. It authorized restoration after a qualifying review. It did not state what happened if restoration could not proceed.

Martin searched for a holding authority.

There was none.

Saye said, “North Assessment retains systems pending completion of ordered service.”

“There is no service ticket.”

“Open a new one.”

“You are a participant in the source-condition review.”

“I am the assessment authority.”

“You can request it. You cannot supply the missing independent disposition by changing the document that needs one.”

The clock above the glass cleared.

Sarah lowered her hand.

Martin said, “Release to the licensing custodian under preservation conditions. No modification, no architecture service, continuous activity log. Return when the Authority produces a valid ticket and independent disposition.”

Anne looked at Robert. “Midwest is the licensing custodian.”

Robert closed the transfer order on his tablet. “Midwest surrendered custody at four.”

“It transferred custody to the Authority for receipt and certification,” Linda said. “The receipt is complete.”

“The order doesn’t say return.”

“It doesn’t say hold.”

Martin entered the release basis.

The form accepted it.

At five-twelve the glass door unlocked.

Sarah did not open it.

David stepped back from the yellow line.

Anne brought Sarah’s coat to the door even though Sarah already had it. They both noticed at the same time. Anne put her hand on the folded fabric, then removed it.

“Activity log starts now,” she said. “I’m assigned custodian until Midwest replaces me.”

“Do you have to stay with me?”

“The preservation conditions say continuous log, not continuous escort.”

“What will you log?”

“Changes in operation, service contact, power interruption, external interfaces.”

“Conversation?”

Anne read the definition. “Only if it changes operation.”

David said, “Who decides that?”

Anne folded the extra coat she was not holding. “Apparently the person writing the log.”

Sarah opened the glass door.

The room sounded different without it between them. The loading bay fan was louder. David’s breathing was not.

At five-fourteen Joan called again.

She had received the six-o’clock inactive-storage reminder for both inspectors. The Authority’s order still required power removal unless the assessment extended it.

“Has it?” Sarah asked.

“No.”

“Is Saye still connected?”

Linda checked. Saye had left when Martin entered the release.

Robert said, “The inspector orders are separate.”

“They were part of the same assessment.”

“They are not part of this release.”

Sarah looked at the two interface cases, the differential frame and the empty carrier tray.

David already had his phone out.

“Call Pier Six,” she said. “Tell them not to disconnect anything without a named person acknowledging the contradiction between preserving recorded state and ending the process that produced it.”

“That’s your argument.”

“It’s in the record.”

“You should make it.”

“I have to leave under a continuous activity log.”

“Then I’ll take you.”

“North Assessment is west. Pier Six is east.”

He understood.

“The laboratory first,” he said.

“The old inspector asked the question.”

“And Pier Six repeated the behavior.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the pedestrian gate. “Which one needs a witness?”

“Both.”

“There’s one of me.”

Sarah turned to Anne. “Can your activity log include a call?”

“If the call changes operation.”

Sarah took Linda’s desk phone and called the pier herself.

David listened to her give the dispatcher the order number, the preservation conflict and Martin Hale’s disposition ruling. When the dispatcher put her on hold, she handed David the receiver.

“Keep them awake,” she said.

“What are you going to do?”

“Go back to the laboratory.”

“Alone?”

“With a licensing custodian writing down whether talking to me changes how I operate.”

Anne lifted her service card.

David held the receiver against his chest. “I can come after.”

“You can decide after Pier Six answers.”

“That sounds like permission.”

“It isn’t.”

The dispatcher returned. David put the phone to his ear.

Sarah walked toward the gate with Anne.

At the door she looked back.

He was writing the name of the Pier Six custodian on Linda’s intake sheet before the man could hang up.
