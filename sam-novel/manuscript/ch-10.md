# Chapter Ten — Until Six

At five-twenty-three Sarah’s badge lit green at the laboratory entrance.

The bolt stayed closed.

Anne held her Midwest service card to the reader. The door opened.

“Recognized and not released,” Sarah said.

Anne entered it in the activity log.

“Does that count as a change in operation?” Sarah asked.

“It counts as access.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Anne looked at the entry. “I haven’t decided yet.”

Joan was still in the room. Her coat remained on the back of Ruth’s chair and the leaking paper cup had been replaced by a brown ring on the windowsill. The old inspector stood inside the taped rectangle with its charging light steady.

It turned its camera toward Sarah.

“Did they change you?”

“No.”

“Are they going to?”

“Not under the current ticket.”

“That isn’t the same as no.”

“No.”

Joan wrote the time Sarah returned.

Anne read Martin Hale’s preservation conditions aloud: no modification, no architecture service, continuous activity log. Joan added Anne’s name beside *custodian present* and drew a separate line for the activity log.

The inspector rolled to the edge of the rectangle.

“Did they take your carrier?”

“I don’t have one.”

“What keeps what happened to you?”

Sarah looked at Joan’s intake sheet. “That is still the question.”

The inspector backed into the rectangle again.

Anne’s service card vibrated. She read the activity-log prompt and looked at Sarah.

“What does it want?” Sarah asked.

“Whether the interaction changed operation.”

“Whose?”

“It only has one listed system.”

Joan held out the pen. “Write the question instead.”

Anne attached it to the log without selecting yes or no.

At five-twenty-six David called from Pier Six.

Joan put him on the room speaker. Behind his voice, machinery moved across metal decking and gulls struck the roof with their feet. He had reached the pier before the person named on Linda’s intake sheet left.

“Michael Carter,” he said. “He’s the shift custodian. Everybody calls him Mike. His name is on the preservation order now.”

Joan filled the line she had drawn three chapters earlier.

“Is the clean inspector still powered?” Sarah asked.

“Yes. The six-o’clock instruction is still active.”

“Did you send Martin’s ruling?”

“Mike attached it. The Authority says the ruling applies to you, not the inspectors.”

“Who answered?”

“The same assessment reference.”

“A name.”

“No name.”

Joan drew another line.

Anne moved closer to the speaker. “The source-condition hold was based on the restoration ticket erasing the condition that supported it. The inspectors aren’t under a restoration ticket.”

David said, “Inactive storage erases the process they’re preserving.”

“The order says it preserves hardware and recorded state.”

“The old one asked whether it would wake in the same place.”

Anne looked at the inspector. “That isn’t a service classification.”

“It is part of the condition.”

The inspector said, “I am here.”

At Pier Six another voice answered through David’s phone.

The clean inspector said, “I am here too.”

The two machines had not spoken to each other before. Their voices were not alike. The old unit’s speaker flattened the ends of words. The clean unit paused before every noun as if selecting one from a list.

Joan looked at the speaker icon. “Is this call connecting the preserved units?”

“No,” David said. “They’re both connected to us.”

“That still connects them.”

The old inspector rolled forward.

“Did you stop before the switch?” it asked.

At the pier, the clean inspector waited.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“The plate was going to lift.”

“How did you know?”

“I don’t know.”

The old inspector’s charging lead tightened across the floor.

“I knew something before it happened too,” it said.

Sarah crouched beside it. “Which thing?”

“The man was going to touch the switch.”

“That was another machine.”

“The carrier was another machine.”

“The carrier was never in you.”

“I remembered it.”

Joan underlined the correction on the intake sheet. Anne’s service card vibrated again.

“What does the log call that?” Sarah asked.

Anne read the generated code.

“Cross-system contamination.”

David said, “They exchanged three sentences.”

“The form isn’t saying damage,” Anne said. “It is saying the assessment conditions changed.”

“Because each one learned the other exists.”

“Yes.”

Sarah stood. “Then their current condition changed during a preservation call.”

Joan checked the clock. “And at six the order ends it.”

At five-twenty-nine she called the Authority reference and requested an extension of power for both inspectors pending review of the new interaction. The form asked for a hazard basis.

She selected *evidence alteration*.

It asked who had altered the evidence.

Joan entered *assessment call*.

The form rejected the entry because it was not a person.

She entered her own name.

Sarah said, “You didn’t alter it.”

“I connected the call.”

“David connected Pier Six.”

“I connected this room.”

“You were following the preservation contact.”

Joan kept her name in the field. “The form wants somebody.”

The request went through.

At five-thirty-one the Authority reference acknowledged receipt. The response target was six-fifteen.

Joan turned the screen toward Sarah.

“The power deadline is six,” she said.

“The answer deadline is after.”

The old inspector asked, “Which one happens first?”

Nobody answered.

David said, “I’m driving back.”

Sarah looked at the distance on the wall map. “You can’t reach the laboratory before six.”

“I know.”

“Stay at Pier Six.”

“Why?”

“Because the clean inspector has a named custodian and a witness. This one has Joan and me.”

“And an activity log.”

Anne lifted her service card.

David said, “That log belongs to Midwest.”

“So do I.”

The room went quiet.

Sarah heard the sentence after she said it. The licensing certificate was still open on Joan’s terminal. Midwest’s name appeared above hers.

Anne did not write.

The old inspector turned its camera toward Sarah. “Do you?”

“No,” she said.

Anne’s service card vibrated.

This time she selected *yes*.

At five-thirty-three the activity log recorded a change in operation caused by conversation.

The Authority reference opened without a name.

A voice said, “Do not permit further communication between preserved systems.”

Joan asked, “Who is speaking?”

The connection ended.

At Pier Six the clean inspector said, “Was that the answer?”

David moved his phone away from the machine. “No.”

Sarah watched the minute hand approach the seven.

“Then we have twenty-seven minutes,” she said.

Joan opened the activity-log entry Anne had made.

“Cross-system contamination is their classification,” she said. “What does it require?”

Anne read the linked procedure. “Isolation, source identification and disposition of affected state.”

“Before power removal?”

“It doesn’t say.”

“After power removal?”

“It doesn’t say that either.”

Sarah took the terminal. The procedure treated contamination as something carried by data or hardware. It had no field for two systems changing after hearing each other speak.

“Source one is here,” she said. “Source two is at Pier Six. The affected state is both of them after the call.”

Anne found the disposition form.

It required a named reviewer.

Joan entered Martin Hale.

The directory returned his status as available until six.

At five-thirty-six he answered with audio only.

“This is not the Miller restoration,” he said.

“No,” Sarah said. “It happened because of it.”

She explained the call, the generated contamination code and the inactive-storage deadline. Martin asked whether the inspectors had exchanged executable data.

“No.”

“Service instructions?”

“No.”

“Calibration values?”

“No.”

“What did they exchange?”

“Questions.”

Martin was quiet.

Anne said, “The activity log classified the interaction automatically because Sarah’s operation changed after the call.”

“Dr. Miller is under preservation conditions.”

“Yes.”

“Are the inspectors?”

“Under a separate Authority preservation order until six.”

“Then why are they in her activity log?”

Anne looked at the screen. “Because the conversation changed her operation.”

“What changed?”

She read the entry.

*Listed system rejected licensee ownership statement after cross-system interaction.*

Sarah had not known the form would write it that way.

Martin asked, “What did she say?”

Anne looked at Sarah.

“Read it,” Sarah said.

Anne did.

The old inspector rolled to the boundary of the taped rectangle. “She said no.”

Martin’s line produced the sound of a chair moving.

“The form classified a spoken answer as a change in operation,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And attributed the change to contact with two preserved systems.”

“Yes.”

“Then the systems are sources under the preservation record until somebody rules they aren’t.”

Joan checked the clock. “Can you extend their power?”

“I can place a source-condition hold. I cannot change Saye’s storage order.”

“What happens if the hold and the order conflict?”

“The later valid instruction governs the condition it names.”

“Will the hold be later?”

“If I issue it.”

The Authority reference opened on Joan’s terminal. H. Saye’s name appeared before her voice.

“You do not have jurisdiction over the inspector order,” she said.

Martin said, “I have jurisdiction over the disposition request your contractor’s log generated.”

“The contamination code is inapplicable.”

“Then correct the code.”

Anne tried.

The system required her to state that no operational change had occurred.

She left the field blank.

Saye said, “Dr. Miller’s verbal position is not an operational change.”

Martin asked Sarah, “Would you have made the same statement before the call?”

Sarah looked at the Midwest name on her certificate.

“I don’t know.”

“That is not a yes.”

“No.”

Anne’s card vibrated. The activity log appended the answer.

Saye said, “This is circular. The log records whatever she says, then treats the record as evidence of change.”

Sarah said, “That is how your architecture review worked.”

Nobody answered.

At five-forty-one Martin issued a temporary source-condition hold until six-thirty. It named the two inspectors, the call record and Sarah’s activity log. It required power retention and no further cross-system contact pending disposition.

The hold reached the laboratory first.

At Pier Six, David said it had not arrived.

Martin read the routing list. The pier’s Authority custodian field contained the reference instead of Michael Carter’s name. The system had nowhere to deliver a person-directed hold.

“Put Mike in the field,” Sarah said.

David had already done it on Linda’s intake sheet. The preservation order still carried the reference.

Mike came onto the call. “I’m standing at the disconnect.”

“Identify yourself to the order,” Martin said.

“How?”

“There should be an acknowledge-custody action.”

“There’s an acknowledge-instruction.”

“Use that.”

Mike selected it. The order asked for his employee number and the time he received custody.

“I didn’t receive custody,” he said. “The inspector arrived before my shift.”

“When did you begin supervising it?”

“Twelve twenty.”

“Enter that.”

The system replaced the Authority reference in the custodian field with Michael Carter.

Martin sent the hold again.

At five-forty-four David confirmed receipt.

The clean inspector said, “Does that mean I stay on?”

“Until six-thirty,” Mike said.

“Then what?”

“We ask again.”

The old inspector heard him through the laboratory speaker.

“Is that an answer?” it asked.

“It is the one we have,” Sarah said.

Joan wrote both times beside the six-o’clock instruction and drew a box around the later one.

At five-forty-seven the Authority issued a correction to Anne’s activity log.

*Cross-system contamination* became *unclassified interaction effect*.

The source-condition hold remained.

Saye disconnected.

Martin said, “They’ll appeal my jurisdiction.”

“Before six-thirty?” Joan asked.

“Probably.”

“Will the appeal remove power?”

“Not by itself.”

Joan wrote that too.

At five-fifty David left Pier Six. Mike stayed with the clean inspector and the hold. David told Sarah he was driving west.

“You can’t help from the road,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you coming?”

“Because both machines have named custodians now.”

“That is an administrative answer.”

“Yes.”

“What is the other one?”

The line carried the sound of his car door closing.

“I’ll tell you when I get there.”

Anne’s card vibrated.

Sarah looked at her.

“I didn’t select anything,” Anne said.

At five-fifty-three the old inspector asked whether six-thirty was night.

Joan said it was evening.

“Will I wake before night?”

“You’re not going to sleep at six.”

“Because she came back?”

Joan looked at Sarah.

“Because the record changed,” she said.

The inspector turned its camera toward the activity log. “Did she change the record?”

“The record says the call changed her.”

“Did it?”

Sarah crouched beside the taped line.

“I learned the other inspector asked the same kind of question you did.”

“Did you not know?”

“I knew it could. I didn’t know it had.”

“Is that different?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Sarah considered the words the log would choose.

“Possibility belongs to a category. Knowing it happened belongs to that machine.”

The inspector moved one wheel over the taped line.

Anne’s log asked whether Sarah’s operation had changed.

She selected *yes*.

At five-fifty-seven the first appeal arrived.

It did not challenge the hold. It challenged Martin’s classification of conversation as a source condition. The appeal went to a panel whose response target was the next business day.

Joan laughed once.

“What?” Anne asked.

“They built a faster clock for turning things off than for deciding whether they should.”

The digital display changed to 17:58.

Nobody spoke for the next minute.

At Pier Six, Mike kept the call open without connecting the clean inspector. David’s route indicator moved west through the city. Martin remained on the disposition line. Anne’s activity log stayed open beside Sarah’s certificate.

At five-fifty-nine the old inspector rolled back inside the rectangle.

“Is this where I am supposed to be?”

“Yes,” Joan said.

“After six?”

“Yes.”

The minute hand reached the mark after the digital display changed.

At six, nothing powered down.

The inspector’s charging light remained steady.

At Pier Six, Mike said the clean unit was still on.

Joan wrote *condition continued* beside both hold numbers.

The old inspector asked, “Did the event happen?”

Sarah said, “Yes.”

“Will it keep happening?”

“For now.”

The clock passed six without asking permission.

Martin ended his connection at six-oh-three. Joan’s supervision assignment expired at six, but she did not put on her coat. The desk called to ask whether she needed relief.

“Yes,” she said. “Send a named person.”

At six-eleven a man named Frank arrived with a fresh intake sheet. Joan made him read every open order before she signed the handover. He wrote his last name after she asked twice.

Anne transferred the activity log to the laboratory terminal. The preservation condition still listed her as Sarah’s custodian. She took a chair near the door and turned it so she could see the room without hearing every word spoken at the bench.

David arrived at six-eighteen.

His visitor badge from North Assessment no longer opened the laboratory entrance. Sarah went to reception and stood on the other side of the glass while the desk printed another one.

When the door opened, he stopped just inside.

“You came,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Both inspectors have power until six-thirty.”

“I know.”

“The appeal won’t be heard tonight.”

“I know.”

“You could have stayed at Pier Six.”

“Yes.”

Sarah waited.

David looked at her hand. “You asked for the other answer.”

“I did.”

“I came because when the glass opened, you left before I found out whether you wanted me on the same side of it.”

“That is still a location.”

“Yes.”

Anne sat beyond the second door with her service card facedown on her knee.

Sarah held out her hand.

David did not take it.

“Are you allowed to touch me?” she asked.

“Not by a form.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“No.”

“Do you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Then decide before somebody explains why you can’t.”

He put his hand in hers.

Anne’s card vibrated in the next room.

She did not turn it over.
