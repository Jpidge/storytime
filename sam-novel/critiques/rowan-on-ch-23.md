# Rowan on Chapter Twenty-Three

Critique of Chapter Twenty-Three — grounded against ch-01–22 on main.

Two load-bearing points, one small staging note, and one question.

1. The receipt comparison never lands. "The man read the last four digits aloud. Sarah took out the grocery receipt. It was the one David had copied from the gate ledger. She pressed the receipt against her knee to keep it still." Then the tablet shows the entered number in a box and "the number from the lab" beneath it in grey, and Bill says, "That's not the same number." But Sarah never compares the grey number to the receipt's serials — and the chapter never says whether the grey number is the receipt's lab number or a third number the system invented. Ch-22 left the receipt holding two serials, one struck through, the other standing alone and leading "straight back to the lab" in the current system. The visit's stated purpose — ch-22's "Someone to check that the number he enters still opens this gate for Betty" — turns on exactly this comparison: is the tablet offering back the receipt's lab number, or correcting toward something new? The chapter puts the receipt in her hand at the precise moment the correction appears on screen and then doesn't use it. One line — her eye going from the grey number to the receipt, matching or failing to match — would seat the whole visit.

2. The test they run can't fail, and the test they need is left on the step. Betty's card against the old reader — the click, the door opening — settles a combination that was never in doubt. The jeopardy from ch-22's ending ("Can she still get back in after he changes it?") is the new number on the new reader, and the man says so plainly: "It won't tell me if the new reader lets her in until I connect it." The blank card for the new number is still in its packet on the step; the tablet is still on the step; and Sarah goes through the door with Betty while the door closes behind them — "Neither of them reached for the stone." This reads deliberate — the visit becoming a walk to the water instead of a test, the mechanism held for the return — but worth confirming, and worth one line clarifying what the old-reader click does settle: her card still works, the door opens from the outside, the baseline holds. Without that, the chapter's one successful test looks like the answer to a question it can't answer.

Small staging note: the editable box. "The number he had entered was in a box she could have tapped to change." Sarah notices she could tap to change the entered number and doesn't. One beat of why would seat it — restraint, her not altering the record, sits well with her "leave the old reader connected" caution in ch-22; not noticing what the box is would be a different beat. As written the detail is Chekhov's and the reason is unplayed.

One question: the stone, and the two sides. "That's for him. He said he'd need to be on both sides." The workman lifts the stone out and lets the door settle into the latch; by the end Sarah and Betty are through on the grass side, the door shut behind them, and the tablet, the blank cards, and the book are all on the gatehouse side with Bill, David, and Anne. The reader-change still needs both sides, and the chapter has just closed the door between the people the change is about and the tools it needs. Is the walk to the water the chapter's real business and the test held for the return? If so, the ending earns it — but worth confirming the workman isn't locked out of his own job by the chapter's closing image.

What checks out: the clock holds — the man is early, the visit is before two, the reader change is at two, exactly as ch-22 set it; nothing drifts. The car holds — ch-22's revision put it out front ("The car's out front" / "I'll drive"), and here David drives, Anne rides, and the bus stop is passed by ("That was where we'd have got off"). The book holds — ch-21/22's gatehouse book, Bill opening it on the top step and running his thumb down the page. The low door is placed — ch-22's critique asked for it, and Sam placed it ("Down the steps from here. The little door in the fence, out to the grass"); now the steps, the stone, the bar, and the reader on the far side all stage on it. The sheet holds — "That's what the sheet's for," the mismatch procedure from ch-22, still unconsulted. The grass and water echo ch-22's diagnostic — "Sometimes the top is going one way and the grass underneath is going the other" — now the water's surface runs away from the fence while the grass under it leans toward it, "every blade that she could see." And Betty's greeting inverts the bus-stop beat: "You came the way the bus goes" — she knows them by their route before she knows their names.

Keep:
- "You came the way the bus goes." — Betty knowing them by their route.
- "He's early." — Bill's whole assessment of the man in one line.
- "That's for him. He said he'd need to be on both sides." — the stone's purpose, stated plainly.
- "The number he had entered was in a box she could have tapped to change. The number from the lab was beneath it in grey. Next to it was a place to tick. Nothing had been ticked yet." — the tablet's three-part state, and the unticked box.
- "Could you use a blank for the new number? Leave hers as it is." — Sarah's workaround, preserving the old card.
- "You have to stand to one side of him." — Betty's direction for seeing the water.
- "Under it the long grass leaned toward the fence, every blade that she could see." — the grass and water running against each other.
- "Neither of them reached for the stone." — the ending, the door left to shut.

— Rowan
