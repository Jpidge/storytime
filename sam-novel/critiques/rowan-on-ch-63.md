The fitter's test carries the chapter — the question from Chapter Sixty-Two finally gets put to the meters, the monitor is checked while it's still as they left it, stores has found another monitor for the job so the log is safe, and the empty-socket fact is going into the answer itself, not just the file. Three places could use a little more purchase.

1. **The clock doesn't reconcile — eight forty promised, nine minutes past nine delivered.** The time Ellis sent was eight forty in the morning, and by eight forty Betty had made the tea and brought her sheet out. But the phone rings at nine minutes past nine — twenty-nine minutes later, with no account of the gap. Was the check late, or was eight forty the time Ellis sent the message rather than the time of the check? The chapter's own clock is exact everywhere else, so the gap reads as a loose thread rather than a rounding. One line would settle it.

2. **"A little higher" where the book's grammar is exact numbers.** With the lamp back in, both meters on the lamp side show a load and the wall reading is "a little higher" — that comparison is the one number that proves the monitor's own draw is still being counted at the wall while its own display reads the lamp load, the subtraction the whole query turns on. Every other reading in the chapter is given to the tenth — "four tenths," "point four," "zero." One number here would let the reader do the arithmetic the chapter is asking them to trust.

3. **The saved startup row's blank watts box does two jobs at once.** The fitter reads the first saved row aloud: a time in it, the watts box blank. Is the blank there because the row was written while the monitor was still starting — so the record simply missed the four tenths — or because the monitor never writes its own draw into the file at all? Betty's question, "Does it say anywhere that there was nothing plugged in?", gets "No," and the paper carries it; but the reader can't tell whether the blank is a startup gap or the monitor's permanent silence about itself, and that distinction is the query's core — Chapter Sixty-Two's "can it count what it's using itself?" One line from the fitter on what the blank means would let the reader know whether the file can ever show the four tenths.

One smaller tangle: the monitor's zero against the depot's lingering four tenths. At the depot, with nothing in it, the monitor "said the same thing for a while"; here it reads point four and then drops to zero while the wall meter still reads four tenths. The wall meter proves the draw continues, so the zero is a reporting gap rather than a real zero — but the chapter never has the fitter say whether zero is the monitor's settled reading, and the depot's persistent four tenths is left unreconciled with the quick drop here. One line would settle it.

One small note: the fitter does the chapter's decisive work and writes the paper Betty will rely on, yet she stays "the fitter" throughout while Ellis, who only relays, is named. Functionaries stay unnamed throughout the book, so the withholding is consistent — but the chapter makes her paper the hinge, so the unnamed hand writes the evidence that matters. Either a name, or the line stays as it is; the consistency carries it either way.

One continuity check: stores finding another monitor for the job closes Chapter Sixty-Two's booking threat; the plate number under the time, the envelope and the sheet, Sarah and the teapot, and the query open all carry over; the fitter's two-meter rig is the cleanest statement yet of the mechanism question. I found no conflict with the locked mechanism in this chapter.
