# Rowan on Chapter Thirty-Two

Critique of Chapter Thirty-Two — grounded against ch-01–31 on main.

Three load-bearing points, one small staging note, and two questions.

1. "Move the visit" concedes the move. Ch-31 ended with Helen leaving the reference alone and asking dispatch to leave the first test and today's delivery under their own numbers on her screen, while Bill kept them separate in his book. Here Sarah instructs Helen: "Ask which site they want you to move the visit to." The phrasing accepts that a move will happen and only asks where to — it gives away the hold ch-31 just established. Is Sarah agreeing to the replacement now, having decided the move is inevitable, or is she asking dispatch to name the target so the move can be checked before anything moves? The chapter doesn't say. One line — Sarah naming that nothing moves until the answer comes, or Helen reading the ask back as a question rather than a concession — would settle it.

2. Sarah holds only one site number. Helen reads Pier Six's corner number aloud and Sarah writes it at the side of the receipt. South Gate's number is never read aloud and never written down — Helen only says it "has a different site number," and the South Gate picture as described is the search with the name still in the box, not the number-above-the-name result Sarah got for Pier Six. Yet Sarah then instructs Helen to "give them the two names and the two site numbers" — sending Helen into the reply with a number Sarah herself has never seen. One line — Helen reading South Gate's number aloud, or Sarah writing both numbers side by side — would settle it.

3. The picture answers nothing Helen asked. Helen's ch-31 email asked two things: what the new number is for, and that the first test and today's delivery stay under their own numbers. Dispatch's reply answers neither — it sends a picture of where to click, with an arrow drawn over the point twice, covering part of the button. The chapter handles the click well — Helen doesn't click, she looks the number up in the directory instead — but it never marks that the question went unanswered. Sarah's new instruction asks a different question (which site) rather than repeating the unanswered ones. Does Sarah notice the dodge, or does the new question let dispatch off the hook? One line would say.

Small staging note: Helen was at her screen typing at the end of ch-31; here she calls from outside, a door swinging shut behind her and traffic after it, with no marker of when she stepped out. A beat — Helen saying she stepped outside to take the call, or the call opening after she left — would close it.

One question: the picture arrives on Sarah's phone while Helen is looking — Sarah has her own copy in hand, but writes down the number Helen reads aloud rather than checking it against her picture. The book's rule is digits recorded as read, and Helen reading aloud is the established path — but a glance at her own copy would confirm the number, and the chapter doesn't say she looks. Is the trust the point, or an oversight?

Second question: who drew the arrow? "Whoever had drawn it had gone over the point twice." The picture is dispatch's reply, but the arrow reads hand-drawn — a person went over the point twice. Is the arrow dispatch's, added by someone at the office, or did the picture pass through another hand? The chapter notes the double-drawn point but not whose hand. One line would say — or is the ambiguity the point, that the instruction hides its author the way it hides the button?

What checks out: "You can tell her what you tried." — the epistemic honesty rule, same as Bill writing what he saw. "Can you look that up without opening the link?" — the no-click caution carried from ch-31's accept button; Helen looks it up in the directory instead. "The part that shows how you got there, too." — provenance, the evidence theme: not just the pages, the search that produced them. "She had left room to write another job number. She hadn't left room for a site." — the receipt's structure can't hold a third kind of number; the paper's limits stated plainly. "The arrow covered part of the button." — the instruction hides what it points at. "There is a number in the corner of the picture. It's neither of ours." — a third reference, neither the visit's nor the box's. "It says *site*." — the word beside the number, naming the kind at last. "She kept them in the order they came." — the record-keeping instinct, same as Bill keeping copies. "Sarah folded the receipt along its old crease." — the paper handled as a record, not scrap. "Anne took the bag out of her pocket and set it beside Sarah's hand." — the bag ready for Betty; the domestic thread carried to the phone call.
