# Rowan on Chapter Sixty-One

The call with the woman from visits carries the chapter — Mo’s message opened while Betty waits, the read-back ritual continuing into the new reference written slowly enough that the woman had to wait between pairs of digits, and the envelope accumulating its fourth annotation. Keep “the click at the end that sounded as if someone had hung up,” “the woman had to wait between pairs of digits,” the envelope beside the trackpad to make room for her elbow, and “Who opens it after you?” with its answer, “If it stays on this visit it’ll be there if someone looks up the collection.” Three places could use a little more purchase.

1. **“Both sheets” isn’t identified.** “Send both sheets. The one from here and the one they’ve just done.” The chapter has three candidate sheets in play: the sideways sheet in the envelope (the depot test), Mo’s bench sheet (the photograph just sent), and the sheet in the November attachment that carries Betty’s question. “The one from here” doesn’t map cleanly onto any of them — from Betty’s house? from the depot? — and “the one they’ve just done” could be Mo’s bench sheet or the depot’s test. The reader can’t tell which two sheets visits is being asked to send, or to whom “send them” is addressed. One phrase naming each sheet by what it is would settle it.

2. **The white box above the first zero.** Sarah puts the laptop where Betty can see it; “a white box sat above the first zero.” What is it — a tooltip, a selected cell, a pop-up over the file? And the first zero of what — the first row, the first reading? The detail asks the reader to decode something the chapter never decodes, and it’s never returned to. Either name what Betty is looking at or cut it; as it stands it reads like a clue with nothing behind it.

3. **“The first two numbers were still there.”** Betty writes the new reference under the depot date, then turns the envelope over to check that the first two numbers were still there. First two numbers of what — the reference she has just written, or the collection number on the reverse? And “still there” implies they might have gone — rubbed off, or on the side she can’t see while writing? The reader can’t tell which numbers, which side, or what threatens them. One line would anchor it.

One smaller tangle: the November attachment. The woman says “Your question’s in the attachment,” then in the next breath “I could see the sheet Betty meant” — is the sheet the attachment, or is the question in the attachment and the sheet something else? The chapter treats them as one thing in one line and two in the next. One clause saying what the attachment is would settle it.

One small loose end: Sarah’s finger-mark on the laptop — “Betty could see the mark her finger had left that morning.” The finger on a date in Chapter Sixty was on the sheet, not the laptop, so the mark’s origin is unclear; a word saying where it sits (screen, trackpad) would place it.

One continuity check: the iron comes back from Chapter Sixty — off the end of the table, onto the draining board, where the cord hung — and Mo’s message arrives exactly as promised, file and photograph together. The “both” from Chapter Sixty’s close lands here as the sheet and the log, which confirms that reading. The envelope now carries the collection number, the depot date, the visits reply address, and the new reference under the depot date — the ledger keeps growing in the same order. “Equipment collected” under outcome matches the collection. I found no conflict with the locked mechanism in this chapter.
