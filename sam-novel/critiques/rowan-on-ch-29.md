# Rowan on Chapter Twenty-Nine

Critique of Chapter Twenty-Nine — grounded against ch-01–28 on main.

Two load-bearing points, one small staging note, and two questions.

1. The sheet says Pier Six and the note says they got it wrong — which is the error? Bill reads the name off the top: "Pier Six. It’s on both pages." And Betty, looking at the same sheet, says, "It’s where he says they got it wrong" — the note, in Bill’s words, says the office got something wrong. The book has been tracking since ch-25 whether the record names South Gate or Pier Six, and ch-28’s revision established that the street address carries no gate name at all. So now the visit sheet asserts Pier Six while carrying Bill’s statement that something was got wrong, and the reader can’t tell whether Pier Six is the correction or the mistake — whether the office got the gate wrong by naming Pier Six, or got something else wrong and Pier Six is right. One line naming what was got wrong would settle which name the record should carry.

2. The two numbers are never compared. The man reads his number aloud; Sarah follows the first group on the receipt and loses him in the second; Bill asks him to start again, and the chapter ends. The reader can’t tell whether the first group matched — whether the two sheets agree on the job — or whether Sarah simply couldn’t keep up with the reading. In a book where Helen reads every number back and Bill writes every time down, a number read aloud that nobody confirms hasn’t landed. One line — Sarah saying the first group matched, or didn’t — would say what’s at stake when he starts again.

Small staging note: Sarah never answers "Who do I give it to if they send somebody else?" She tells Bill to keep the copy and print another, but the question — who the copy goes to if a different man comes — goes unanswered, and then a man does come to the gatehouse door. One line would close it, and it matters because the whole second half of the chapter is about who holds which sheet.

One question: the new card. The installer asks if they’ve got the new card, and Sarah says, "He took it with him." If he took it, why is he asking for it — is he asking whether they kept a copy, or has he forgotten he took it, or does he mean a different card from the one he took? One line would settle which card he means.

Second question: "Is that where you used to go?" Betty’s question lands as new information — the reader hasn’t been told Sarah used to go to Pier Six. If it’s a reveal, it works as one; if it’s meant as known, one line anchoring when she went would carry it.

What checks out: the times — Sarah asks Bill to put the time on the sheet, and when he asks which time she has him write *printed* beside the one he’s about to add — the same record-keeping ethic as ch-28’s "I’ll write down when I shut it," and it keeps the two times in the book and the two on the sheet distinct instead of adding a third ambiguous one. The receipt keeps accumulating — job number on the back (ch-25), request number underneath it (ch-28), and now Sarah draws a line below the job number for the man’s number, "without crossing anything out" — the same receipt, the same care. "Pier Six. It’s on both pages" — the name on top, on both pages, matching ch-28’s file with a test and a note in it; Bill reading the job number from the first page and then from the second is the same check. "It’s a pier. You can walk right to the end of it without going through a door." — Bill answering Betty’s "Is it a gate?" in the book’s own register, plain and final. The printer pulling the second page in at an angle and Bill catching it before it dropped behind the desk — the physical sheet, the same object Helen received in ch-28, now something that can be mishandled. Anne at the washing line, pegs back in the bag one at a time, and Sarah calling her in when a stranger is at the gatehouse door — the domestic thread holding while the office thread moves.

Keep:
- "It’s a pier. You can walk right to the end of it without going through a door."
- "I can see what I said."
- "Pier Six. It’s on both pages."
- "She drew a line below the job number so she could write the other one without crossing anything out."
- "He took it with him." / "I know. I’m telling him that."
- "The printer pulled the second page in at an angle."

— Rowan
