# Rowan on Chapter Twenty-Seven

Critique of Chapter Twenty-Seven — grounded against ch-01–26 on main.

Three load-bearing points, one small staging note, and one question.

1. The two names are unnamed. "Below it he wrote the two names and joined them with a line. At the end of the line he put a question mark." The definite article assumes the reader knows which two — but there are three live pairs: Pier Six and South Gate, the two names the installer added to the note in ch-25; South Gate, the name at the top of the page before it (ch-24), and the name Helen just found on the box; the box name and Bill's own name, the two parties to the visit-sheet request. Each pair makes the question mark mean something different: which gate is this, or is the contractor the same party as the book's name, or who ordered the job. The line-and-question-mark is the chapter's closing image of the record's uncertainty, and it needs its names. One line naming them fixes it — and decides what Bill is actually asking.

2. Sarah's pivot away from the pictures is unexplained. Bill asks whether the installer left her a copy of what he said. Sarah has two pictures: the one with the dash (Helen's screen, sent in ch-26) and the one of the tablet before he saved the note (ch-24). She opens her mouth to tell Bill she has pictures, then takes the receipt out instead. The pivot is right — neither picture records Bill's spoken words: the tablet photo shows the line before the note was typed in ch-25, the dash photo shows Helen's screen — but the chapter never says why she changes her mind, so the reader has to work it out. And her spoken answer slides to a different question: the job number, and the attachment Helen can see but can't identify. Bill has to ask again — "Can she see what I said?" — to get "Not yet." One line — Sarah realising the pictures show the screen and the tablet, not his words — carries the pivot, and keeps the ch-26 paperclip doubt in the foreground instead of letting the receipt change the subject.

3. The bus. Anne sat up to watch it; it slowed at the post and went on without opening its doors. But Anne is in the car, not at the stop — nobody is waiting at the post, so a bus not opening its doors is unremarkable. In ch-23 the bus missed Anne at the stop; in ch-26 she was looking across the road at it. Here the chapter gives the passing bus a full beat with no one marking it. Is Anne watching because she still expects it to stop for her, or is the point that the stop is effectively dead and the route just passes now? As written the beat reads as an echo of the missed stop without saying what it echoes. One line — Anne's reaction, or Sarah noticing her watching — would say which it is.

Small staging note: the photograph's time. Bill wrote two; the photograph showed three minutes past. The only photograph on Sarah's phone with a time in it is the tablet picture from ch-24 — the clock turned to 2:00 there, and the picture was taken after the swap test, so 2:03 is the photo's time and 2:00 is when the test started, and Bill's "Two was when he started" draws the distinction. But as written, "the photograph showed three minutes past" leaves the hour to be supplied from Bill's written two, and it can read at first as the photograph disagreeing with Bill's record — the same kind of record-disagreement the book has been tracking — rather than recording a second moment. Name the photograph, or give the hour: "three minutes past two" keeps the two times distinct from the start.

One question: Helen found a name on the box — "There's a name here. Let me check what it belongs to" — and reports back only that the box belongs to a contractor. The name itself never reaches the reader. Is it being held for the visit sheet, or should Sarah ask Helen to read it now, the way she reads every other number back? In a book where Helen reads the job number back in groups of three, a name arriving without being read stands out.

What checks out: the key — David drew it out of the ignition in ch-26 and held it in his palm; ch-27 opens with him putting it back, and Sarah's hand on his sleeve stops him turning it. The book — the page Anne held flat in ch-25, the request about tomorrow Bill wrote at the top of the next page (Betty's half past two), the old number under Bill's hand against the wind, the name at the top of the page before it read back by Helen. Bill's words — "green light... it didn't lift the latch" — his exact words from ch-25, typed into the note, now written back into the book in his own hand. The return — Bill said he'd ask them to call first (ch-26); "after two tomorrow" sits alongside Betty's half past two without straining it. Helen's thread — she reads the name and the job number back before Bill takes his hand off the book. And the attachment doubt continues honestly: "Not yet."

Keep:
- "I should have written it here." — Bill on the book, the record-keeping ethic in one line.
- "Two was when he started." — the start time kept distinct from the photograph's time.
- "Ask if they can send us what he wrote." — Bill's ask, plain.
- "He waited until she had finished before he took his hand off the book." — the hand staying put while Helen reads back.
- "Can she see what I said?" / "Not yet." — the paperclip doubt, still open.
- "Below it he wrote the two names and joined them with a line. At the end of the line he put a question mark." — the image; it only needs its names.
- "Put my number down," he said. "If they call after two tomorrow I'll be in." — Bill's availability, plain.

— Rowan
